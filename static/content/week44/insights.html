First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week we met Jeremiah as a young man who said, "I cannot speak: for I am a child" (Jeremiah 1:6), and we watched him warn a city that would not listen. This week the warnings come true. Babylon's army surrounds Jerusalem, the prophet is thrown into prison and then into a pit, and the city falls. Lehi's family had left about a decade before.

And yet the week opens with some of the most hopeful chapters in the Old Testament. Before the story reaches the ruin, the Lord speaks of homecoming, a new covenant, and a Branch from David's line. After the ruin comes Lamentations, five poems of grief, with one of scripture's best-loved lines of hope set in the middle of them.

The reading skips around (Jeremiah 31–33, then 36–39, then two chapters of Lamentations), and Jeremiah's own chapters are not in time order. The Overview walks every chapter in between, and Historical & Cultural Context, section 1, lays the events out in the order they happened.

Comfort That Has a Name ▶︎

The first poem of Lamentations pictures Jerusalem as a widow weeping alone, and it keeps coming back to one line: there is no one to comfort her. If you have ever sat with a grief that no one else seemed to see, you know that line from the inside.

Here is what makes it more than sad. The Hebrew word for comfort in that refrain, nāḥam (נָחַם), is the word Ephraim uses for repented in Jeremiah 31:19, "after that I was turned, I repented." (Ephraim, the leading tribe of the old northern kingdom, stands there for all the northern tribes Assyria had carried away, pictured as the Lord's son.) One word holds both: to be comforted, and to repent. And when the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament rendered Lamentations, it used a word from the same family as Comforter in John's Gospel, where the Savior promises the Holy Ghost: "I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter" (John 14:16). Word Studies, study 1, shows the evidence.

For Latter-day Saints, the comfort Lamentations could not find has a name. The Comforter does not wait for us to stop grieving before He comes, and He does not ask us to pretend we are not hurting. But comfort and turning are close neighbors. Sometimes the comfort begins in the moment we say to the Lord what Ephraim said: "turn thou me, and I shall be turned."

Mercies You Can Count ▶︎

The poet of Lamentations 3 could not see the restoration Jeremiah had promised. The temple was gone, the city was empty, and he describes himself as walled in, his prayers shut out. What he could see was smaller: "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed" (Lamentations 3:22). He was still alive. His people were still there. And he decided that counted.

The word he uses is plural, mercies; Word Studies, study 2, shows what that plural adds. And they are "new every morning" (Lamentations 3:23). An ancient Jewish teacher, Rabbi Alexandri, read that line as a small daily promise of the resurrection: each morning we wake, and each waking tells us God can be trusted to raise us at the last (Jewish Perspective, section 4). Latter-day Saints know where that promise was kept: at a tomb, "very early in the morning… at the rising of the sun" (Mark 16:2).

Try this tomorrow. Before you reach for your phone, name three mercies from the last day. Small ones count. The poet started with we are not consumed.

The Servant Who Trusted ▶︎

Two men faced the same crisis in Jerusalem's last days: the king, and a servant in his house. The king was Zedekiah, who believed the prophet enough to ask for his counsel and was too afraid to take it. The servant was Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian in the palace, whose name means "servant of the king."

Ebed-melech had no power to save the city. What he could do was walk to the city gate, say out loud that what had been done to Jeremiah was wrong, and then, before he pulled the prophet out of the mud, go find some soft old rags so the ropes would not cut. Key Passages, passage 6, tells the story in full.

The promise the Lord sent him is worth reading slowly: "thy life shall be for a prey unto thee: because thou hast put thy trust in me" (Jeremiah 39:18). His life would be spared, carried out of the fallen city like a soldier's plunder. The Lord had offered the king life as well, in different words (Jeremiah 38:17), and he had refused it.

Most of us will never be kings. We are servants in someone else's house, with no power over how the big story ends. Ebed-melech is evidence that this does not matter to the Lord. Courage in one hard conversation, kindness in one small detail, and trust in Him: that was enough for a promise with his name on it.

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

Before the siege and the pit, before the fire and the ruins, the Lord said this to His people:

"Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee." — Jeremiah 31:3

Lovingkindness there is ḥeseḏ (חֶסֶד), the same Hebrew word that becomes "mercies" in Lamentations 3:22, after the fall (Word Studies, study 2). What He promised before the worst happened, His people found still true on the other side of it.

Whatever this week holds for you, that is the order of things: the love comes first, and it is still there in the morning.
