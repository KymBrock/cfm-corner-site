First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week's prophets spoke mostly to the Northern Kingdom of Israel and to its neighbors. This week the scene moves south, to Judah. Read in order, the four books follow one empire falling and the next rising. Micah promises a ruler who "shall be the peace, when the Assyrian shall come into our land" (Micah 5:5). Nahum announces the fall of Assyria's capital, Nineveh. Habakkuk hears that the Lord will "raise up the Chaldeans" (Habakkuk 1:6), the Babylonians. And Zephaniah calls Judah to seek the Lord before its own "day of the LORD's anger" (Zephaniah 2:3). CFM Corner's field guides Assyria: The Rod of the North and Babylon: The Empire and the Exile tell the story of both empires.

The Overview places each prophet in his historical setting and walks through all four books in order, and Historical & Cultural Context fills in the empires and the dates. This page steps back to look at what the four books say together.

Four Prophets, One Question ▶︎

Micah's name, a short form of Micaiah, asks a question: "Who is like the LORD?" His book's closing praise asks it aloud, "Who is a God like unto thee…?" (Micah 7:18; Key Passages, passage 3). Read the four books side by side and each one gives its own answer.

  • Micah: no one else pardons like Him. "…he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy" (7:18).
  • Nahum: no one else is both so powerful and so safe to run to. "The LORD is good, a strong hold in the day of trouble" (1:7).
  • Habakkuk: no one else stays when everything else fails. "The LORD God is my strength" (3:19).
  • Zephaniah: no one else rejoices over His people like this. "…he will joy over thee with singing" (3:17).

Four books often read as books of doom each describe the Lord in words of mercy, strength and joy. If you have ever wondered what the Lord is really like, these four books answer in their own words.

Three Weeks at Sinai ▶︎

For three weeks now, the prophets have kept returning to one moment: the Lord passing before Moses on Sinai and describing Himself, "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6).

Joel quoted those words to call Judah home (Joel 2:13). Jonah quoted them to explain, angrily, why he had run from his call to Nineveh (Jonah 4:2). This week Micah closes his book with an echo of them, and Nahum opens his with the half Joel and Jonah left out: that the Lord, for all His patience, "will not at all acquit" the guilty (Nahum 1:3; Key Passages, passages 3 and 4, show the details).

Different prophets, different centuries, different audiences, and the same description of the same Lord. When His people were in trouble, the prophets did not invent a new picture of God. They went back to what He had said about Himself.

Wait for It ▶︎

Three of this week's four books tell the reader to wait.

Micah, alone in a dark time: "I will wait for the God of my salvation: my God will hear me" (Micah 7:7). Habakkuk, told that the answer is coming: "though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come" (Habakkuk 2:3). Zephaniah, speaking for the Lord: "Therefore wait ye upon me" (Zephaniah 3:8).

None of them knew how long the wait would be. Micah's Bethlehem promise (5:2) waited centuries for its fulfillment. But it came, in the very town he named.

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

Habakkuk imagined every crop and every flock failing, and then wrote:

"Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." — Habakkuk 3:18

Habakkuk called Him "the God of my salvation" (Habakkuk 3:18). Centuries later an old man named Simeon held the infant Jesus in the temple and said he had now seen that salvation with his own eyes: "For mine eyes have seen thy salvation" (Luke 2:30). The One Habakkuk trusted had come, born in the town Micah named.
