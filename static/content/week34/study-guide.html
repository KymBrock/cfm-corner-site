1. Week 34: Overview

For a thousand years before Christ, when the covenant people wanted to praise, to plead, to repent, or to remember, they reached for the Psalms. The Psalter is Israel's hymnbook and prayer book — one hundred and fifty poems, roughly half of them tied to King David, gathered after the exile into a single book with a deliberate design. This week we read a sampling from its opening third: the gateway psalms (1–2), the great creation poems (8, 19), the suffering-and-shepherd sequence (22–24), and the psalm of stillness (46).

Coming to the Psalms, a reader raised on English poetry may feel lost — there is no rhyme, no regular meter. But Hebrew poetry works differently: it rhymes ideas, not sounds. Its music is made of lines that echo, balance, contrast, and climb. Learning to hear that music is the first skill of reading a psalm, and this week's study guide includes an interactive companion — How Hebrew Poems Are Built — that maps the forms in these very psalms.

The Psalter opens with two psalms that function as its front door. Psalm 1 sets the great choice: the way of the righteous, who delight in the law and are "like a tree planted by the rivers of water," against the way of the ungodly, which "shall perish" (1:3, 6). Psalm 2 lifts the eyes from the individual to the nations, and to the LORD's Anointed King — "Thou art my Son" (2:7) — the first of the Psalter's messianic psalms. Together they announce the two themes the whole book will hold: faithfulness to God's word, and hope in God's King.

Between those doors and the closing hallelujahs, the Psalms run the entire range of the human heart before God. There are psalms of soaring praise (8, 19, 24, 29, 33) and psalms of raw lament (22). There are songs of trust (23), of forgiveness (32), of patience (40), and of stillness under threat (46). The Psalms do not pretend life is only joyful; roughly a third of the Psalter is lament. That is part of their gift: they give us words for the days we have none of our own.

Read down the "Bridge" rows and a pattern appears: they are overwhelmingly laments. The reading

keeps most of the praise and trust psalms and passes over much of the complaint — which is worth

noticing, because complaint is nearly a third of the Psalter. If the Psalms are going to teach us to

pray in the hard seasons as well as the glad ones, the skipped psalms are where much of that

teaching lives.

  • Creation speaks (Psalms 8, 19). Two of the Bible's greatest creation poems. Psalm 8 stands beneath the night sky and asks, "What is man, that thou art mindful of him?" (8:4) — and answers that God has "crowned him with glory and honour" (8:5). Psalm 19 hears the heavens "declare the glory of God" (19:1) and then turns to a second, clearer revelation: "The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul" (19:7).
  • The suffering, the shepherd, the King (Psalms 22–24). These three sit together for a reason. Psalm 22 is the cry of forsakenness — "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (22:1) — with details the Gospels saw fulfilled at Calvary: pierced hands and feet, parted garments (22:16, 18). Psalm 23, the best-loved psalm in the world, answers it: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (23:1). Psalm 24 lifts the eyes to the temple gates and the "King of glory" who enters them (24:7–10).
  • Refuge and stillness (Psalm 46). "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble" (46:1) — and its center holds one of scripture's great commands for the anxious heart: "Be still, and know that I am God" (46:10).

The Psalms are the prayer book Jesus Himself grew up praying. He quoted the Psalms more than any other Old Testament book; from the cross He prayed Psalm 22 and Psalm 31. To read the Psalms is to learn the language of prayer from the people of God — and to discover that many of the songs point beyond David to David's Lord. The suffering of Psalm 22, the shepherd of Psalm 23, the King of Psalm 24, the begotten Son of Psalm 2: the Psalter is, from its opening door, quietly full of Christ.

And it is full of us. Whatever the week has done to your heart — praise or grief, confidence or fear — there is a psalm for it. That is why the covenant people never stopped singing them, and why we still open this book when we do not know how to pray.

2. Week 34: Historical & Cultural Context

The book of Psalms is, in the Bible Project's summary, "a collection of 150 ancient Hebrew poems, songs, and prayers" (Book of Psalms Summary, BibleProject). They were composed across centuries and, after the Babylonian exile, "intentionally arranged" into a book with a design of its own. That design is visible even in English: the Psalter is divided into five books (1–41, 42–72, 73–89, 90–106, 107–150), each closing with a burst of praise — a doxology — and the whole collection ends with five "hallelujah" psalms (BibleProject). The five-fold shape quietly echoes the five books of Moses: as the Torah is God's word to Israel, the Psalms are Israel's word back to God.

(A note for readers comparing Bibles: from Psalm 10 onward the numbering in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles — which follow the ancient Greek and Latin — runs one behind the Hebrew and Protestant numbering, because they count two of the psalms differently. The beloved shepherd psalm is "23" in most English Bibles but "22" in the Latin Vulgate.)

Not all psalms do the same thing, and naming the type helps you read one rightly. The Interpreter Foundation study aid lists the main kinds and even suggests sorting each psalm as you read: is it "one of praise, or petition, or prophecy, or worship, or gratitude, or testimony" (Jonn Claybaugh, Come, Follow Me… Study and Teaching Helps, Interpreter Foundation)? The great categories scholars use are:

  • Hymns of praise — pure adoration of God as Creator and King (Psalms 8, 19, 29, 33).
  • Laments — cries of grief, complaint, and plea, often turning to trust before they end (Psalm 22). Roughly a third of the Psalter is lament.
  • Thanksgiving — gratitude for deliverance already given (Psalm 30).
  • Royal / messianic — psalms of the king, which the tradition read as pointing to the Anointed One to come (Psalms 2, 24).
  • Wisdom — reflection on the two ways of living (Psalm 1).

The Bible Project notes the two dominant moods and where they fall: lament "predominate[s]" in the earlier books, while praise poems outnumber lament toward the end, "culminating in the hallelujah conclusion." The Psalter is a journey from trouble to praise.

The superscriptions attribute many psalms to David — the Interpreter study aid notes he "is credited with having written about half of the psalms, many being pleas and praises to God which evidence David's repentant heart" (Claybaugh). Jewish tradition preserved a fuller picture. The Talmud teaches that "David wrote the book of Psalms by means of ten elders" — incorporating older compositions "authored by Adam the first man, by Melchizedek king of Salem, and by Abraham, and by Moses, and by Heman, and by Jeduthun, and by Asaph," and the sons of Korah (Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 14b–15a). David is the great collector and composer, gathering the prayers of the ages into one book and adding his own.

The rabbis loved the image of David the poet at work. A lyre, they said, "hung over David's bed, and once midnight arrived, the northern midnight wind would come and cause the lyre to play on its own. David would immediately rise from his bed and study Torah until the first rays of dawn" (Berakhot 3b). The Psalms were born, in this telling, in the middle of the night, from a king who could not sleep for wanting to praise.

The Psalms were not written to be read silently; they were sung, and they structured Israel's worship. The Levites sang a set "psalm of the day" in the temple: on the first day of the week, Psalm 24, "The earth is the LORD's" — chosen "in commemoration of the first day of Creation, because on that day He acquired the world" (Rosh Hashanah 31a; Tamid 33b). Groups of psalms had fixed liturgical roles: the Hallel (Psalms 113–118) at the festivals, and the Ashrei (Psalm 145) recited daily. Even Psalm 23 had its place — some sang "The LORD is my shepherd" over the fourth cup of the Passover meal, "in appreciation of the food he ate" (Pesachim 118a).

Traces of the music survive in the text itself. The word Selah, which "occurs in 39 of the Psalms," is "thought to be an instruction related to reading or to music" (Claybaugh) — a pause, or a swell, whose exact meaning is now lost. Many psalms carry musical superscriptions ("To the chief Musician," "upon Gittith," "A Psalm of David"). We are reading the lyrics of songs whose tunes have faded but whose words the covenant people never stopped singing.

Of all the Old Testament, the Psalms left the deepest mark on the New: "of the almost 300 Old Testament quotes that are found in the New Testament, more than 100 come from Psalms" (Claybaugh). Jesus quoted the Psalms more than any other book, prayed them from the cross, and taught that they spoke of Him (Luke 24:44). This is the context that matters most for a Latter-day Saint reader: the Psalms are not only ancient Israel's hymns but the prayer book of the Savior Himself — and, from Psalm 2's begotten Son to Psalm 22's pierced hands to Psalm 24's King of glory, a book quietly full of Christ.

3. Week 34: Key Passages Study

The Text

"When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him? For thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour." (8:3–5)

The Insight

Psalm 8 asks the question every honest soul asks under a night sky: against the scale of the cosmos, what is a human being? The world's answer is nothing much — a speck on a speck. The psalm's answer is the opposite, and it is stunning. Set beside the moon and the stars, humanity is not diminished but crowned — "a little lower than the angels," yet given "glory and honour" and dominion. Both truths are held at once: we are small enough to wonder that God notices us, and dignified enough that He has crowned us. The psalm's frame — the identical line that opens and closes it, "O LORD our Lord, how excellent is thy name" (8:1, 9) — tells us the point is not human greatness but the greatness of the God who stoops to lift us (see Word Studies on kavod, glory).

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • President Boyd K. Packer, "The Pattern of Our Parentage" (October 1984): "What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him?' (Ps. 8:4; see also Heb. 2:5–7.)" — read as a witness of our divine parentage.
  • President Thomas S. Monson, "You Make a Difference" (April 1988): quotes Psalm 8:1, 3–4 — "When I consider thy heavens… What is man, that thou art mindful of him?" — to teach each soul's worth to God.
  • President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, "Forget Me Not" (October 2011): "What is man… For thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour" — God does not forget the small.
  • Scripture: "the worth of souls is great in the sight of God" (D&C 18:10).

Study Questions

  1. Psalm 8 holds our smallness and our crowning together. Which do you more need to hear right now — that you are small before God, or that He has crowned you?
  2. The psalm frames the worth of man inside the majesty of God's name. Why is human dignity safest when it rests on God's greatness rather than our own?

The Text

"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork." (19:1)

"The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple." (19:7)

The Insight

Psalm 19 reads God's two great books. The first is creation — the heavens "declare the glory of God" in a speech without words, "day unto day" and "night unto night" (19:1–2), a witness every nation can see. But creation can only point; it cannot save. So the psalm turns, halfway through, to the second and clearer book: the word of God — "The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul." Notice the movement. The stars show God's power; the law shows God's will, and it does what the stars cannot: it converts, makes wise, rejoices the heart, enlightens the eyes (19:7–8). The same God who wrote the sky wrote the scriptures — and it is the scriptures that turn a soul (see Word Studies on torah).

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Elder Gordon B. Hinckley, "Be Not Faithless" (April 1978): "The heavens declare the glory of God… Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge.' (Ps. 19:1–2.)"
  • Elder Joseph B. Wirthlin, "Pure Testimony" (October 2000): "the Psalmist wrote, 'The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the Lord is sure'" (Psalm 19:7).
  • Elder Don L. Lind, "The Heavens Declare the Glory of God" (October 1985): an astronaut's witness of Psalm 19:1 from the perspective of space.
  • Scripture: "all things denote there is a God; yea, even… all the planets which move in their regular form do witness that there is a Supreme Creator" (Alma 30:44).

Study Questions

  1. Psalm 19 moves from the silent witness of the stars to the converting power of the word. Why can creation point to God but only His word convert the soul?
  2. When did creation last "declare the glory of God" to you — and does that witness send you back to the scriptures, or stop at wonder?

The Text

"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me…?" (22:1)

"…they pierced my hands and my feet." (22:16)

"They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture." (22:18)

The Insight

Psalm 22 is the deepest lament in the Psalter — a righteous sufferer who feels utterly forsaken by God, surrounded by enemies, his life poured out. And it is written a thousand years before the event it seems to describe. On the cross, Jesus prayed its opening line aloud: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). The Gospel writers saw the rest fulfilled before their eyes: the pierced hands and feet (22:16), the soldiers dividing His garments and casting lots for His vesture (22:18; John 19:23–24), the mockery of the onlookers (22:7–8). This is the most-cited psalm in the New Testament. Yet the psalm does not end in the dark: it turns, at verse 22, to praise and deliverance — "I will declare thy name unto my brethren" — so that the same prayer Jesus began in forsakenness ends in triumph. Psalm 22 is the cross and the empty tomb in a single song.

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, "None Were with Him" (April 2009): "He cries in ultimate loneliness, 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?'" — on the Savior's withdrawal of the Spirit's comfort at the last.
  • President Gordon B. Hinckley, "The Empty Tomb Bore Testimony" (April 1988): "He cried out, 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' (Matt. 27:46)."
  • Scripture & study: the Gospels apply Psalm 22 directly to Calvary (Matthew 27:35, 39, 46; John 19:24). The wording of verse 16 ("pierced" vs. the alternative "like a lion") is a known textual question that Latter-day Saint scholarship has examined in light of the Dead Sea Scrolls (see Shon D. Hopkin, "'My God, My God, Why Hast Thou Forsaken Me?' Psalm 22 and the Mission of Christ," BYU Studies Quarterly) — worth reading before teaching the detail.

Study Questions

  1. Jesus chose to pray Psalm 22 from the cross — a psalm that begins in forsakenness and ends in praise. Why might He have reached for these exact words in His darkest hour?
  2. Psalm 22 lets a sufferer say "why hast thou forsaken me?" to God, not away from Him. How does it change grief to know the Savior Himself prayed those words?

The Text

"The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want… Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me… Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life: and I will dwell in the house of the LORD for ever." (23:1, 4, 6)

The Insight

The best-loved poem in the world is only six verses long, and its power is in a quiet turn most readers never notice. It begins by speaking about God — "He maketh me… he leadeth me… he restoreth my soul" (23:2–3). Then, at the exact center, in the valley of the shadow of death, the pronoun changes: "for thou art with me" (23:4). Just where the danger is deepest, the psalmist stops describing God and starts talking to Him. That is the whole doctrine of the psalm: the nearness of God is felt most in the dark. The shepherd who leads becomes the companion who walks beside, and the psalm ends not in a safe pasture but in the "house of the LORD for ever" — provision that runs past death itself (see the chiasm and metaphor maps in How Hebrew Poems Are Built, and Word Studies on nephesh and chesed).

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Elder Robert E. Wells, "Know the Shepherd" (October 1980): recites the whole psalm — "The LORD is my shepherd… my cup runneth over" — and urges us to know the Shepherd, not just know about Him.
  • Elder Gerrit W. Gong, "Good Shepherd, Lamb of God" (April 2019): "With the shepherd boy who became a king, we declare: 'The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. He maketh me to lie down in green pastures… He restoreth my soul.'"
  • Elder Richard G. Scott, "He Lives" (October 1999): "David rejoiced: 'The Lord is my shepherd… though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil.'"
  • Scripture: "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep" (John 10:11) — the LORD of Psalm 23 names Himself.

Study Questions

  1. The psalm turns from "he" to "thou" in the valley. Have you found God's presence more real in your hardest passages than in your easy ones? Why might that be?
  2. "I shall not want" is written by a man who is not promised an easy life — only the Shepherd. What is the difference between not wanting and not lacking?

The Text

"Who shall ascend into the hill of the LORD? or who shall stand in his holy place? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart…" (24:3–4)

"Lift up your heads, O ye gates… and the King of glory shall come in. Who is this King of glory? The LORD strong and mighty… the LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory." (24:7–8, 10)

The Insight

Psalm 24 is a temple psalm — very likely sung antiphonally, by two groups of voices, as worshippers approached the sanctuary gates. It asks the entrance question: who may come up to God's holy hill? The answer is not about lineage or sacrifice but about integrity — "clean hands, and a pure heart" — the outward life and the inward one, both. Then the psalm swings its gaze upward to a greater ascent: the King of glory Himself entering His gates, and the doors commanded to lift up their heads to receive Him. It is a psalm of two processions — the worshipper going up to God, and God coming down to His people — meeting at the temple gate. For Latter-day Saints, "clean hands, and a pure heart" is temple language still: the standard for entering the house of the Lord (see the call-and-response map in How Hebrew Poems Are Built).

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Elder David A. Bednar, "Clean Hands and a Pure Heart" (October 2007): "Consider the penetrating questions posed by the Psalmist: 'Who shall ascend into the hill of the Lord?… He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart…' (Psalm 24:3–4)."
  • Elder Dallin H. Oaks, "Why Do We Serve?" (October 1984): "the high test expressed in the Twenty-fourth Psalm: 'Who shall ascend into the hill of the Lord?… He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart.'"
  • Scripture: "Yea, and the pure in heart, and the clean hands, and the healthy soul, are they whose habitations shall be exalted" (D&C 97:21, cf. Matthew 5:8).

Study Questions

  1. The gate of the holy hill asks for "clean hands and a pure heart" — the outward act and the inward motive. Which is easier for you to keep, and why does God ask for both?
  2. Psalm 24 is a psalm of two ascents: we go up to God, and God comes down to us. How does worship become the place where those two meet?

The Text

"God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." (46:1)

"Be still, and know that I am God: I will be exalted among the heathen, I will be exalted in the earth." (46:10)

The Insight

Psalm 46 is a psalm for the day the world seems to be coming apart — mountains carried into the sea, waters roaring, nations raging (46:2–6). Against all of it stands one steadying fact: "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." Not a distant help, not a help that arrives eventually — a present help, here, now, in the trouble itself. And at the psalm's climax comes the command that has quieted anxious hearts for three thousand years: "Be still, and know that I am God." The stillness is not passivity; it is the deliberate act of stopping our frantic striving long enough to remember who actually governs the world. The antidote to a raging earth is not more effort but a stiller trust.

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Elder Russell M. Nelson, "Blessed Are the Peacemakers" (October 2002): "The Psalmist said, 'God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble.' 'He maketh wars to cease unto the end of the earth.'"
  • President Boyd K. Packer, "Reverence Invites Revelation" (October 1991): "'Be still, and know that I am God.' (Ps. 46:10.)" — the stillness in which the Spirit speaks.
  • Scripture: the Lord's own echo of the phrase — "be still and know that I am God" (D&C 101:16) — spoken to a persecuted people to steady them.

Study Questions

  1. "Be still" is a command, not a suggestion. What would it look like this week to deliberately stop striving long enough to "know that I am God"?
  2. Psalm 46 calls God "a very present help in trouble" — not a help that removes the trouble. How is God's presence in a hard thing different from His taking it away?

4. Week 34: Word Studies

Strong's H8451 — "instruction, law" (Blue Letter Bible), from a root meaning to throw, to point, to aim — and so to point the way, to teach. This is the word we render "law," but its heart is a parent's or teacher's instruction, the finger that points a child in the right direction. That is why the blessed man of Psalm 1 does not merely obey the torah — he delights in it and "meditate[s]" on it "day and night" (1:2). You delight in guidance from someone who loves you; you only endure a legal code.

Strong's H3519 — "glory, honour… abundance… splendour… dignity, reputation" (Blue Letter Bible). The root behind kavod means to be heavy. God's kavod is His weightiness — the felt density of His presence, not its brightness. So when the heavens "declare the glory of God" (19:1), and man is "crowned with glory and honour" (8:5), and the "King of glory" enters His gates (24:7), the word underneath is not shine but substance.

The Greek psychē and Latin anima had come, by the psalmists' translators, to suggest an immortal soul housed within — and separable from — a mortal body, a split the Hebrew simply does not make. When Psalm 23 ends, "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life" (23:6), the word is chesed — and the verb "follow" is stronger than it sounds in English: it means to pursue, chase after. God's covenant love does not trail behind us; it hunts us down all our days.

5. Week 34: Jewish Perspective

Who wrote the Psalms? The Talmud's answer is generous and surprising — David gathered the songs of the ages and made them a book:

"David wrote the book of Psalms by means of ten elders of previous generations… He included psalms authored by Adam the first man, by Melchizedek king of Salem, and by Abraham, and by Moses, and by Heman, and by Jeduthun, and by Asaph… and by the three sons of Korah."

— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 14b–15a (Sefaria, CC BY-NC)

David is not, in this telling, merely the author of "his" psalms; he is the great editor of Israel's prayer, taking up compositions reaching back to Adam himself and forming them into one hymnbook. The Psalter is a chorus of many voices across the ages, gathered by one singing king.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The instinct that scripture is a collected record — many prophets' words gathered and preserved by an inspired compiler — is deeply familiar to Latter-day Saints. Mormon did for the Nephite record what the tradition says David did for Israel's praise: he abridged and arranged the words of many into one book, "that ye might believe the gospel of Jesus Christ" (Mormon 7:9). A hymnbook, like a scripture, is a community's memory made singable.

The rabbis loved to picture David composing — and they set the scene at midnight:

"A lyre hung over David's bed, and once midnight arrived, the northern midnight wind would come and cause the lyre to play on its own. David would immediately rise from his bed and study Torah until the first rays of dawn."

— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 3b (Sefaria, CC BY-NC)

David defends his own piety by this practice: "all of the kings of the East and the West sleep until the third hour of the day, but although I am a king like them, 'At midnight I rise to give thanks'" (Berakhot 4a, quoting Psalm 119:62). The image is of a king so hungry to praise that the night wind itself woke him to it.

Latter-day Saint Connection

David's midnight lyre is a picture of worship that will not wait for convenient hours. "I will sing with the spirit, and I will sing with the understanding also" (1 Corinthians 14:15); "Did I not speak peace to your mind concerning the matter?" (D&C 6:23) — the still hours are often where God is heard. The Psalms were born of a heart that rose in the dark to give thanks; they still teach us to seek God before the day's noise begins.

The rabbis noticed that Psalms 1 and 2 are bound together as a single unit, sealed at both ends by the same word:

"Every chapter that was dear to David, he began with 'happy is' and concluded with 'happy is.' He opened with 'happy is': 'Happy is the man who has not walked in the counsel of the wicked…' (Psalms 1:1). And he concluded with 'happy': '…Happy are those who take refuge in Him' (Psalms 2:12). We see that these two chapters actually constitute a single chapter."

— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 10a (Sefaria, CC BY-NC)

The Hebrew word is ashrei — "happy, blessed, fortunate." The rabbis heard Psalms 1–2 as one great gateway, framed front and back by blessedness: the psalm begins by blessing the one who delights in the law, and ends by blessing the one who trusts in God's Anointed. Torah and Messiah, held inside a single frame of joy.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The inclusio the rabbis heard — a unit framed at both ends by "blessed" — is the very form the Savior chose for the Sermon on the Mount, opening with the Beatitudes, the "blesseds" (Matthew 5:3–12; 3 Nephi 12:3–12). To begin and end with blessing is to say that the whole way between them — delighting in the word, trusting in the King — is the path of happiness. (On this framing device, see the interactive How Hebrew Poems Are Built.)

When Psalm 8 asks, "What is man, that thou art mindful of him?" (8:4), the rabbis heard in it an old heavenly argument. In their retelling, when God proposed to create humanity, the angels quoted this very verse in protest:

"The angels said before him: Master of the Universe: 'What is man that You are mindful of him? And the son of man that You think of him?' (Psalms 8:5), i.e., a creature such as this is not worth creating."

— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 38b (Sefaria, CC BY-NC)

In the parallel midrash the angels finally yield, closing with the psalm's own last line: "'Lord, our Master, how mighty is Your name throughout the world' (Psalms 8:10) – do what pleases You" (Genesis Rabbah 8:6). The rabbis turned the psalm's question into the great debate of creation: is this small creature worth it? — and let God's answer be the making of us.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The Restoration answers the angels' objection with a resounding yes — and reveals why. Humanity is worth creating because "this is my work and my glory — to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man" (Moses 1:39). The "son of man" the angels doubted is crowned "with glory and honour" (Psalm 8:5) precisely because he is a child of God with a divine destiny. What the angels questioned, the plan of salvation vindicates.

Psalm 24 was the temple's Sunday song. The Levites sang it as the psalm of the first day of the week — and the rabbis explained why:

"On the first day of the week… the psalm beginning with the phrase: 'The earth is the Lord's, and its fullness' (Psalms 24:1), in commemoration of the first day of Creation, because on that day He acquired the world and transferred it to man."

— Babylonian Talmud, Rosh Hashanah 31a (Sefaria, CC BY-NC)

Its command to the gates — "Lift up your heads, O ye gates… that the King of glory may come in" (24:7) — generated a dramatic tradition about Solomon dedicating the temple: the gates would not open until, after reciting the psalm, he invoked the merit of his father David (Shabbat 30a). The psalm of entering God's holy hill became, in Jewish memory, the very words that opened the temple doors.

Latter-day Saint Connection

Psalm 24's entrance liturgy — who shall ascend the hill of the Lord? answered by he that hath clean hands, and a pure heart — is temple language Latter-day Saints still live. The house of the Lord still asks the same question at its door, and the same answer opens it. "The earth is the LORD's, and the fulness thereof" (24:1) is echoed in modern revelation: "the earth is full, and there is enough and to spare" (D&C 104:17) — the world belongs to God, entrusted to His covenant people.

Above all, the Psalms became Jewish prayer. The Talmud fixes the Hallel (Psalms 113–118) at the festivals and promises that whoever "recites: 'A Psalm of David' (Psalms 145) three times every day is assured of a place in the World-to-Come" (Berakhot 4b). And in a striking reading, the rabbis put Psalm 22 — "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" — on the lips of Queen Esther as she risked her life before the king and felt God's presence withdraw (Megillah 15b): the psalm of forsakenness became the prayer of every believer who has felt God's face hidden.

Latter-day Saint Connection

That the Psalms became the very words of prayer — recited daily, leaned on in crisis — points to their highest use. The Savior did the same: He prayed Psalm 22 from the cross and Psalm 31 as He died (Luke 23:46). When we do not know how to pray, the Psalms are still doing what they have always done — putting God's own words back into the mouths of His children, in praise and in the dark alike.

6. Week 34: Teaching Applications

The single most transformative thing a class can do with the Psalms is to start praying them. The Psalter is God's gift of words for the times we have none of our own — words for praise (Psalm 8), for trust (Psalm 23), for forgiveness (Psalm 32), for fear (Psalm 46). Application: invite class members to choose one psalm from this week and pray it slowly, in the first person, as their own prayer this week — even reading it aloud. Teach that this is exactly what Israel did, what the Savior did, and what the Saints have always done: "I will sing with the spirit, and I will sing with the understanding also" (1 Corinthians 14:15).

Roughly a third of the Psalms are laments, and they do something our culture rarely permits: they let a believer say "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1) to God, honestly, without pretending. Application: ask whether anyone has felt that faith requires hiding pain from God. Show that the Savior Himself prayed a lament from the cross — and that Psalm 22 turns, ending in praise. Laments are not a failure of faith; they are faith bringing its whole broken heart to God and trusting Him to be there. This is powerful for anyone carrying a grief they've felt they must keep quiet.

Psalm 23 is prayed at more deathbeds and gravesides than any other passage in scripture, and for good reason: its center is God's presence in the valley — "for thou art with me" (23:4). Application: as a ministering tool, teach members to know the Shepherd, in Elder Robert E. Wells's phrase ("Know the Shepherd," October 1980), before the valley comes — so the psalm is already theirs when they need it. Consider committing Psalm 23 to memory as a family; it is a lamp you can carry into any dark passage, your own or another's.

Psalm 46:10 is not a platitude; it is a command with a method — and the Key Passages section unpacks what that stillness is and is not. Application: teach "be still" as an actual practice — a pause, a breath, a moment of quiet before prayer, in which we consciously hand the raging things back to God, "a very present help in trouble" (46:1). President Boyd K. Packer taught that reverent stillness is where revelation comes ("Reverence Invites Revelation," October 1991). Invite class members to build one still moment into each anxious day this week.

The Savior quoted the Psalms more than any other book and prayed them at the deepest moments of His life. Application: teach the class to read the Psalms Christologically — watching for where David's words reach beyond David. Trace the thread through this week's reading: the begotten Son of Psalm 2, the crowned "son of man" of Psalm 8, the pierced sufferer of Psalm 22, the shepherd-King of Psalms 23–24. Then read Luke 24:44, where the risen Lord says "all things must be fulfilled… in the psalms, concerning me." The Psalms are not only Israel's prayer book; they are a portrait of Christ drawn a thousand years early.

We are reading the lyrics of songs. The Psalms shaped Israel's worship in music, and many of our own hymns are Psalms set to melody — "The Lord Is My Shepherd" (Hymns, no. 108) is Psalm 23. Application: open or close with a hymn drawn from a psalm, and point out to the class that they are singing scripture. Discuss how music does something to a truth that reading alone cannot — how a psalm sung lodges in the heart and returns unbidden in hard hours. (For how the Hebrew poetry itself was built to be heard, see How Hebrew Poems Are Built.)

Psalm 1 draws the clearest line in scripture between two paths — the one that "delight[s]… in the law of the LORD" and flourishes "like a tree planted by the rivers of water," and the one that "shall perish" (1:2–3, 6). Application: with youth, make the tree concrete: what are the "rivers of water" that keep a life fed and green (scripture, prayer, Sabbath, covenant), and what happens to a tree cut off from water? Invite each person to name one "river" they will stay planted beside this week. Psalm 1 is the whole Psalter's front door — and a clear, hopeful map for a young life choosing its way.

7. Week 34: Study Questions

The Psalms reward slow, personal reading. Choose a few of these — and where a question asks you to pray or remember, actually do it, rather than only discussing it.

The Two Doors (Psalms 1–2)

  1. Psalm 1 pictures the faithful life as "a tree planted by the rivers of water" (1:3). What are the "rivers" that keep your own life fed and green — and are you staying planted beside them?
  2. Psalm 1 delights in "the law of the LORD" (1:2). Knowing the Hebrew torah means instruction more than legislation (see Word Studies), how might your relationship to the commandments change if you heard them as a loving Parent's teaching?
  3. Psalm 2 answers rebellion not with argument but with a King — "Thou art my Son" (2:7). Why do you think the Psalter puts the Messiah on its very first pages?

The Worth of a Soul (Psalm 8)

  1. Under the night sky, Psalm 8 asks, "What is man, that thou art mindful of him?" (8:4). When you feel small and unnoticed, does the vastness of creation make you feel less significant or more — and why?
  2. The psalm says God has "crowned him with glory and honour" (8:5). What would it look like to live this week as someone God has crowned, not merely made?

Two Books of Revelation (Psalm 19)

  1. Psalm 19 hears creation "declare the glory of God" (19:1) and then turns to the word that "convert[s] the soul" (19:7). Why can nature point to God but only His word change us?
  2. When did creation last testify of God to you? Did that wonder send you back to the scriptures, or did it stop at the view?

The Cross and the Shepherd (Psalms 22–24)

  1. Jesus prayed Psalm 22 — a psalm of forsakenness — from the cross. How does it comfort you (or unsettle you) that the Savior reached for words of abandonment in His last hour?
  2. Psalm 23 turns from speaking about God ("he leadeth me") to speaking to God ("thou art with me") exactly at the valley of the shadow (23:4). Have you found God's presence most real in your hardest passages? Why might that be?
  3. "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me" (23:6) — but the Hebrew chesed means God's covenant love pursues you, all your days. How does it change your week to picture God's loyal love chasing you rather than trailing behind?
  4. Psalm 24 asks who may "ascend into the hill of the LORD" and answers, "clean hands, and a pure heart" (24:3–4) — the outward act and the inward motive. Which is harder for you to keep, and why does God ask for both?

Refuge and Stillness (Psalm 46)

  1. "Be still, and know that I am God" (46:10) is a command, not a suggestion. What is the frantic striving in your life that God may be asking you to stop long enough to know Him?
  2. Psalm 46 calls God "a very present help in trouble" (46:1) — help in it, not necessarily out of it. How is God's presence within a hard thing different from His removing it?

The Psalms as a Whole

  1. Roughly a third of the Psalms are laments. Why do you think God gave His people so many words for grief, complaint, and fear — and what does that permission mean for your own prayers?
  2. The Savior said "all things must be fulfilled… in the psalms, concerning me" (Luke 24:44). As you read this week, where did you see Christ in David's words?
  3. The Psalms were sung. Is there a hymn drawn from a psalm that has returned to you in a hard hour? What does music do to a truth that reading alone does not?
  4. Jewish tradition says David rose at midnight to praise (see Jewish Perspective). What would it take to build one deliberate moment of praise into your day, before the noise begins?
  5. If you could keep only one psalm from this week's reading to carry with you, which would it be — and what does that choice reveal about where your heart is right now?
