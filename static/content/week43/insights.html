First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week we finished Isaiah, and the book ends looking far ahead: "the new heavens and the new earth, which I will make, shall remain before me, saith the LORD, so shall your seed and your name remain" (Isaiah 66:22).

This week the voice changes. Isaiah preached while Assyria threatened Jerusalem. Jeremiah preaches as Babylon rises, in the last decades before the city falls; for some of those years Lehi was preaching there too. And where Isaiah's book is mostly his message, Jeremiah's book lets us see the messenger: his fear, his arguments with God, his tears, his worst day.

The reading skips around (chapters 1–3, then 7, then 16–18, then 20), but the Overview walks all twenty chapters in order, so you can see what happens in between.

A Prophet Who Said "I Cannot Speak" ▶︎

This is the thing most worth knowing before you start: Jeremiah did not feel qualified, and the book never pretends he did.

His first words to God are "I cannot speak: for I am a child" (1:6). Later he tells God, "thou hast deceived me" (20:7). He asks why the wicked prosper (12:1). He wishes he had never been born (20:14). And yet he keeps going, for more than forty years, through mockery, plots and the stocks (a restraint for prisoners, 20:2).

What kept him going was not confidence. It was the Lord's two answers on the day of his call: "I have put my words in thy mouth" (1:9), and "I am with thee to deliver thee" (1:8). The Overview follows the first answer from chapter 1 to the fire in his bones in chapter 20. Key Passages, passage 7, follows the second, the promise that his enemies "shall not prevail."

If you have ever been asked to do something you did not feel ready for, Jeremiah is good company. The Lord did not wait for him to feel ready. He knew him first.

The Fountain You Do Not Have to Fill ▶︎

There is a kind of tiredness that comes from trying to fill something that will not hold.

In Jeremiah 2:13 the Lord says His people "have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water." In their land a fountain was a spring, where the water welled up without anyone filling it. A cistern was a tank cut into rock to store rain, and families needed theirs; digging one was good, ordinary work. The wrong was leaving the living source and making something of their own the thing they relied on instead. And a cracked cistern could not even keep what it held.

Most of us know some version of this. Approval, accomplishment, a comfortable evening at home: none of these is wicked, and many of them are gifts. The trouble starts when we ask one of them to be the source, to tell us we are enough or to keep us safe. Then it has to be filled again tomorrow, and it still leaks.

The verse does not scold the thirst. It points to a source: "the fountain of living waters." A fountain is not filled by the person drinking. It keeps rising. That does not make life effortless; people still walked to the spring and carried the water home. Nor does it mean a faithful person never feels dry. Jeremiah, the Lord's own prophet, at his lowest asked whether God would be to him "as waters that fail" (15:18). Two chapters later the book calls the Lord "the fountain of living waters" again, in a verse that still admits the people had forsaken Him (17:13). Centuries later the Savior sat by a well and offered "living water" to a woman who had come to draw water at Jacob's well (John 4:10). Key Passages, passage 2, follows the fountain into the rest of scripture, and Word Studies, study 2, shows why Hebrew calls such water living.

The fountain also invites trust. We draw what we need today, trusting the Lord’s continued faithfulness rather than believing everything depends on what we can secure for ourselves. That trust does not eliminate work or preparation. It changes where our confidence rests. We still carry the water, but we do not have to create its source. Faith means continuing to turn toward Him, even when we cannot yet see how tomorrow’s needs will be met.

Jeremiah 17 paints the same picture. The one "that trusteth in the LORD" is "as a tree planted by the waters," which "shall not be careful in the year of drought" (Jeremiah 17:7–8). Careful there means anxious: the tree still lives through the drought, but it is not ruled by it (Key Passages, passage 5, reads the two trees).

If you would like to follow the roots further. A companion study, Tree & Menorah, grew from our study of Isaiah's trees. There a root rots and a blossom turns to dust when the Lord's word is despised (Isaiah 5:24). Then new life comes: a Branch grows out of Jesse's roots (Isaiah 11:1), a promise fulfilled in Christ. Jeremiah's tree by the waters pictures that life in anyone who trusts the Lord. A question about sources. Nephi, shown in vision what his father had dreamed, saw two kinds of water. The rod of iron, the word of God, "led to the fountain of living waters," and those waters "are a representation of the love of God" (1 Nephi 11:25). The river of "filthy water" stood for filthiness and for "an awful gulf, which separated the wicked from the tree of life" (1 Nephi 12:16; 15:26–29). As a personal application, and not something Nephi himself says, those two waters can prompt a question about where our understanding comes from. Ideas about the gospel pass from one person to another, and along the way they are sometimes made clearer and sometimes bent. A fair test is whether an interpretation brings us closer to the Lord or carries us away from Him. That goes for commentary too, this guide included: it is meant to send you back to the scriptures and to the Lord in prayer, not to stand in their place. Careful study of the Hebrew and Greek can help us read the words in their context, but it does not replace His guidance.

So it is worth asking this week: what have I been asking to be my source of worth or safety that was only ever meant to be a cistern?

While the Clay Is Still Soft ▶︎

In chapter 18 the Lord sends Jeremiah "down to the potter's house" (18:2). He watches the potter working at his wheel. The pot goes wrong while it is still being shaped, and the potter does not throw the clay away: "he made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter to make it" (18:4). Then the Lord applies the scene to His people: "as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are ye in mine hand, O house of Israel" (18:6). He adds that a warning is not His last word: if a nation He has warned turns from its evil, He will hold back what He had warned of (18:7–8).

The scene is usually read as a picture of God's power over the clay. It is just as much a picture of His patience with it. Key Passages, passage 6, walks through the chapter, and through chapter 19, where a pot that has already been fired can no longer be reshaped, only broken.

What is worth carrying into your own week is the moment just before the people's reply. The Lord sent Jeremiah to "the men of Judah, and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem" with a plea: "return ye now every one from his evil way" (18:11). They answered, "There is no hope: but we will walk after our own devices" (18:12). In their mouths, "no hope" was not despair. It was a refusal, a way of turning down a return they had just been offered.

If some part of your life feels marred, the potter's house is good news for you: in 18:4 the marred vessel is exactly the one the potter makes again. The text does not say why the vessel was marred, only what the potter did next. And if you have ever said "there is no hope" in a hard season, that is not Judah's answer; theirs came with a decision to keep walking away. Where turning back is what you need, the Lord's invitation in chapter 3 is still open to you: "Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings" (3:22). In Hebrew, return and backsliding come from the same root (Word Studies, study 3).

When You Cannot Hold It In ▶︎

In chapter 20 Jeremiah decides to stop speaking in the Lord's name. He has been mocked "daily" (20:7–8), beaten, and held in the stocks overnight (20:2–3). He says, "I will not make mention of him, nor speak any more in his name."

Then: "his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was weary with forbearing, and I could not stay" (20:9).

Elder Jeffrey R. Holland called this the turning point of Jeremiah's life; Key Passages, passage 7, gives his account.

That is worth hearing on the weeks when scripture study feels dry. The word may be doing more in you than you can feel. Five chapters earlier, at another low point, Jeremiah wrote, "Thy words were found, and I did eat them; and thy word was unto me the joy and rejoicing of mine heart" (15:16). The words he ate in chapter 15 were the fire in chapter 20.

And then, honestly, the chapter ends in despair (20:14–18). The book lets the fire and the despair both stand. If you are somewhere in the dark part right now, Jeremiah's book is evidence that the Lord does not let go of the people He has called. He kept the fire burning in a man who wished he had never been born.

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

The Lord said it to a frightened young man on the first day, and repeated it later, when everything had gone wrong (15:20):

"Be not afraid of their faces: for I am with thee to deliver thee, saith the LORD." — Jeremiah 1:8

The promise is not that you will face nothing hard; Jeremiah faced almost everything. The promise is presence: I am with thee.

The same Lord who knew Jeremiah before he was formed knows you. And on the day you are tempted to say I cannot speak, He has a promise ready.
