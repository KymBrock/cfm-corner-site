1. Week 36: Overview

Every book of Psalms you have read so far has been building toward this week, and most readers never notice.

The Hebrew name for the book is Tehillim — "Praises." That has been a slightly odd name all along, because roughly a third of the Psalms are laments: people in trouble, saying so plainly. Last week you read the worst of them — a man who had done something no offering covered, another lying awake asking whether God had stopped caring. If you had to name that book from its contents, Praises is not the word you would choose.

This week is why it is called that. The Psalter — the old name for the whole book of Psalms — does not simply stop; it climbs. The last five psalms — 146 through 150 — each open and close with the same two words, "Praise ye the LORD," and they get shorter and louder until the very last verse of the very last psalm, which is one line long:

"Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD." (150:6)

That is the end of a book that spent a hundred and forty-nine chapters being honest about how hard life is. It does not arrive at praise by pretending the laments away. It arrives by going through them — which is the only route any of us gets.

And on the way there, this week hands you some of the most-loved lines in scripture. "Bless the LORD, O my soul" (103:1). "As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us" (103:12). "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet" (119:105). "Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it" (127:1). "This is the day which the LORD hath made" (118:24). If you have sung a hymn or heard a talk in the last month, you have probably met one of them already.

One more thing before you begin, and it is the reason this week matters most. Psalms 113 through 118 are a set the Jews call the Hallel — the praise psalms, sung together at Passover, still sung at Passover today. Three of them are in your reading. And on the night before He died, at the end of the Passover supper, Matthew records something easy to read past: "And when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30).

The hymn was almost certainly these psalms. So this week you are not only reading about the Savior — you are reading the words He sang, hours before Gethsemane.

The psalm the Savior used to end an argument. Psalm 110 is short and it is quoted in the New Testament more than almost any other. Its first line is strange in English: "The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand" (110:1) — two different words, both printed Lord. The Savior asked the Pharisees about exactly that. If the Messiah is simply David's descendant, why does David call Him my Lord? "And no man was able to answer him a word" (Matthew 22:46). He was pointing at a psalm and asking them to notice that David was writing about someone greater than himself.

And verse 4 is why this psalm matters so much to us: "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." It is the only place outside Genesis that the Old Testament mentions Melchizedek at all — and the priesthood every worthy man in the Church holds carries that name because of this verse and its use in Hebrews.

The longest chapter in the Bible is built out of the alphabet. Psalm 119 runs 176 verses, and its shape is not decoration — it is the argument. Twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, eight verses each, and in Hebrew every verse of a stanza begins with that stanza's letter. Your King James Bible prints the letter names right in the text; look at verse 105 and you will see NUN sitting above "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet."

Why build a poem that way? Because the psalm is about loving God's word, and it says so using every letter there is — the whole alphabet, the raw material of writing itself, turned toward one subject. A poem about scripture, made out of the building blocks of scripture. And it ends, after 175 verses of devotion to God's law, with the most human line imaginable: "I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant" (119:176).

The last word of the Psalter is "breath," and it is the same breath God breathed into Adam.

Psalm 150 ends: "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD." The Hebrew for breath there is neshāmâ (הַנְּשָׁמָה) — and it is the same word Genesis uses when God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7, nishmat chayyim, נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים). The book of Praises ends by calling on everything that carries the breath God gave it to give some of that breath back.

The old translators heard it. Jerome's Latin renders the line "Omnis spiritus laudet Dominum" — and spiritus, like the Greek pneuma (πνεῦμα), means breath and Spirit at once. That double meaning is not a stretch; it is why the Holy Ghost is called what He is called. Breath, wind, Spirit — one word, in both languages.

And there is a connection worth mentioning honestly, because it is lovely and it is not settled. The Latin word for a musical note in medieval chant is neume — the marks written above the words of a psalm to show how to sing it. It is traditionally derived from that same Greek pneuma, breath; but the derivation is contested, and other scholars trace it instead to Greek neuma, "a nod" or "a sign," which fits a written mark just as well. Our own sources do not settle it, and this guide will not pretend to. Take it as a suggested connection rather than an established one — the kind of thing worth knowing about and worth being careful with.

What is not in doubt is the thing underneath it: singing is done on the breath, breath and Spirit are the same word in the languages of scripture, and the Lord told Emma Smith that "the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me" (D&C 25:12). A psalm sung is breath returned to the God who gave it — which is as close to a definition of worship as you are likely to find.

A psalm sung by two choirs. Psalm 136 is called the Great Hallel, and it has one feature you can see at a glance: every single verse — all twenty-six — ends with "for his mercy endureth for ever." That was not a printing quirk. One voice sang the line, and everyone else answered with the refrain, over and over, twenty-six times. Read it out loud with your family, one person taking the first half and everyone else the response, and you will have done exactly what Israel did with it.

Two things to know before the table.

The Psalms are five books, and this week you are finishing the last one. Book Five runs from Psalm 107 to the end, and it is where the Psalter gathers its collections for the close: the Hallel (113–118), the Songs of Ascents (120–134), and the final Hallelujah psalms (146–150). Most of this week sits inside it. (The five-book structure is explained in Historical & Cultural Context.)

The Songs of Ascents were walking songs. Psalms 120–134 were sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for the festivals — up, because Jerusalem sits on a height and you climbed to reach it. Psalms 127 and 128 are in your reading, and they are family songs: the house, the children, the bread you eat. Ordinary people singing about ordinary life on the road to the temple.

There is a reason the Psalms end this way, and it is worth carrying past this week.

The book could have ended at Psalm 137, by the rivers of Babylon, with the harps hung up in the trees and nobody able to sing. That would have been an honest ending — and for a while it was the true one. Instead it ends with every instrument named and every breathing thing called to praise. Not because the grief was cancelled, but because it was not the last word.

That order matters. A hundred and forty-nine chapters of praise and lament, argument and repentance, exile and homecoming — and then the doxology. The Psalms earn their ending. They do not hand it to you on the first page, and they do not pretend you can skip to it.

And the Savior is in every part of that climb. He is the my Lord of Psalm 110 that David could not fully explain. He is the stone the builders refused in Psalm 118, and He said so out loud. He is the priest after the order of Melchizedek, which is the reason the priesthood in your ward carries that name. He sang Psalms 116 through 118 with His Apostles on the last night of His life — praise psalms, at a table, hours before the garden. He praised God on the way to Gethsemane. If He could sing that night, the ending of this book is not naive. It is the truth learned the hardest way anyone has ever learned it.

So take this week as it comes. If you are somewhere in Psalm 102, at the end of what you have, that is a real place and the Psalter has a chapter for it. If you are somewhere in Psalm 137, unable to sing in a strange land, that is here too, and nobody edited it out.

But read to the end. "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD" — and the point of that last line is its reach. Not everything that understands. Not everything that has it figured out. Everything that breathes. Which, this morning, includes you.

2. Week 36: Historical & Cultural Context

This week you finish the book. Understanding how it was assembled makes the last psalms land harder, because the ending was built — somebody chose to put those five psalms there.

The Psalms are five collections, not one. Week 35 introduced that; here is what matters for this week: Book Five runs from Psalm 107 to Psalm 150, and it is where the Psalter gathers its groups for the close.

Every one of the first four books ends the same way — a short burst of praise tacked on to the last psalm, which scholars call a doxology (from Greek doxa, "glory," and logos, "word": a word of glory). Psalm 41 ends "Blessed be the LORD God of Israel… Amen, and Amen." So do Psalms 72, 89 and 106.

Book Five does not end with a doxology. It ends with five of them. Psalms 146 through 150 each open and close with "Praise ye the LORD" — the Hebrew is one word, halelû-yāh (הַלְלוּ־יָהּ), "praise Yah," using the short form of the divine name. The book does not finish; it accelerates.

And it is not a coincidence of length. Read them in order and watch them get shorter: Psalm 146 has ten verses, 147 has twenty, 148 fourteen, 149 nine, 150 has six — and the last of those six is a single line. A book of a hundred and fifty poems ends on one sentence.

Psalms 113–118 are sung together at Passover, and have been for a very long time. Jewish practice calls them the Hallel — simply "the praise" — and splits them around the meal: 113–114 before, 115–118 after.

Three of them are in your reading, and 118 is the last one, the psalm that ends the meal.

Now set that beside Matthew's account of the Last Supper. It was a Passover meal (Matthew 26:17–19). And when it was over: "And when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30).

The hymn was almost certainly the Hallel — which means the last thing the Savior sang before Gethsemane included these words:

"The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner." (118:22)

"This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it." (118:24)

"O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever." (118:29)

He sang about the rejected stone, and about rejoicing in the day, on the night He was betrayed.

A caution about certainty. The Gospels do not name the hymn. That it was the Hallel is a strong inference from Passover practice, not a stated fact, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. What is certain is that He sang — Matthew says so — and that the psalms sung at that meal were these.

Psalms 120–134 all carry the same heading: "A Song of degrees" in the King James, or "of ascents" — Hebrew maʿălôt (מַעֲלוֹת), "goings up."

Three explanations have been offered and they are not exclusive:

  • Pilgrims sang them going up to Jerusalem. The city sits on a ridge, so you ascended to

it from any direction. Israelites travelled there three times a year for the festivals.

  • The Levites sang them on the fifteen steps of the temple — one psalm per step, and there

are fifteen psalms.

  • They were sung by the exiles going up from Babylon.

Psalms 127 and 128 are your two, and they are domestic — the house, the children, the bread. Ordinary family life, sung on a journey to worship. That combination is the point: these are not psalms about temple ritual. They are psalms about home, sung on the way to the temple.

Psalm 127 carries a heading no other Song of Ascents has: "for Solomon." The psalm about building a house is attributed to the man who built the temple — and it opens by saying the builder is not the one who matters: "Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it."

Psalm 110 is short, strange, and quoted in the New Testament more than almost any other psalm.

Its opening line is confusing in English on purpose. "The LORD said unto my Lord" (110:1) — two different Hebrew words behind the two Lords. The first is the covenant name; the second is ʾādôn (אָדוֹן), an ordinary word for a master or superior. David, the king, is calling someone else my Lord — and that is the whole puzzle.

The Savior put it to the Pharisees directly. If the Messiah is simply David's son, "how then doth David in spirit call him Lord?" (Matthew 22:43–45). "And no man was able to answer him a word" (22:46).

Verse 4 is why this psalm matters so much to Latter-day Saints: "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." Outside Genesis 14, this is the only place the Old Testament mentions Melchizedek at all. The book of Hebrews builds its entire argument about Christ's priesthood on it (Hebrews 5:6; 7:17), and the priesthood held in every ward carries that name because of this verse.

(The Interpreter Foundation's article on Melchizedek treats Psalm 110:4 directly — it is filed under the Genesis 18–23 lesson rather than a Psalms one, which is why it is easy to miss.)

176 verses, twenty-two stanzas, eight verses each. Twenty-two is the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet, and in Hebrew every verse of a stanza begins with that stanza's letter — eight verses starting with aleph, then eight with beth, and so on to tav.

Your King James Bible prints the letter names as headings, so you can see the structure without reading Hebrew. Look above verse 105 and you will find NUN.

Why write a poem that way? Two reasons usually given, and they work together:

  • It is a memory aid. An acrostic is far easier to memorise, and this is a psalm about

keeping God's word in the heart (119:11).

  • It is a claim about completeness. Every letter, aleph to tav — the whole alphabet

turned toward one subject. Everything language can say, said about the word of God.

Nearly every verse mentions God's word under one of eight terms — law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, judgments, word, ways. Eight terms, eight verses per stanza. Whether that is deliberate is debated; that the psalm is saturated with them is not.

Psalm 137 is the one psalm in this week that does not praise. "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion" (137:1). The exiles have been asked to perform their temple songs for their captors, and they refuse: "How shall we sing the LORD'S song in a strange land?" (137:4).

It is worth noticing where the editors put it. Psalm 137 sits in Book Five, on the way to the Hallelujah psalms — not edited out, not softened, and not last. The book that ends in praise keeps the psalm about being unable to sing.

Its final verses are the hardest in the Psalter, invoking violence against Babylon's children. This guide does not soften them and does not recommend them as a prayer. They are the words of people who had watched exactly that done to their own city. Reading them honestly means recognising real grief and real rage in scripture, spoken to God rather than acted on — which is what the Psalms consistently do with both.

3. Week 36: Key Passages Study

Five passages carry this week. Each is given its text, what it is doing, and where it connects.

The Text

8 The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and plenteous in mercy.

10 He hath not dealt with us after our sins; nor rewarded us according to our iniquities.

12 As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us.

13 Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him.

14 For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust.

How the Passage Works

Verse 10 is the hinge, and it is easy to read past. "He hath not dealt with us after our sins." That is not saying God overlooks sin. It is saying He does not give us what the transaction would require — which is the whole difference between justice alone and mercy.

Verse 12 is a deliberately unmeasurable distance. East and west never meet. Had the psalmist said north and south he would have given you two poles and a fixed span between them; east and west run away from each other forever. The removal has no far side.

And verse 14 is the tenderest line about God's patience in scripture. "He knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." Not He tolerates that we are dust — He remembers it. The word for frame is yēṣer (יֵצֶר), from the verb used of a potter shaping clay, the same verb in Genesis 2:7 when God formed man of the dust. God remembers what He made us out of, and is gentle accordingly.

Verse 8's "slow to anger" is, in Hebrew, "long of nostril" — the flaring nose being how the language pictures anger, so a long one is a long fuse. (Full study: Word Studies, study 4.)

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Doctrine and Covenants 58:42 — *"Behold, he who has repented of his sins, the same is

forgiven, and I, the Lord, remember them no more."* Psalm 103:12 removes the sin; D&C 58:42 says the Lord's memory goes with it. The same promise, a hundred generations apart.

  • Verse 13's fatherhood is doctrine, not metaphor, for us. *"Like as a father pitieth his

children"* reads differently when the Father is literally that. The psalm's comfort rests on a family relationship we affirm as real.

  • Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, on the reach of that mercy, in a talk built on the labourers

hired at the eleventh hour: "It is never too late so long as the Master of the vineyard says there is time" (The Laborers in the Vineyard, April 2012). A thematic parallel rather than a treatment of this psalm — he does not cite Psalm 103 — but it is the same claim about how God deals with people who have fallen behind.

Study Questions

  1. Verse 10 says God has not dealt with us after our sins. What would it look like if He did?
  2. Why east and west rather than any other pair of directions?
  3. What changes if you read verse 14 as God remembering rather than merely tolerating?

The Text

1 The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool.

4 The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek.

How the Passage Works

Two different words are printed Lord in verse 1, and the difference is the point. The first is the covenant name; the second is ʾādôn (אָדוֹן), a title for a superior. So the line reads: the LORD said to my master. David — Israel's king, with no earthly superior — is calling someone else his master.

The Savior used exactly that to close an argument. Asked whose son the Messiah is, He quoted this verse and asked: "If David then call him Lord, how is he his son?" (Matthew 22:45). "And no man was able to answer him a word."

Verse 4 opens a second door. "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." Melchizedek appears in Genesis 14 — a priest-king of Salem who blesses Abraham — and then vanishes from the Old Testament except here. A single verse, in a short psalm, becomes the foundation of the book of Hebrews' argument that Christ holds a priesthood older and greater than Aaron's (Hebrews 5:6; 7:17).

And it is why the priesthood in your ward has that name.

  • Alma 13:14–19 treats Melchizedek at greater length than the entire Old Testament does —

  2. What does "a priest for ever" claim that an Aaronic, inherited priesthood could not?
  3. Why might the Lord have sworn an oath about this, when scripture says elsewhere He does not repent?

The Text

22 The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner.

24 This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it.

26 Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the LORD.

29 O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever.

How the Passage Works

Psalm 118 is the last psalm of the Passover meal, and Matthew records that after the Last Supper "when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30). (Why that is almost certainly this psalm: Historical & Cultural Context, section 2.)

Verse 22 is a builder's image. A stone is rejected as unusable, and turns out to be the one the whole corner rests on. The Savior applied it to Himself, out loud, in the temple, days before He died — and then asked the chief priests, "Did ye never read in the scriptures…?" (Matthew 21:42). Peter used it again before the very council that had condemned Him (Acts 4:11).

Verse 26 is spoken twice in the Gospels, by opposite parties, and this is worth slowing down for. The crowd shouts it at Him on Palm Sunday: "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" (Matthew 21:9). Days later He says it back to them — as a prophecy about a city that would not receive Him: "Ye shall not see me henceforth, till ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" (Matthew 23:39).

Verse 24 is the one you have heard in a hundred talks — "This is the day which the LORD hath made" — and it belongs to this setting. It was sung at the table on the night of the betrayal.

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Ephesians 2:20 — Christ as "the chief corner stone." Paul is reading this psalm.
  • Jacob 4:15–17 treats the same image at length: the Jews *"by the stumbling of the Jews

they will reject the stone upon which they might build and have safe foundation."* The Book of Mormon works Psalm 118's image into a prophecy of its own.

  • Verse 24 as a discipline, not a mood. The psalm does not say this is a good day. It says

The Text

9 Wherewithal shall a young man cleanse his way? by taking heed thereto according to thy word.

11 Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee.

105 Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path.

How the Passage Works

The longest chapter in the Bible is built out of the Hebrew alphabet — twenty-two stanzas, eight verses each, every verse of a stanza beginning with that stanza's letter. Your King James Bible prints the letter names; NUN stands above verse 105. (Full structure: Historical & Cultural Context, section 5.)

Verse 105's image is smaller than it sounds. A lamp in that world was a clay dish of oil with a wick — enough light for the next few steps, not for the whole road. The psalm does not promise to show you the destination. It promises light where your foot is about to land.

Verse 11 is about storage, not study. "Hid in mine heart" — put somewhere it cannot be taken and will be there when needed. That is why the psalm is an acrostic: an alphabet poem is built to be memorised.

And the psalm ends where you would not expect. After 175 verses of devotion to God's law, the last is: "I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant; for I do not forget thy commandments" (119:176). The longest love song to scripture in the world ends with the singer admitting he is lost and asking to be found.

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • 1 Nephi 15:23–24 — the iron rod is "the word of God", and those who hold fast *"never

could perish."* Lehi's dream and Psalm 119 are making the same claim about the same thing.

  • The eight terms the psalm cycles through — law, testimonies, precepts, statutes,

commandments, judgments, word, ways — are worth noticing as eight angles on one thing, the way scriptures, the gospel, and the doctrine are for us.

  • A verse-a-day, a letter-a-week practice: eight verses is a stanza. The psalm was built to

be taken in pieces.

Study Questions

  1. What is the difference between studying scripture and hiding it in your heart?
  2. Why might God give a lamp for your feet rather than a light for the whole road?
  3. What does it mean that the psalm ends with "I have gone astray"?

The Text

1 Praise ye the LORD. Praise God in his sanctuary: praise him in the firmament of his power.

2 Praise him for his mighty acts: praise him according to his excellent greatness.

6 Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD.

How the Passage Works

Thirteen commands to praise in six verses, and they answer four questions in order: where (the sanctuary, the firmament), why (His mighty acts, His greatness), how (trumpet, psaltery, harp, timbrel, dance, strings, organs, cymbals), and finally who.

The answer to who is the last line of the Psalter: "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD."

The word for breath is the same word as the breath of life. Hebrew neshāmâ (הַנְּשָׁמָה) — and Genesis 2:7 says God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (nishmat chayyim, נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים). The book ends by calling on everything that carries the breath God gave it to give some of that breath back.

Jerome's Latin heard it and rendered the line "Omnis spiritus laudet Dominum" — spiritus meaning breath and spirit at once, exactly as Greek pneuma does.

Notice the reach of that last line. Not everything that understands. Not everything that has its life in order. Everything that breathes.

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • Doctrine and Covenants 25:12 — *"For my soul delighteth in the song of the heart; yea, the

song of the righteous is a prayer unto me."* A direct treatment of what this whole book has been doing. A psalm sung is a prayer, and Psalm 150 is the instruction to sing it.

  • The instruments matter. Verses 3–5 name eight, including dance. Praise here is physical,

loud, and communal — not only quiet and interior.

  • Alma 5:26 — "if ye have felt to sing the song of redeeming love, I would ask, can ye feel so now?"

Alma treats the song as evidence of a converted heart, which is Psalm 150's assumption.

  2. What does the psalm assume about who is qualified to praise God?
  3. You have breath this morning. What does the last verse of the Psalms ask of you because of it?

This is the last word of substance in the Psalter, and it is not the ordinary word for breathing. Neshāmâ is the breath that makes something alive — and its most famous use is Genesis 2:7, where God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (nishmat chayyim, נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים). Same root.

The derivation is contested — others trace it to Greek neuma, "a nod, a sign," which suits a written mark just as well, and our sources do not settle it. Take it as a suggested connection, not an established one. What is certain is the thing underneath: singing is done on the breath, and in the languages of scripture breath and Spirit are one word.

Psalm 136:1 — "O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever"

כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ

This is the word repeated twenty-six times in Psalm 136 — once in every verse, without exception — and it is one of the hardest words in the Old Testament to put into English.

Ḥesed is not mercy as leniency. It is the loyalty owed inside a covenant — the kindness of someone who has bound themselves to you and will not walk away. Translators have reached for mercy, lovingkindness, steadfast love, covenant faithfulness, and none of them is quite it. The King James chose mercy here; it chose lovingkindness elsewhere for the same word.

Why it matters that it is this word in this psalm. Psalm 136 recites history — creation, the Exodus, the wilderness, the land — and after each event the congregation answers for his ḥesed endureth for ever. The claim is not that God is generally nice. It is that the same covenant loyalty ran through every one of those events, and has not lapsed.

Psalm 119:105 — "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet"

Psalm 119 cycles through eight terms for God's instruction — law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, judgments, word, ways — and dābār is the broadest of them.

It means word, and it also means thing or matter. Hebrew does not separate the saying from the deed as cleanly as English does: what God says and what God does are the same noun. That is why the Ten Commandments are, in Hebrew, "the ten words" (ʿăśeret haddebārîm).

So "thy word is a lamp" is not only about text. It is about what God has said and done — His speech and His acts together — being the thing that shows you where to put your foot.

Psalm 103:8 — "The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and plenteous in mercy"

רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן יְהוָה אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם

Read literally, the Hebrew says "long of nostril."

ʾAph (אַף) means nose — and it means anger. The same word does both jobs, because Hebrew names the feeling by what the body does with it: the nostrils flare, the face heats. To be angry, in Hebrew, is for your nose to burn.

So "slow to anger" is "long of nostril" — a long fuse. It is a picture, not an abstraction: the distance between provocation and the flare.

The phrase is a formula, and it comes from a particular moment. It is what the Lord proclaims about Himself to Moses on Sinai (Exodus 34:6, where the King James renders it "longsuffering"), and it echoes through the Old Testament afterward. When Psalm 103 says it, the psalm is quoting God's own description of Himself — and then, four verses later, saying what it produces: "as far as the east is from the west."

Psalm 150:6 — "Praise ye the LORD"

This is one word in Hebrew, not four, and you already know it: hallelujah.

Halelû is a command — praise — addressed to more than one person. Yāh is the short form of the divine name. So the word is an imperative shouted at a crowd: all of you, praise Yah.

It is not a feeling and it is not a statement. Nobody in the Psalter says "I feel like praising." The psalms give an order, usually to a group, often to people with no obvious reason to comply. Psalms 146–150 each begin and end with it — the Psalter's last word is a command given ten times over.

And English never translated it. Hallelujah came into Greek, then Latin, then English as sound rather than sense — which is why it is one of a handful of Hebrew words an English speaker knows without knowing it is Hebrew. (The others in common use: amen, hosanna, sabbath.)

5. Week 36: Jewish Perspective

Most of this week's material comes from rabbinic sources — the collected discussions of Jewish teachers, written down between roughly the third and twelfth centuries. Two collections appear below:

  • The Talmud — the great record of rabbinic debate. A citation like "Pesachim 118a" means

the tractate (Pesachim, on Passover), the folio, and the side of the page.

  • Midrash — commentary that works by association: a rabbi reads one verse, hears another

answering it, and sets the two side by side.

None of this is Latter-day Saint doctrine, and none of it is offered as a practice to adopt. It is here because these are the readers who lived closest to this text, in its own language, for the longest time — and because this week in particular, they are the ones who can tell us what the Savior was doing at the Last Supper. The Hallel — the psalms sung at Passover, 113 through 118 — is their liturgy. Every citation below links to Sefaria, a free digital library of Jewish texts.

Psalms 113–118 are sung at Passover, and the practice is old enough that the Talmud debates its details rather than establishing them. Tractate Pesachim, which our source sweep surfaced for this week, is the Passover tractate, and it discusses which psalms are said when, and who answers whom.

The relevant structure is simple: Psalms 113–114 before the meal, 115–118 after it. Psalm 118 is the last.

Why this matters to a Latter-day Saint reader. Matthew says that after the Last Supper "when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30). The Gospels do not name the hymn — but the meal was Passover, and this is what is sung at the end of Passover. The Jewish sources are how we know what those words most likely were.

Latter-day Saint Connection

We do not sing the Hallel. But we do something structurally identical: we close the sacrament with a hymn, and the hymn is chosen to carry the meaning of what was just done. When we sing after the emblems, we are doing what He did after the bread and the cup — praising, in a set form, at the end of a covenant meal.

And the content is worth sitting with. If the hymn was Psalm 118, then the last thing He sang before Gethsemane was "The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner" — a line about being rejected and vindicated, sung by the person it was about, hours before the rejection.

Psalm 136 has one feature nobody can miss: twenty-six verses, and every one of them ends "for his mercy endureth for ever."

Jewish tradition calls it the Great Hallel and reads it as antiphonal — sung by two parties, not one. A leader sings the first half of each line; everyone else answers with the refrain. Twenty-six times.

That changes what the psalm is. Read silently it looks repetitive. Sung as designed, the repetition is the congregation's part — the thing they came to say, and the thing they say about every event in the list, from creation through the Exodus to the land.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The nearest thing in our practice is the sacrament prayers, which are the only prayers we say in a fixed form, word for word, every week. We do not usually pray in set words — but for the covenant, we do, and the fixedness is the point: the same words, so that everyone is saying the same thing.

Psalm 136 works the same way. It is not that the congregation had nothing of their own to say. It is that they had one thing to say together, and said it twenty-six times.

The rabbis had a strong instinct that the letters of the Hebrew alphabet are not neutral — that the alphabet is itself a subject worth contemplating, letter by letter. Mystical Jewish writing is often organised that way, one section per letter.

Psalm 119 is the biblical version of that instinct: twenty-two stanzas, one per letter, eight verses each. A poem about loving God's word, made out of every letter available.

The traditional Jewish reading of why: an acrostic is built to be memorised, and this is a psalm about hiding God's word in the heart (119:11). The form serves the content. You do not carry a book with you everywhere; you carry what you have learned by heart, and an alphabet poem is designed to be carried.

Latter-day Saint Connection

"Treasure up in your minds continually the words of life" (D&C 84:85). The promise there is about what will be given "in the very hour" it is needed — which assumes something has been stored beforehand. Psalm 119 is 176 verses about that storage, written in a form built for it, by someone who ends up saying "I have gone astray like a lost sheep" anyway (119:176).

Memorising did not make him perfect. It gave him something to say when he was lost.

Psalm 137 is the exile's psalm: "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept." It is read in Jewish practice on Tisha B'Av, the fast day commemorating the destruction of both temples — the day set aside for national grief.

Its last verses are the hardest in the Psalter, calling for violence against Babylon's children. Jewish commentators have not smoothed them over, and neither should we. What they have generally said is that the psalm records what the exiles felt, in the presence of God, having watched precisely that done to Jerusalem — and that bringing such a thing to God is categorically different from doing it.

Latter-day Saint Connection

We have our own text of this kind. Doctrine and Covenants 121 opens with Joseph Smith in Liberty Jail asking "O God, where art thou?" and continues "how long shall thy hand be stayed?" — a prophet's raw grievance, preserved in scripture rather than edited out. The answer, when it comes, does not rebuke the question.

Psalm 137 belongs to the same category: scripture is willing to hold what people actually felt. The Psalter does not require you to arrive at praise before you are allowed to speak.

The Hebrew name for the Psalms is Tehillim (תְּהִלִּים) — "Praises."

Jewish readers have long noticed the oddity: perhaps a third of the book is lament. If you named the collection from its contents, Praises is not what you would pick. The traditional answer is that a book is named for where it arrives, not for the middle of the journey — and this book arrives at Psalm 150.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The Lord told Emma Smith that "the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me" (D&C 25:12), and that same revelation asked her to make a selection of hymns for the Church. We are, in that sense, a people who were given a hymnbook by revelation — and our hymnbook has the same shape as theirs: it holds Come, Come, Ye Saints and Abide with Me, and it is not called Laments.

The Psalter's title is a claim about where the road goes. So is ours.

6. Week 36: Teaching Applications

Seven settings, and you are not meant to use all of them. Each takes one idea from the week and does something with it. Nothing here needs preparation beyond the study guide — no handouts, no slides, and in most cases nothing to buy. Pick the one that matches who is in front of you.

Sing Psalm 136 the way it was built. One person reads the first half of each verse; everyone else answers "for his mercy endureth for ever." All twenty-six. It takes three minutes and children get it immediately, because their job never changes. Then ask: why would God want the same sentence twenty-six times?

The breath activity, for the last verse of the Psalms. Have everyone take one deep breath and hold it. Then read Psalm 150:6 — "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD." The Hebrew word for breath there is the same word as the breath God breathed into Adam in Genesis 2:7. The point lands without explanation: the breath you are holding is the one He gave you.

A quieter option. Psalm 103:14 — "he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." For a family having a hard week, that is the whole lesson. God is not disappointed that you are tired. He made you, and He remembers what out of.

Open with the hymn nobody names. Read Matthew 26:30 aloud — "when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives" — and ask what the hymn was. Most classes have never wondered. Then walk them to Psalms 116–118: the Hallel, the group of praise psalms sung at Passover, of which 118 closes the meal. Read Psalm 118:22–24 and let them sit with the fact that He sang it that night.

The question that carries the hour: the last thing He sang before Gethsemane was "This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it." What does that tell you about rejoicing?

A second route. Psalm 110:1 — "The LORD said unto my Lord." Put Matthew 22:41–46 beside it and let the class work out why nobody could answer Him. It is one of the few places where you can watch the Savior teach directly from a psalm, and win.

Hallelujah is one word. Teach that "Praise ye the LORD" is a single Hebrew word, hallelujah — a word they already know, and it means exactly that. Then read Psalms 146–150 and have them raise a hand every time they hear it. It opens and closes all five.

Count with Psalm 150. Six verses, and it names instruments — trumpet, harp, timbrel, strings, organs, cymbals. Bring anything that makes noise. Praise in the last psalm is loud, and children are relieved to learn that.

Psalm 127 for the little ones. "Except the LORD build the house." Build a tower together, then talk about who builds families.

A book that does not pretend. Show them Psalm 137 — "How shall we sing the LORD'S song in a strange land?" — and then show them where it sits: a few pages before the book erupts in praise. Scripture kept the psalm about being unable to sing. That is worth saying plainly to teenagers who have been told that faithful people are always cheerful.

Psalm 119 as a challenge. 176 verses, eight per Hebrew letter, the whole alphabet. Ask them why someone would build a poem that way. Then give them the real answer — it is built to be memorised — and ask what they have hidden in their hearts that they did not mean to.

A discussion question that usually goes somewhere. Psalm 119 is the longest chapter in the Bible about loving God's word, and its last verse is "I have gone astray like a lost sheep." What does it mean that the psalm ends there?

Psalm 110 and the Melchizedek Priesthood. Trace it: the psalm names a priesthood "for ever after the order of Melchizedek" (110:4) · the Old Testament mentions Melchizedek nowhere else but Genesis 14 · Hebrews builds its argument on it (5:6; 7:17) · Alma 13 treats him at more length than the whole Old Testament · and D&C 107:2–4 explains why the priesthood carries his name rather than the Lord's. Five texts, one verse at the centre.

How the Psalter — the whole book of Psalms — was assembled to end. Each of the first four books closes with a short doxology. Book Five closes with five whole psalms of it, getting shorter and louder — 10 verses, 20, 14, 9, then 6. Let the class find the pattern themselves by looking at the lengths.

Psalm 103:8 and the flare of the nostril. "Slow to anger" is, in Hebrew, "long of nostril." It is the formula God uses of Himself to Moses at Sinai (Exodus 34:6, where the King James says "longsuffering"). Psalm 103 quotes God's own self-description, then says what it produces four verses later: "as far as the east is from the west."

Psalm 117 is the shortest chapter in the Bible and it is a missionary text. Two verses, addressed to "all ye nations." Paul quotes it precisely to prove the gospel was always meant for everyone (Romans 15:11). The universality is not a New Testament innovation; it is in the hymnbook.

Psalm 119:105 for a teaching model. "A lamp unto my feet" — a clay dish of oil, light enough for the next few steps. Investigators are rarely given the whole road. They are given enough for the next step, which is what a lamp is for, and that is worth saying out loud to someone who wants the entire answer before they will move.

Psalm 150:6 on who qualifies. "Every thing that hath breath." Not everyone who understands, not everyone who has their life sorted. Everything that breathes. A missionary who has that verse in mind will knock on doors differently.

Read Psalms 146–150 out loud, in order, in one sitting. It takes about six minutes. Watch them get shorter and louder. That is the ending of the book, and it was built to be heard.

Take Psalm 119 a stanza a day. Eight verses, one Hebrew letter. Twenty-two days. The psalm was built in exactly those pieces.

If this is a hard season, start at Psalm 102. "I watch, and am as a sparrow alone upon the house top." Then read 103 straight after. The Psalter puts them in that order deliberately, and you are allowed to need the first one for a while.

And a question worth carrying. D&C 25:12 says "the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me." Which hymn, sung honestly this week, would be your prayer?

7. Week 36: Study Questions

Seven questions. They are meant to be sat with rather than answered quickly, and several have no settled answer.

1. Why is a book that is a third lament called Praises?

The Hebrew name for the Psalms is Tehillim — "Praises." Perhaps a third of the book is complaint, grief, or protest, and last week you read some of the rawest of it.

What does it mean to name a collection for where it ends rather than for most of its contents? And what would change if it had been called Laments instead?

2. He sang, on that night. What do you make of that?

The Last Supper was a Passover meal, and Matthew records that "when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30). The hymn at the end of Passover is Psalm 118, which includes "This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it."

What does it do to that verse to know when He sang it? And what does it ask of you the next time you say it on an ordinary morning?

3. Why could no one answer Him?

The Savior asked the Pharisees a question from Psalm 110:1 — if the Messiah is David's son, why does David call him my Lord? — and "no man was able to answer him a word" (Matthew 22:46).

Why was that question unanswerable to them? And what does it show about how He read the Psalms?

4. What is the difference between studying scripture and hiding it in your heart?

Psalm 119:11 says "Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee." The psalm is an acrostic — built, deliberately, to be memorised.

What have you actually stored, as opposed to read? And notice where the psalm ends, after 175 verses of devotion to God's law: "I have gone astray like a lost sheep." What does it mean that memorising did not prevent that?

5. Why is Psalm 137 still in the book?

"By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept." It is the one psalm this week that cannot praise, and its final verses are the hardest in the Psalter. It was not edited out, and it was not put last — it sits a few pages before the book erupts in praise.

What is scripture doing by keeping it? And what does that permit you to bring to God?

6. God remembers that we are dust. Is that comforting or diminishing?

Psalm 103:14 — "For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." The word for frame comes from the verb used of a potter shaping clay, the same verb used when God formed man from the ground.

Does it comfort you that God remembers what He made you out of, or does it sting? And what would change if you remembered it about yourself — or about someone who has disappointed you?

7. Everything that breathes. Including you.

The last line of the Psalter is "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD" (150:6). The word for breath is the same one used when God breathed into Adam.

Notice who is not excluded. Not everything that understands, not everything that has its life in order, not everything that feels like it. Everything that breathes.

So: what is the praise you owe today, on the strength of nothing more than being alive?

A last one, if you want it. Psalms 146 through 150 each open and close with the same word — hallelujah, one word in Hebrew, a command shouted at a crowd. It is an order, not a mood. When has praising God been something you decided rather than something you felt — and what happened?
