First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Three weeks ago you opened Israel's hymnbook. Two weeks ago the floor gave out — a man who had done something no offering covered, another lying awake at three in the morning asking whether God had stopped caring.

This week the book ends. And it does not end the way it has been going.

Read the last five psalms out loud, one after another, and you will hear something happen. Each one opens with "Praise ye the LORD." Each one closes with "Praise ye the LORD." They get louder. More instruments arrive — trumpet, psaltery, harp, timbrel and dance, stringed instruments, organs, loud cymbals, high sounding cymbals — until the last verse puts the instruments down altogether and asks only for breath.

The Hebrew name for the book is Tehillim, Praises. That has been an odd name for a book where roughly a third of the poems are laments. This week is the week it earns the title — not by pretending the laments did not happen, but by refusing to let them have the last word.

That distinction is the whole point of this week, and it is worth holding onto: the Psalter does not end in praise because everything worked out. Psalm 137 is still in the book. The exile still happened. The ending is a decision, not a description.

A Letter to Fellow Students ▶︎

Let me name the difficulty in this week before it catches you quietly.

You are about to read something close to fifty psalms, and a great many of them will tell you to praise God. Not invite. Tell. Praise ye the LORD. Praise him. Praise him. Over and over, for chapters.

And some of us will read that on a week when we do not feel it.

That is an uncomfortable position to be in with an open scripture, and the usual ways out are both bad. You can perform it — say the words, feel nothing, and add a quiet guilt to whatever you were already carrying. Or you can close the book until a better week comes, which is exactly when people stop reading altogether.

The Psalter anticipated you, and it did something about it that is easy to walk straight past.

Look at where the editors put things. Psalm 137 — "How shall we sing the LORD'S song in a strange land?" — is a psalm about being unable to praise. Asked to perform their worship music for the people who had destroyed their temple, the exiles refuse. They hang the harps in the trees.

That psalm sits nine psalms before the ending. It is inside this week's reading. The book that finishes in five straight chapters of praise keeps, on the way there, the song about not being able to sing.

Now look at how the week opens. Five psalms carry the heading "A Prayer" — two of David, one of Moses, one of David in the cave. Psalm 102 is the one that names no person at all: "A Prayer of the afflicted, when he is overwhelmed, and poureth out his complaint before the LORD."

Every other title tells you whose prayer it was. This one tells you what state you have to be in to need it — which means it was left open on purpose, and the space where the name goes is yours.

So the ending is not built on pretending. Every hard thing is still on the page. The praise is what the book decides to do next, and it decides it in full view of everything that happened.

There is one more thing worth having, and it is hiding in the grammar of a single verse. Psalm 146 opens "Praise ye the LORD. Praise the LORD, O my soul." In English those look like the same sentence twice. In Hebrew they are not even the same number. The first is halelû — a command to a crowd. The second is singular, and the psalmist is aiming it at himself.

So the book gives you both, in one breath. There is the praise you are carried into by a congregation on a morning you arrived empty — the choir, the sacrament hymn, everyone around you meaning it while you are still finding your place. And there is the harder, quieter one, where nobody else can stand in for you and you turn to your own soul and tell it what is true.

The Psalter does not ask you to feel finished before you begin. It asks you to stand where the singing is, and then — when you can — to say it to yourself as well.

"His Mercy Endureth For Ever" — What He Sang Before He Went Out ▶︎

Here is a sentence you have read many times and possibly never stopped on.

"And when they had sung an hymn, they went out into the mount of Olives."

— Matthew 26:30

That is the Last Supper. The next thing that happens is Gethsemane.

Find the hymn. Matthew does not name it, but the meal does. The Passover seder closes with the second half of the Hallel — Psalms 115 through 118 — and that is what was sung at the table of the Last Supper. Psalm 118 is in your reading this week.

So read it knowing whose voice you are hearing:

"The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner." (118:22)

"This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it." (118:24)

"O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever." (118:29)

He sang "his mercy endureth for ever" hours before Gethsemane. He sang "this is the day which the LORD hath made" on the worst night in the history of the world. And He sang the line about the rejected stone knowing exactly who the builders were and what they were about to do.

Count what that costs. Not the singing of a happy song by a man who did not know. The singing of a true song by the one man who knew everything.

That is the week's first answer to why the Psalter ends in praise. Praise is not a verdict on your circumstances. It is a statement about God that stays true when the circumstances do not.

The Book Does Not Pretend ▶︎

Psalm 137 is in this week's stretch, and it is the hardest passage in the Psalter.

It begins in a way anyone can love — "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion" — and it ends somewhere most readers wish it did not, with a line about infants and stones that has troubled the Church and the synagogue for two thousand years.

Do not skip it, and do not soften it. Here is what it is doing.

The exiles have been asked by their captors to perform. "They that carried us away captive required of us a song… saying, Sing us one of the songs of Zion." Their own worship, demanded as entertainment by the people who burned the temple. And they say the thing that is true: "How shall we sing the LORD'S song in a strange land?" (137:4)

Notice where the editors put it. Psalm 137 sits in Book Five, on the road to the Hallelujah psalms — not edited out, not moved to the back, not tucked behind a warning label. The book that ends in praise keeps the psalm about being unable to sing.

That is not a flaw in the collection. It is the collection telling you something about itself: this book will not ask you to pretend. The praise at the end is not purchased by deleting the grief in the middle. If your faith has ever had to survive a season where you could not sing, Psalm 137 is scripture's acknowledgement that the season is real — and its position in the book is scripture's promise that it is not the end of the road.

The Turn Happens in the Middle of a Sentence ▶︎

Before the book turns, one psalm turns first — and it is the psalm your reading opens on.

Psalm 102 begins as badly as the Psalter ever begins. "Hear my prayer, O LORD, and let my cry come unto thee" (102:1). What follows is eleven verses of a man in genuine trouble: his days are consumed like smoke, his bones burned as a hearth, his heart smitten and withered like grass. He is not being poetic about a hard week. The superscription calls it "A Prayer of the afflicted, when he is overwhelmed."

Then verse twelve happens.

"But thou, O LORD, shalt endure for ever; and thy remembrance unto all generations." (102:12)

Find the hinge, because it is one word. But. Nothing in his circumstances changed between verse eleven and verse twelve. He did not get better. The psalm does not report a rescue. What changed is what he is looking at — he stops describing himself and starts describing God, and the whole poem re-orients around the second subject.

That is the Psalter's entire argument compressed into a single line break. The book does not move from lament to praise because the lamenting stopped working. It moves because at some point the singer turns his head.

And this psalm has one more thing in it that a Christian reader should not miss. Its closing verses describe the Lord laying the foundation of the earth, and the heavens as the work of His hands — and the author of Hebrews quotes those verses and addresses them to the Son (Hebrews 1:10–12). A psalm written by an afflicted man about the God who outlasts him is applied, in the New Testament, to Jesus Christ.

So the one who endures for ever, in the psalm that turns on that fact, is Him.

"So Great Is His Mercy" — the Psalm That Measures Things ▶︎

Psalm 103 does something unusual. It reaches for distances.

"For as the heaven is high above the earth, so great is his mercy toward them that fear him." (103:11)

"As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us." (103:12)

Look at the second one closely, because the choice of axis is deliberate. North and south have limits — travel far enough north and you begin going south. East and west do not meet. You can travel east forever and never once begin travelling west. The psalmist chose the one direction on the compass that does not turn back on itself.

And then the psalm says why, and it is not what a reader braced for a lecture expects:

"Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." (103:13–14)

He knoweth our frame. Not He overlooks our frame, and not He is disappointed by our frame. He knows what we are made of, and that knowledge is the reason for the mercy rather than an obstacle to it.

And the word underneath "pitieth" is worth knowing, because English makes it sound like sympathy from a distance.

The Hebrew is rāḥam (רָחַם) — to love deeply, to have compassion. It is built on the same three letters as reḥem (רֶחֶם), the word for a womb. Hebrew does that: it grows a family of words from one root, and the family carries the root's sense into every branch.

So when Psalm 103 says the Lord pitieth those who fear Him, the word reaches for the tenderness a mother has for the child she carried — not a superior's tolerance of a lesser, but the fierce and physical attachment of someone whose own body kept you alive. A dark, protective, nourishing place. That is the register.

Hold that beside verse 14. He knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust. The one who knows exactly what you are made of feels toward you what a mother feels. Those two verses are the same sentence said twice, and English hides the second half of it.

Try this in your own study this week. Take something you have asked forgiveness for more than once — the thing you are tired of bringing back. Read 103:12 against it and take the geography seriously. As far as the east is from the west. Not filed. Not suspended. Removed, along an axis that does not return.

Then read verse 13 again knowing what the word is doing, and notice that the psalm gives you the reason before you can ask for it. The mercy is not a ruling handed down. It is a relationship you were already inside.

Twenty-Two Letters, and Why Anyone Would Do That ▶︎

Psalm 119 is the longest chapter in the Bible, and most readers meet it as a wall.

Here is the shape underneath. Twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, eight verses each. Inside a stanza, every one of the eight verses begins with that same letter. And your King James Bible prints the letter names — ALEPH, BETH, GIMEL, all the way to TAU — right there in the margin. Look above verse 105 and you will find NUN.

Count them. Twenty-two headings, and the whole poem is about one subject: God's word, named under eight different terms in nearly every verse.

So why write that way? Two answers, and they work together. It is far easier to memorise — and this is a psalm about keeping God's word in the heart (119:11). And it is a claim about completeness: every letter available, spent on one subject. Everything language can say, said about the word of God.

There is a third answer worth knowing about, and it is older than most people assume. The rabbis treat the alphabet itself as worth contemplating letter by letter, and that instinct is not a late mystical development — the Talmud, the collected rabbinic discussions compiled by about the sixth century, records it as something already settled. Alef beit is read as elaf binah — learn wisdom; gimmel dalet as gemol dalim — give to the poor, and the leg of the gimmel reaches toward the dalet because it is the way of one who gives to pursue the poor. You can read the passage yourself: Shabbat 104a.

The Talmud is not where this begins. It is a snapshot — a sixth-century record of what was already the tradition of its time, being recited by schoolchildren rather than argued among scholars, which is what a practice looks like once it is old enough to be taught to children. What it photographs is far older than the photograph, and plausibly reaches back to the same instinct Psalm 119 was built on. Jewish tradition holds two things at once here: the letters are a memory device, and they are worth reading and studying. This guide stays with the first. The second is a longer, but fascinating road. Unfortunately we only have time to scratch the surface in this lesson, but this is a topic that has been very influential in my own study and research, and one that has not only benefited me tremendously, but one that truly has no end.

If you want a place to start, More Than Modern: The Ancient Roots of Hebrew Letter Traditions traces where the practice actually begins — in the primary sources, rather than in the medieval movement it usually gets credited to.

What the form does to a reader is the point. A poem that spends the entire alphabet on one subject is making an argument by its shape: there is no part of language — any language — that does not belong to this. Almost every alphabet on the planet shares a history with these same phonetic roots, which is worth stopping on for a moment: the letters you are reading this sentence in are cousins of the ones Psalm 119 is built from.

The word is in our records, our songs, our traditions, and in our thoughts. You cannot exhaust it, and you cannot get away from it. There is no end of the letters, because they permeate every subject.

"The LORD Said Unto My Lord" — the Question Nobody Could Answer ▶︎

Psalm 110 is short, strange, and quoted in the New Testament more often than any other passage in the Old.

Its first line is confusing in English on purpose, and the confusion is the point.

"The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool." (110:1)

Look at the two words. The first LORD, in small capitals, is the divine name. The second Lord is a different word — a title of respect for a superior. So David, the king, is reporting a conversation between God and someone David calls his own master.

And there was nobody above David. He was the king. That is what makes the sentence strange, and it sat in Israel's hymnbook, strange, for a thousand years.

Then the Savior asked about it. Near the end of His life, with the Pharisees questioning Him, He turned the questioning around and asked them whose son the Christ is. David's, they said — the standard, correct answer. And He asked them why, then, David calls him my Lord:

"If David then call him Lord, how is he his son?" (Matthew 22:45)

"And no man was able to answer him a word, neither durst any man from that day forth ask him any more questions." (Matthew 22:46)

Sit with what He was doing. He was not scoring a point. He was showing them a verse they had sung their whole lives and asking them to notice that it does not fit the answer they had settled on. The Messiah is not only David's descendant. He is David's Lord — and He was that before David was born.

And notice who is speaking in Matthew 22. That is His own voice, quoting this psalm. It is one of the few places in this week's reading where the words in the New Testament are His rather than Peter's or Paul's or a crowd's — and the study guide is careful about that distinction throughout, because it matters who said a thing.

Verse 4 carries the other half, and it names a man from a single strange chapter of Genesis:

"Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." (110:4)

Outside Genesis 14, this is the only place the Old Testament mentions Melchizedek at all. Hebrews builds its whole argument about Christ's priesthood on it — a priesthood that does not pass by descent, held by one who does not die.

And for us the name is not an inference from these verses. It was revealed. "Before his day it was called the Holy Priesthood, after the Order of the Son of God. But out of respect or reverence to the name of the Supreme Being, to avoid the too frequent repetition of his name, they, the church, in ancient days, called that priesthood after Melchizedek, or the Melchizedek Priesthood" (D&C 107:3–4).

So the priesthood's own name is the Holy Priesthood after the Order of the Son of God, and Melchizedek is what reverence chose to say instead — the same instinct that led Israel to say Adonai rather than pronounce the divine name.

And the substitute name is itself a title. Malki-Tsedeq (מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק) is two words joined — melekh (מֶלֶךְ), king, and tsedeq (צֶדֶק), righteousness. The man Abraham met at Salem was called King of Righteousness, and the New Testament says so outright rather than leaving it to be noticed: "first being by interpretation King of righteousness" (Hebrews 7:2).

So the name points past the man. A priesthood called after Melchizedek is called after the King of Righteousness — which is a description of Christ before it is the name of anyone else, and the reason the reverent substitution works at all. He is the King of Righteousness, and He is "KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS" (Revelation 19:16).

Which means the two halves of Psalm 110 are one thing. A Lord who was David's before He was David's son, and a priesthood named for Him by a people who did not dare say His name too often.

"I Was Brought Low, and He Helped Me" ▶︎

Psalm 116 is a small psalm and it does something the big ones do not. It tells you a story in the first person, and it is a story about nearly dying.

"I love the LORD, because he hath heard my voice and my supplications." (116:1)

"The sorrows of death compassed me, and the pains of hell gat hold upon me: I found trouble and sorrow." (116:3)

"I was brought low, and he helped me." (116:6)

Read that sixth verse aloud. Six words in the Hebrew and there is no decoration on any of them. Not He delivered me from mine enemies. Not a metaphor about wings or fortresses. I was brought low, and he helped me.

Then the psalm asks the question that a rescued person actually asks:

"What shall I render unto the LORD for all his benefits toward me?" (116:12)

And its answer is not what you would guess. Not I will be better. Not a list of resolutions. "I will take the cup of salvation, and call upon the name of the LORD" (116:13) — he takes something else, and keeps praying.

This psalm is in the Hallel. It was sung at the Lord's table, the night of the Last Supper.

"Except the LORD Build the House" — a Song for Walking ▶︎

Psalms 120 through 134 carry a heading you have probably read past: A Song of degrees — Shir HaMa'alot, the Songs of Ascents. These are temple songs before they are anything else. The Mishnah describes fifteen steps rising from the Court of the Women to the Court of Israel, "corresponding to the fifteen [songs of] ascents mentioned in the Book of Psalms, and upon which the Levites used to sing" (Middot 2:5) — and the Levites standing on those same stairs with lyres, harps and cymbals (Sukkah 5:4). One psalm to a step, sung by the Levites, as liturgy. Two of them are in your reading.

Psalm 127 opens with a line most Latter-day Saints can finish from memory:

"Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it." (127:1)

Notice what it does not say. It does not say do not build. The builders in that verse are building. The labour is real and it continues. What the psalm denies is that the labour is the thing holding the house up.

And the psalm goes straight from houses to children — "Lo, children are an heritage of the LORD" (127:3) — which is not a change of subject. The house being built is a family. Psalm 128 finishes the thought with a picture of one: a table, a wife, children around it "like olive plants."

Here is why it matters where these were sung. They belonged to the liturgy — the Levites singing them on the temple stairs, one psalm to a step — and the families climbing the road to the feast were coming up to meet the very songs they had been singing on the way. That is what turned a road full of separate households into one congregation: they were all walking toward the same song, and they knew it before they arrived. And the song they were walking toward said out loud that the household each of them had left behind for a week was not ultimately theirs to hold together. That is a hard thing to sing while you are away from it, and they sang it every year.

If you are carrying a household this week — a home, a calling, a business, a person you are worried about — Psalm 127 is not telling you to stop building. It is telling you whose house it is, and that you may walk to the feast without it falling down.

One More Echo — the Word That Ends the Book ▶︎

The Psalter's last line is six words in the Hebrew, and one of them has been waiting since Genesis.

"Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD." (150:6)

"Breath" here is neshāmâ (נְשָׁמָה). It is not the common word for wind or spirit. It is the specific word used in Genesis 2:7, when God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul."

So the last line of Israel's prayer book reaches back to the first thing God gave a human being. The qualification for praising Him is the thing He handed you at the beginning. Not competence. Not worthiness. Not a good week.

Breath.

Which is why the verse is so wide. Every thing that hath breath — the man who wrote Psalm 51 after the worst thing he ever did; the exiles who could not sing in a strange land; the psalmist who was brought low; a Primary child who cannot read yet; you, on a Sunday when you do not feel like it.

And read the phrase more carefully, because it is wider than one lifetime. Hath breath is not only the breath you are drawing this morning. It is breath you have ever drawn. The qualification is not that you are alive today — it is that you were given breath at all, and neshāmâ is not something God takes back at a graveside. So the Psalter's last line reaches past everyone now living to everyone who has ever lived, and the praise it calls for is not closed to them.

That distinction is the plan of salvation in a single Hebrew word, and it is what temple work is for — making it possible for those who have breathed to answer a verse that was always addressed to them too.

The Invitation ▶︎

The Psalms have been teaching you to talk to God for three weeks, and they finish by teaching you how to end.

Not with a resolution. With praise — offered on purpose, in a book that never once pretended things were fine.

So this week, do three things, and none of them takes long.

Read Psalm 150 out loud. All six verses, standing up if you can. It takes twenty seconds and it is meant to be heard rather than scanned.

Then read Psalm 118:22–29 slowly, knowing He sang it that night. "His mercy endureth for ever," in His own voice, hours before the garden. Let the timing land.

And take the last verse personally, because it was written to include you. "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD." The Hebrew for breath there is neshāmâ — the same word God breathed into Adam, so that the Psalter's final line reaches back to the moment a human being first became a living soul. The entry requirement for the book's closing sentence is breath — and the verse counts every breath ever drawn, not only this morning's. Not that you are steady, or finished, or feeling it. You met it before you were old enough to know you had, and so did every name you have ever carried to the temple.

You have the breath. That is the whole qualification, and the last verse of the Psalter says so.
