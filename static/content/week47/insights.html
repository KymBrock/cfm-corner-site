First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week Daniel and his friends stayed true to the Lord in a foreign court, far from home. This week goes back in time and closer to home: to the Northern Kingdom of Israel, still living in its own land, still prosperous, and slowly forgetting the God who gave it everything. Within a generation it would be gone, carried away by Assyria (2 Kings 17:6).

Into that kingdom the Lord sent Hosea. Most of his book is warning, but underneath the warnings runs a love letter. The Bible Dictionary sums up the whole book in one line: "Hosea's fundamental idea is the love of God for His people."

The Overview walks every chapter of Hosea and Joel in order, so you can see the whole of both books.

The Son of Beeri and the Son of Pethuel ▶︎

Hosea. Almost everything we know about Hosea comes from his own book. Its first verse names his father, Beeri, and the kings in whose days he preached (Hosea 1:1); beyond that, we know only the wife and three children of chapters 1–3. His name means salvation. The Bible Dictionary calls him "the only prophet of the northern kingdom who has left written prophecies." He began preaching late in the reign of Jeroboam II, in the middle of the 700s BC, and he seems to have died before the kingdom's last, chaotic years. Isaiah, Amos and Micah preached in the same generation. So when Hosea speaks of Israel's unfaithfulness, he is not looking in from outside. He is speaking to his own people, in his own land, about a covenant he loved.

Joel. We know even less about Joel. His book gives only his father's name, Pethuel (Joel 1:1). He spoke to Judah, the southern kingdom, and his date is so uncertain that the Bible Dictionary allows anything from the 800s BC to after the return from Babylon. What is certain is the occasion: drought and locusts. Perhaps that suits him. Joel's book is not about Joel.

Why their books are in our Bible. Hosea and Joel open the last twelve books of the Old Testament, the "minor prophets," minor only in length. In Jewish tradition those twelve were copied together onto a single scroll and counted as one book, "the Twelve." The Talmud, the great collection of rabbinic teaching compiled after the time of Christ, gives a homely reason for keeping Hosea there: "Were it written separately, since it is small it would be lost" (Bava Batra 14b, William Davidson Edition on Sefaria, CC BY-NC). Small as they were, these books were treasured as the word of the Lord, and later scripture shows why. The Savior quoted Hosea in His own ministry (Matthew 9:13; 12:7; Luke 23:30). And Joel was the first scripture Peter quoted at Pentecost (Acts 2:16–21). Historical & Cultural Context, sections 1 and 5, gathers the details of each prophet's time.

"Thy Maker Is Thine Husband" ▶︎

This week the Lord speaks as a husband and Israel as His bride, sometimes tenderly and sometimes in pain. It helps to know before you start that Hosea did not invent this picture, and he was not the last to use it.

Why a marriage? A covenant is a promise that binds two parties to each other, and the closest covenant most people ever make is marriage. So the prophets reached for marriage to describe the covenant between the Lord and His people. The Old Testament Student Manual explains: "Because most people had personal knowledge of marriage, they understood the Lord better when the prophets used marriage terms to describe symbolically the covenants God made with them and they with Him." In that picture, "God is the husband and Israel, the covenant people, is the bride." The manual traces the marriage to the covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17), renewed at Sinai (Exodus 19:4–8).

The picture works both ways. Keeping the covenant is faithful love; turning to other gods is unfaithfulness. That is why Hosea's language can feel so strong. It is the language of a betrayed marriage, because that is how the Lord describes idolatry (Hosea 1:2).

The same picture across scripture. Once you know it, you will find it everywhere:

Who the Bridegroom is. The Old Testament speaks of the Lord as husband to a whole people. In the New Testament, Jesus calls Himself the bridegroom (Matthew 9:15), and modern revelation pictures His Second Coming as a wedding still ahead, asking His people to be "ready at the coming of the Bridegroom" (Doctrine and Covenants 33:17). So when Hosea's chapters turn hard, remember what kind of story you are reading. It is the story of a Husband who keeps His side of the covenant when His bride does not, and who goes looking for her. The Love That Goes Looking ▶︎

The book's most important scene is small: the Lord sends Hosea after the wife who has left him, "Go yet, love a woman" (Hosea 3:1).

Notice the order. He does not wait for her to clean up her life and come knocking. He goes after her. That is how the Lord describes Himself all through the book: "I will allure her… and speak comfortably unto her" (Hosea 2:14). Key Passages, passage 1, reads the whole story.

If you have ever felt that you wandered too far to be wanted, Hosea begins with a God who comes looking.

"Come, and Let Us Return" ▶︎

Halfway through the book the people say the right words: "Come, and let us return unto the LORD" (Hosea 6:1). But the Lord sighs. Their goodness was "as the early dew it goeth away" (6:4). They meant it at breakfast. By noon it was gone.

Most of us know that feeling. We resolve, and the resolve fades.

So the last chapter does something tender. It gives Israel the very words to say: "Take with you words, and turn to the LORD" (14:2). And then the Lord promises to be what Israel could not be: "I will be as the dew unto Israel" (14:5). The dew that kept evaporating from their goodness will now come from Him, every morning. The Jewish teachers noticed the same thing (Jewish Perspective, section 3).

So it is worth asking this week: where has my good resolve burned off by noon, and what would it mean to let the Lord be the dew?

"I Will Love Them Freely" ▶︎

The title of this week comes from the Lord's answer to that prayer: "I will love them freely" (Hosea 14:4).

The Hebrew word behind freely, nᵊḏāḇâ (נְדָבָה), is the word for a freewill offering (Word Studies, study 4). The Lord does not love His returning people because they have finally earned it, or as a payment owed. He loves them the way a worshipper brings a gift he did not have to bring: because He wants to.

That changes the way we come back. We do not have to arrive with a case to make. We bring words, a willing heart, and our need for healing. He brings the love.

What the Locust Ate ▶︎

Joel's people had watched four waves of locusts strip their fields, one after another, until nothing was left (Joel 1:4). Then the Lord made a promise that reaches further than crops: "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten" (2:25).

Not the harvest. The years.

Joel spoke that promise to a whole people about real fields, and the verses around it describe the promise being kept: "the floors shall be full of wheat" (2:24), and "ye shall eat in plenty, and be satisfied" (2:26). Many readers have also found in it a hope for their own lost years. Joel does not say that every loss is undone in this life, or that what happened did not happen. What he shows is the kind of God who restores, and the way back he gives in one line: "rend your heart, and not your garments" (2:13).

Poured Out ▶︎

Joel's last great promise is the one Moroni repeated to the young Joseph Smith in 1823 (Joseph Smith—History 1:41): "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh" (2:28).

Notice the word pour. Not a drip, not a trickle. And notice who receives it: sons and daughters, old and young, servants and handmaids. No one is left outside. The manual quotes Elder David A. Bednar's reminder that the Spirit is usually "still and small and incremental over time," so the outpouring may come quietly, in ordinary days. Key Passages, passage 6, reads the prophecy and its fulfillments.

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

The book of Hosea opens with a broken marriage and ends with a promise:

"I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him." — Hosea 14:4

Whatever you have turned to, and however long you have been gone, this is what the Lord says when you turn back. He heals. He loves. And He does it freely.
