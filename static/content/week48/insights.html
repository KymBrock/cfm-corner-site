First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week, Hosea pleaded with the Northern Kingdom of Israel to come home to the Lord, the way a husband pleads with a wife who has left. This week we stay in the same kingdom, in the same years. Open Hosea 1:1 and Amos 1:1 side by side: both name Uzziah, king of Judah, and "Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel". Hosea and Amos were speaking to the same people at the same time.

What changes this week is the view. Hosea spoke to one nation that had wandered. This week's three short books look outward as well. Amos speaks to Israel at the height of its wealth and comfort, a few decades before Assyria conquered it. Obadiah, probably writing later, after Jerusalem itself had fallen, speaks to Edom, Israel's brother nation. And Jonah is sent to Nineveh, a great city of the empire Israel feared most.

The Overview walks through all fourteen chapters in order and introduces everyone in them. This page looks at what the three books say together, and what they might say to us.

Three Books, One Question: Who Deserves the Lord's Mercy? ▶︎

This is the thing most worth knowing before you start, because it ties the week together. Each book speaks to a different people, and each people thought it already knew the answer.

  • Israel, the Lord's own people. They kept up their sacrifices and feasts while cheating the poor, and they were sure the Lord was on their side. They looked forward to "the day of the LORD," expecting Him to come and defend them. Amos told them that day would be "darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18). Being the Lord's people did not excuse them. It made them more responsible: "You only have I known… therefore I will punish you" (Amos 3:2).
  • Edom, Israel's brother nation. The Edomites were descended from Esau, Jacob's twin. When Jerusalem fell, they stood by and were glad. They thought their homes high in the mountains, "in the clefts of the rock" (Obadiah 1:3), kept them out of reach. Obadiah told them, "as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee" (1:15).
  • Nineveh, Israel's enemy. Nineveh was a great city of Assyria. Jonah was sure it deserved no mercy. When the Lord sent him to warn it, he sailed the other way. When he finally preached, the city repented and the Lord spared it, and Jonah "was very angry" (Jonah 4:1).

In each book, the Lord's answer is not what people expected. His own people were not safe just because they were His. Edom's mountains could not hide it. And the enemy city, when it repented, was forgiven.

The question is still a live one. It is worth asking whether there is anyone we have quietly decided the Lord should not forgive.

Waiting for the Holy Day to End ▶︎

Amos's hardest words were not for unbelievers. They were for people who never missed a feast.

Read Amos 8:5 slowly, because it is easy to miss what it shows. The merchants are keeping the holy days. They are not working on the new moon, and they are not selling on the Sabbath. But they are waiting it out: "When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the sabbath, that we may set forth wheat, making the ephah small, and the shekel great". Their bodies were in the holy day. Their minds were already back at the scales, planning how to give the poor less grain for more silver.

Amos sees the same thing at the dinner table. The comfortable lie on ivory beds and drink "wine in bowls," and the charge against them comes at the very end of the sentence: "but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" (Amos 6:6). They had not done anything dramatic. They simply did not feel the trouble of the people around them.

That is why the Lord's answer is not more worship but a different kind of life: "let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" (Amos 5:24). A stream does not stop at the end of the service. It runs all week, past the gate where the poor wait for justice and the market where the grain is weighed.

If you have ever sat through something sacred while your thoughts were already on what came next, Amos is not writing you off. He is inviting you to let what you worship reach the rest of your week.

"Ye Shall Live" ▶︎

This week's title is a promise from Amos: "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (Amos 5:4). In these books, to live means more than to stay alive. It is the life that comes from turning to the Lord, and He offers it to more people than His own prophets expected.

Notice where the promise is spoken. Amos has just sung a funeral song over Israel: "The virgin of Israel is fallen; she shall no more rise" (5:2). And two verses later the Lord says, live. In that one chapter the word comes three times, and each time it follows the word seek: seek me, seek the LORD, seek good (5:4, 6, 14). Seek, and live. The Lord does not wait until the mourning is over to offer life. He offers it in the middle of the mourning.

In Jonah, life is the thing the prophet nearly loses and then is given back: "yet hast thou brought up my life from corruption, O LORD my God" (2:6). Corruption there means the pit of the grave he was sinking into, not moral corruption. Then, after Nineveh repents and is spared, the same prophet says twice, "it is better for me to die than to live" (4:3, 8). The Lord had given him life, and he could not bear to see the Lord give it to Nineveh too.

The Prophet Who Prayed and the Prophet Who Ran ▶︎

Amos and Jonah were both the Lord's prophets, but they felt very differently about the people they were sent to.

Amos's message to Israel was mostly warning. Even so, when the Lord showed him a vision of locusts destroying the land, Amos pleaded for the people: "O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small" (Amos 7:2). The Lord answered, "It shall not be" (7:3), and He answered the same way when Amos pleaded again after a vision of fire (7:5–6). Amos prayed for the very people who would soon reject him. Not long after, Israel's priest at Beth-el ordered him to go back to Judah (7:12).

Jonah's message to Nineveh was short, and it worked: the whole city repented. But Jonah was angry, and he told the Lord why he had run away in the first place: "I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness" (Jonah 4:2). Jonah ran because he knew the Lord would forgive Nineveh, and he did not want Him to.

Jonah's feelings make more sense once you know what Assyria was. Its kings boasted on their own palace walls of how they punished the peoples who resisted them, and a few decades after Jonah the empire carried the Northern Kingdom of Israel away. CFM Corner's Assyria field guide describes the empire's methods in The War Machine, and its chapter Assyria & the Bible has a section on Jonah's mission.

Both men knew the Lord was merciful. The difference is that Amos wanted that mercy for others and Jonah did not. The Lord was patient with both: He answered Amos's prayers, and He kept teaching Jonah to the very end of the book. If you have ever found it easier to want mercy for yourself than for someone who hurt you, Jonah's story is written for exactly that moment, and the Lord does not give up on him.

A Brother's Day ▶︎

Obadiah's short book is about one day: the day Jerusalem fell. Obadiah says to Edom, "thou stoodest on the other side" (1:11), and Edom was glad. In four verses Obadiah uses the word day ten times (Obadiah 1:11–14) as he lists what Edom did that day. It stood by, it rejoiced, it carried off Jerusalem's goods, and it stood at the crossroads to catch the people of Judah who tried to escape.

What makes it bitter is the word Obadiah keeps returning to: "thy brother Jacob" (1:10), "the day of thy brother" (1:12). Jacob and Esau had quarrelled, but years later Esau ran to meet his brother and the two of them wept together (Genesis 33:4). Their descendants threw that peace away on the worst day a brother could have.

But the book does not end on that day. Its last verse looks forward to "saviours" on mount Zion (1:21). Latter-day prophets have taught that this describes temple work: going to the Lord's house to do for the dead what they cannot do for themselves (Key Passages, passage 4). Edom refused to help its brother on his worst day. The Lord invites His people to do the opposite, even for family they have never met.

"Should Not I Spare Nineveh?" ▶︎

The book of Jonah ends in an unusual way. It never tells us whether Jonah changed his mind. It ends with the Lord asking Jonah a question, "should not I spare Nineveh, that great city" (Jonah 4:11), and we never hear Jonah's answer. The question is left for the reader.

Read the whole verse to its last words. The Lord counts the people of the city, "more than sixscore thousand persons," and then adds, "and also much cattle". He has been keeping track of the animals too. Jonah had grieved over a single plant he had not even grown. The Lord's concern reaches every person in an enemy city and every animal in its fields.

Jewish congregations still read the book of Jonah aloud on the Day of Atonement, the day they ask the Lord to forgive them (Jewish Perspective, section 4). It is a fitting day to hear about a God who forgave Nineveh.

The Savior also pointed to Nineveh: "The men of Nineveh… repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here" (Matthew 12:41). Jonah spent three days inside the fish. Jesus spent three days in the tomb and then rose again. Through His death and Resurrection, the mercy Jonah did not want to share is offered to everyone, including Jonah.

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

Amos told Israel to stop relying on its famous holy places, Beth-el, Gilgal and Beer-sheba (Amos 5:5), and to seek the Lord Himself:

"Seek ye me, and ye shall live" — Amos 5:4

That promise still stands. And those who seek the Lord find that He was already looking for them: "For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).
