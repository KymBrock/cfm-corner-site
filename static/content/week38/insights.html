First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week ended with a man who had tried everything — pleasure, building, learning, wealth — and came back with "fear God, and keep his commandments." Proverbs and Ecclesiastes had spent the whole week on one question: how should a person live?

This week the question changes. Isaiah is not mainly asking how to live. He is asking who God is, and what it does to you to see Him.

That is a different kind of reading, and it is worth knowing before you start. Wisdom literature leans on what you can test against your own experience — try it and see whether it holds. Isaiah leans the other way. He puts you in a courtroom in chapter 1 and a throne room in chapter 6, and the weight falls less on whether the counsel works than on who is speaking. Both books ask both questions; the emphasis is what shifts.

Before You Start ▶︎

Isaiah has a reputation for being hard, and the reputation is earned. The King James is difficult here in a way it is not in Genesis. The chapters jump. A verse about a virgin sits next to a verse about butter and honey, and neither seems to know the other is there.

Here is something to do before you open the first chapter. Read these four lines and see how many of them you have met before: "They shall beat their swords into plowshares." "Though your sins be as scarlet." "Unto us a child is born." "Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God." Every one of them is in this week's twelve chapters. Most of us have met these lines on their own — on a plaque, in a hymn, at Christmas — and a line lifted out of Isaiah usually loses the situation it was answering. "Swords into plowshares" sits in a chapter that ends with people throwing their idols to the bats; "unto us a child is born" has a war on one side of it. So read the verses either side of each one, and expect the surroundings to be rougher than the quotation led you to believe. That roughness is the point of them.

If the size of the book is what daunts you, start with its shape. Isaiah runs to sixty-six chapters; this week covers the first twelve. BibleProject's two-part animated overview — Isaiah 1–39 and Isaiah 40–66 — lays that shape out in about sixteen minutes between them, and is an unusually good place to begin. Chapters 1–39 move back and forth between judgment and hope; 40–66 develop the restoration that follows.

One connection from that overview is worth carrying into this week's reading. At the end of Isaiah 6, after the vision and the coal, the prophet is told the land will be emptied like a tree cut down — and that a "holy seed" remains in what is left standing (6:13). Five chapters later that stump puts out a shoot: "there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse" (11:1), the coming King. The felled tree and the seed left in it are one image, carried across the week. (That reading of the tree and the seed is BibleProject's synthesis of the book's structure. It is offered as an accessible starting point — not a substitute for the text, and not an official Church interpretation.)

Nephi said the difficult part out loud, and then said what closes the gap. He grants plainly that "Isaiah spake many things which were hard for many of my people to understand; for they know not concerning the manner of prophesying among the Jews" (2 Nephi 25:1). He had an advantage his people did not — "I came out from Jerusalem, and mine eyes hath beheld the things of the Jews" (2 Nephi 25:5). But he does not make that a qualification a reader has to earn first. What he offers instead is this: the words of Isaiah "are plain unto all those that are filled with the spirit of prophecy" (2 Nephi 25:4). And the simplest of the reasons he gives for copying so much Isaiah at all is that Isaiah had seen what he had seen — "for he verily saw my Redeemer, even as I have seen him" (2 Nephi 11:2). (His other stated reasons, and Jacob's, are set out in Historical & Cultural Context, section 6.)

And the tools for this were built over the last several weeks. Wisdom literature trained the habit of reading an image that means more than it says. The Psalms trained the ear for the paired line, where the second half answers the first. Isaiah is made of both, and the week's Historical & Cultural Context supplies the third thing these chapters ask for — the world they were spoken into. None of that is a prerequisite. It just means a reader coming from those weeks is not starting cold.

The Overview walks all twelve chapters in order, and the Historical & Cultural Context explains the empire pressing on Isaiah's world while he preached. What follows is not a summary of either. It is a handful of things in these chapters worth slowing down for.

He Was Cleansed Before He Volunteered ▶︎

This is the detail in Isaiah 6 that is easiest to read straight past.

Isaiah sees the Lord. His first words are not "send me." They are "Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips" (6:5). Then a seraph takes a coal from the altar and touches his mouth, and only after that does the Lord ask "whom shall I send?" — and only then does Isaiah say "Here am I; send me" (6:8).

The order is the point. He is not cleansed because he volunteered. He volunteers because he has been cleansed.

The instinct runs the other way. Earn the standing first — get the habit fixed, the doubt settled, the record respectable — and then offer to serve. Isaiah's account puts it the other way round: the offer follows something done to him that he could not do for himself.

(The verb is kāp̄ar (כָּפַר) — the atonement word, and it is passive here. Worked through in Key Passages, passage 4.)*

Be careful what that does and does not mean. It is not a reason to stop repenting — the confession is the first thing Isaiah does, and repentance is how anyone comes to the coal rather than something the coal replaces. What it removes is the waiting. The order in this chapter is confession, then a cleansing he could not perform on himself, then the call — so the thing to stop is the attempt to make yourself adequate under your own power before you offer to serve. Repent, yes. Rely on Christ to do the cleansing, rather than on your own record to make it unnecessary — and do not wait to be asked until you feel finished.

The Question That Is Actually Hard ▶︎

Isaiah 5 is a love song about a vineyard, and it is a searching one. The owner does everything right and gets wild grapes. Then the song turns and asks the listeners to judge the case — and only afterwards tells them whose vineyard it is.

The hard question is not whether we deserve the verdict. It is the one the song asks out loud: "What could have been done more to my vineyard, that I have not done in it?" (5:4).

Sit with that as a real question rather than a rhetorical one. It is God asking what else He could possibly have given. Try to answer it. The difficulty of finding an answer is the point.

A Prophecy Can Be True More Than Once, and This Bothers People ▶︎

Isaiah 7:14 had a first home, and it had a clock in it. A terrified king, two armies marching on him, and a sign: a child would be born, and "before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings" (7:16). Before one child could tell right from wrong, the crisis would be over. That was a real promise to a real king, and it came true in his lifetime.

And the butter and honey of 7:15 belong to that same sign — the verse that looked as though it had wandered in from somewhere else. The chapter explains the food itself a few verses later. When the land is ruined, a man will keep "a young cow, and two sheep" and live on what they give, "for butter and honey shall every one eat that is left in the land" (7:21–22), while the vineyards go to "briers and thorns" (7:23). So it is not a luxury. It is what is left to eat when the farms are gone — and the child of the sign grows up eating it, on the far side of the invasion the sign announces. (Readers do differ: some take the food as a picture of plenty rather than of ruin. Isaiah 7:21–25 is why this guide reads it the other way.)

Then Matthew. Writing about the birth of Jesus Christ, he says "all this was done, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet" (Matthew 1:22), and quotes Isaiah 7:14. Note whose voice that is — Matthew's own narration, not words the Savior quoted. And note what it does to the first fulfilment: nothing. The sign given to Ahaz was not used up when Ahaz's crisis passed.

That is what makes this ask for more care rather than less. If finding one fulfilment does not close a passage, no reading is finished just because an answer fits. But the opposite mistake is nearer to hand: a passage that can mean more than one thing looks, to a reader in a hurry, like a passage that can mean anything. Both errors live in the same verse — stopping too early, and running too far.

What keeps the second reading honest is not ingenuity. Matthew does not offer his as a clever discovery; he says it was "spoken of the Lord," and he writes under the same Spirit that spoke it. That is the safeguard Elder Dallin H. Oaks names in "Scripture Reading and Revelation" (Ensign, January 1995): because these prophecies can carry more than one meaning, it "underscores the importance of our seeking revelation." Not our cleverness. Revelation.

Which makes the work ordinary and the help promised. Read the passage in its own setting first — who is speaking, to whom, about what — and then ask. That is a relief rather than a burden: the reading is not a puzzle anyone has to be clever enough to solve alone. (The fuller Oaks passage and the pattern behind it are in Historical & Cultural Context, section 4.)

Moroni Quoted Chapter 11 to a Seventeen-Year-Old ▶︎

On the night of 21 September 1823, Moroni "quoted the eleventh chapter of Isaiah, saying that it was about to be fulfilled" (Joseph Smith—History 1:40). Joseph was seventeen — born in December 1805, three years past the First Vision and four years from the plates.

Read Isaiah 11 knowing that. "There shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots" — growth from a cut-back stock rather than from a tree at its height. Out of it comes someone who judges the poor with righteousness. Then the wolf and the lamb. Then, in verse 11, the Lord setting His hand "the second time" to recover the remnant of His people, gathered from every direction.

That image — life kept in something that looks finished — turns up in more than one place in scripture, and the Lord Himself answered a direct question about what this particular stem is. (Set out in full in Key Passages, passage 7.)

That is our doctrine of the gathering, in a chapter written while an empire was carrying Isaiah's neighbours away. It was not a comfortable thing to say. It was said anyway, to people watching the worst thing they knew of happen to the family next door.

And Then Chapter 12, Which Is Six Verses Long ▶︎

After eleven chapters of arraignment, woe, invasion and stumps, the whole thing resolves into a song — and the line the week is named for: "Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust, and not be afraid" (12:2).

That sentence is built on a familiar root, and Word Studies, study 5 takes it apart properly. The word for salvation here is yᵊšûʿâ, and the name Jesus — Yēšûaʿ — is built from the same Hebrew root, the verb yāšaʿ, to save.

And so is the prophet's own name. Isaiah is Yᵊšaʿyāhû, which means "the LORD saves."

So the hymn that closes these twelve chapters says, in a sentence, what the prophet's name says in a word — God is my salvation. Whether Isaiah noticed that or arranged it, the text does not tell

us. What it does show is that the song closing these twelve chapters and the prophet's own name make the same claim. *(Chapter 12 ends this opening section of Isaiah, not the book —

there are sixty-six chapters in all.)*

Keep the claim the right size. This is a shared root, the way save, saviour and salvation share one in English — not a hidden second translation of the verse, and not the name Jesus concealed in Isaiah 12:2. The verse says what it says. What the root adds is that the words for rescue, the prophet's name, and the Saviour's name all grow from the same three letters.

The Invitation ▶︎

If you read nothing else this week, read Isaiah 6 slowly, and notice the order — the seeing, the undoing, the coal, and only then the sending.

And then try this. Take a line from these chapters that you have heard before and read the whole chapter around it. "Unto us a child is born" is the easiest one to test, because it is usually met in December, on its own. Read Isaiah 9:1 through 9:21 straight through.

Why bother, when you already know verse 6? Because the verse has a war on one side of it and, from verse 8, a people who answer their own ruin by planning a grander rebuild. On its own the promise sounds like the end of the trouble. In place, it arrives in the middle of it — which is the more useful thing to know about a promise. (The chapter's three units are walked through in the Overview, at Isaiah 9.)

These twelve chapters turn on two things that are hard to hold at once: God is holy, and God comes close.

Here is why they pull against each other. Holy carries goodness, but it carries more than

that: it is the language of what is set apart, of the sanctuary and what may come near it. That is the sense doing the work in chapter 6 — the seraphim say the word three times, the doorposts

shake, and Isaiah's first reaction to seeing God is not joy but "Woe is me! for I am undone." He does not expect to survive being that close. He says it because he has just seen the difference

between what God is and what he is.

And the week answers that twice.

The coal. The distance is real, and it is closed from God's side — something is carried to Isaiah

from the altar, and the very part of him he had just confessed was unclean is the part that is

touched. He does not become worthier and then approach. He is made able to stay.

The Child. The names in 9:6 put "the mighty God" and a born infant in one sentence, and the

sign of 7:14 is Immanuel — God with us. The same problem answered from the other direction: not

a man made fit for the throne room, but God arriving inside the human situation.

Latter-day Saints read both as pointing to Jesus Christ — the altar that made Isaiah able to

stay, and the Child who came all the way in. *(That is what the gospel makes of these chapters; it is

not a claim that the coal or the Hebrew words name Him outright.) It is why a stretch of scripture this severe can end with somebody singing. (Chapter 12 closes this opening unit, not the book;

Isaiah runs on to 66.)*
