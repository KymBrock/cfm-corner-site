First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week was a homecoming. When the Persian king Cyrus let the exiles go, the Jews who wanted to return went back to Jerusalem to rebuild the temple. This week is about the ones who stayed.

Not everyone went home. Many Jews had been living in Persia for generations, in cities far from Jerusalem, and they remained there — a scattered people (the word for this is the diaspora, "the scattering") living under a foreign king. Esther is their story.

Here is a detail worth holding onto: Esther takes place during the very same years as Ezra and Nehemiah. The king the book calls Ahasuerus is almost certainly Xerxes, who ruled Persia in between the kings those two books describe. So at the same moment last week's Jews were laying stones in Jerusalem, this week's Jews were surviving in a pagan royal court a thousand miles to the east — the same people, the same era, at opposite ends of the empire.

That difference is the whole point. The Jews back in Jerusalem had a temple, a homeland, and prophets. The Jews in Persia had none of that. They were a small, hidden minority in someone else's kingdom — no temple, no prophet, and, as we'll see, no God who ever speaks or acts out in the open. Esther asks a question the returning exiles didn't have to face the same way: How does a covenant people survive when God seems completely absent?

A Letter to Fellow Students ▶︎

Esther is the strangest book in the Old Testament, and one of the most quietly powerful — because of what it never says.

Across ten chapters of palace intrigue, deadly danger, and a last-minute rescue, the name of God is never mentioned once. No prayer is answered. No prophet speaks. No sea parts. In a Bible where God is usually the loudest voice on the page, He goes completely silent for an entire book. That silence isn't an accident — it's the whole design. And once you notice it, you can't stop noticing it. You start reading the story listening for a voice that never comes.

And yet an unseen hand is clearly arranging everything. Watch the "coincidences" pile up. A queen is removed, leaving the throne empty — before anyone knows a crisis is coming. A young Jewish orphan named Esther, raised by her cousin Mordecai, is chosen as the new queen, and she keeps her Jewish identity secret. Mordecai happens to overhear a plot to kill the king, reports it, and it gets written into the royal records — and forgotten. Then the villain arrives: Haman, so enraged that Mordecai won't bow to him that he decides to destroy every Jew in the empire. To pick the day, he casts pur — a lot, like rolling dice (3:7). A whole people's survival now hangs on one man's wounded pride and the roll of a die.

Then comes the turning point: Mordecai's message to Esther — "who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14) — and her answer, "if I perish, I perish" (4:16). She risks her life to approach the king, and he receives her. From there the story turns, through a chain of events too precise to be luck: one night the king "could not sleep" (6:1), so the forgotten record of Mordecai's loyalty is read aloud — at the exact moment Haman arrives to ask for Mordecai's execution. The gallows Haman built for Mordecai becomes his own (7:10). And on the very day the enemies of the Jews expected to destroy them, "it was turned to the contrary" (9:1).

The book that never names God is, start to finish, a testimony that He is running everything — most powerfully when He seems most hidden. That's what we'll sit with this week.

The God Who Is Never Named ▶︎

Here is what most of us read right past, because we're so used to a Bible where God is always speaking: in Esther, He isn't. Not once. There is no "and the Lord said," no answered prayer, no angel, no prophet, no miracle. The most religious moment in the book is a three-day fast (4:16) — and even there, the text pointedly never says anyone prayed or that God answered. For one whole book of scripture, heaven is quiet.

You could read that as a book where God is absent. And the whole art of Esther is to make you feel that absence — and then make it impossible to believe.

Because look at the "coincidences" side by side. A king, in a drunken rage, removes his queen — years before a Jewish girl will need that empty throne. That girl's cousin happens to overhear an assassination plot, and the record is filed away and forgotten — until the one night, years later, when the king can't sleep and asks for old records to be read, and those are the pages that come up, at the exact hour Haman is walking in to demand that cousin's death. A villain builds a gallows tall enough to be seen across the city — and dies on it himself. A lot is rolled to pick a random day for genocide, and that random day becomes the day of rescue. Any one of these is a coincidence. All of them in a row, all pointing the same direction, are something else.

That is the message of the book: hidden providence. God is completely in control — but He works through means so ordinary they look, from the inside, like luck. He isn't in the whirlwind or the fire here. He is in the king's insomnia. He is in the forgotten file. He is in the timing of a footstep at a door. The book leaves His name out so that you'll do the one thing that teaches you to see Him: look at the whole pattern and draw the conclusion yourself.

The Jewish teachers, who love this book, saw that hiddenness written into its very language. (These teachers are called the rabbis, and their collected discussions, written down over centuries, are called the Talmud.) They asked a playful question: where might the story of Esther be hidden in the earlier books of the Bible? Their answer is unforgettable. In the book of Deuteronomy, God warns that if His people turn away, "I will surely hide (in Hebrew, haster astir) my face" (Deuteronomy 31:18). And the name Esther sounds almost exactly like the Hebrew word astir, "I will hide." So the book of the hidden God is heard, they said, right inside God's promise to hide His face — a phrase they called hester panim, "the hiding of the face" (Talmud, Chullin 139b). For a book about a God who hides, they found the whole thing hidden in plain sight.

Here is why this matters for you and me. Most of us don't live in the book of Exodus, with its pillars of fire and parted seas. We live in the book of Esther. Most days we don't hear a voice from heaven or watch a miracle. We face real trouble with no visible answer — and we're tempted to conclude God is absent. Esther says: no. The silence is not absence. "Verily thou art a God that hidest thyself, O God of Israel, the Saviour" (Isaiah 45:15) — the God who hides His face is not the missing God but the saving one, working the whole time behind events you'll only recognize when you look back. The rabbis put it as a rule: God "does not strike at the Jewish people unless He has already created a remedy for them beforehand" (Talmud, Megillah 13b). Read Esther and you'll see it — the queen was already crowned (chapter 2) before the villain even hatched his plot (chapter 3). The cure was in place before the wound.

You can't see God's hand while you're inside the moment. You can only see it when you turn around and look at the whole shape of things. Esther is a book-length invitation to turn around and look.

A Parable Heard, Not Written ▶︎

Read as a kind of parable, the book opens with two queens, and the contrast between them says a lot.

The first is Vashti. Her name is Persian and means "beautiful," or "most desirable." She is the queen the king shows off — summoned to appear "to shew the people and the princes her beauty" (1:11). And she refuses to come. "Vashti refused to come at the king's commandment" (1:12), and the crown is taken from her.

In her place comes a hidden Jewish girl with two names: Esther, and her Hebrew name Hadassah, which means "myrtle," a small evergreen tree (2:7). That's a hopeful name. The prophet Isaiah uses the myrtle as a picture of God restoring His people: "instead of the brier shall come up the myrtle tree… for an everlasting sign that shall not be cut off" (Isaiah 55:13; see also Isaiah 41:19). And there's a gentle wordplay a Hebrew ear would catch: Hadassah sounds like chadashah, the Hebrew word for "new." It's the kind of echo you hear rather than read — fitting for a book that says so much without saying it out loud. One queen was desired and would not answer the king's call; the other was hidden, and became a sign of new life.

The Jewish festival of Purim, which celebrates Esther's story, keeps that theme of things not being what they seem. It's a joyful holiday of costumes and masks — a fitting way to remember a book in which God's people live in disguise, their queen hiding her very identity (2:10), surviving in a court full of foreign gods and rival powers where God is never once named.

And that's a picture of our world, too. In the last days, God's covenant people again live in a place where heaven can seem silent, surrounded by many competing voices and loyalties. Esther's story asks us the same question it asked her: when the King calls, will we be too comfortable to answer — or will we rise "for such a time as this"? Are we listening?

"For Such a Time as This" ▶︎

The most famous line in Esther is also the most misused, so let's read it slowly. When Mordecai learns of the plan to destroy the Jews, he sends word that Esther must go to the king and plead for her people. She hesitates — and for good reason: approaching the king uninvited could get her killed (4:11). Mordecai's answer is the heart of the whole book:

"For if thou altogether holdest thy peace at this time, then shall there enlargement and deliverance arise to the Jews from another place; but thou and thy father's house shall be destroyed: and who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14)

Notice what he does not say. He does not say, "It all depends on you." Just the opposite: rescue "will arise… from another place" — with or without her. God's purpose is certain. Esther is not holding up the universe. And yet — in the same breath — "who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" Her position is not an accident. She has been placed, on purpose, exactly where the crisis will be decided. If she refuses the moment, she loses it (and dies anyway).

This is the book turning down two easy mistakes at once. It rejects giving up — "nothing I do matters; God will handle it." And it rejects self-reliance — "it all rests on me." It holds both truths together: God's purpose is sure, and your courage is still required. God arranges the moment; you still have to walk into the room.

That's why the verse has become shorthand for a real principle. President Monson applied it straight to us: "you are some of our Heavenly Father's strongest children, and He has saved you to come to the earth 'for such a time as this'" (May You Have Courage, April 2009). President Faust told the women of the Church the same: "I believe your spirits may have been reserved for these latter days; that you, like Esther, have come to earth 'for such a time as this'" (The Virtues of Righteous Daughters of God, April 2003). This isn't flattery — it's responsibility. If God places people on purpose, then your place is an assignment, and the courage it asks of you is the reason you're there.

So the question Esther leaves us with isn't "are you important?" It's sharper: Where has God placed you — and what is that placement asking of you right now?

"If I Perish, I Perish" ▶︎

God sets the stage. But courage still has to step onto it. And Esther's courage is worth a close look, because of what it's made of.

"…so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish." (4:16)

She has no guarantee. The king may welcome her or he may not, and nothing promises which. She doesn't know how it ends. What she does decide is how she'll face it — and her decision has three parts. Fasting: she calls the whole community to fast with her for three days. Faith: she acts without knowing the outcome. And surrender: "if I perish, I perish" — she hands the ending to God. That's the opposite of both shrinking back and self-confidence. She neither runs away nor trusts in her own cleverness. She turns herself over, and walks in.

President Monson summed it up: "Esther had gathered her courage and would stand firm and immovable for that which was right… Esther, through fasting, faith, and courage, had saved a nation." Sister Camille Johnson described the same surrender as letting God write the story: "Esther is another beautiful example of letting God prevail. Rather than sticking with a cautious narrative of self-preservation, she exercised faith, turning herself completely over to the Lord" (Invite Christ to Author Your Story, October 2021). And Johnson names the hard part: Esther "was willing to let the Savior write her story even though, through the lens of mortality, the ending may have been tragic." That is what "if I perish, I perish" means. It isn't confidence that everything will work out. It's the willingness to do the right thing and let God hold the outcome — the same faith three young men showed before another royal death sentence: "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us… But if not… we will not serve thy gods" (Daniel 3:17–18).

There's a deeper pattern here, too. Someone who steps between a doomed people and the throne, willing to die so they can live, is a figure the Old Testament keeps reaching toward — and it points forward to Jesus Christ, who fulfilled it. In offering her life for her people, as the Interpreter Foundation's study aid puts it, Esther "becomes a type of Jesus Christ" — an early picture of the Savior, "willing to die to save her people." Centuries later, President Lorenzo Snow told the covenant people the same could be asked of them: "It may become necessary in the future… for some of the Saints to act the part of Esther" — to "sacrifice anything and everything… for the purpose of working out the deliverance" of God's people (Journal of Discourses 23:290).

It Was Turned to the Contrary ▶︎

Then everything flips — and it flips completely. The whole book is built on reversal, and it says so in its own words: "it was turned to the contrary" (9:1). The Hebrew phrase there is venahafoch hu, "and it was turned around," and it became the Jewish nickname for the entire story — the great turnaround (a Jewish commentary called Esther Rabbah 10 calls it a miracle "the like of which has never been performed").

Look at how complete the reversal is. Haman builds a towering gallows for Mordecai — and dies on it himself (7:10). The lot cast to destroy the Jews ends up marking the day of their rescue. The decree of death is answered by a decree of life. "The month which was turned unto them from sorrow to joy" (9:22). And the book explains exactly how: the king ordered that Haman's "wicked device, which he devised against the Jews, should return upon his own head" (9:25). That's not just a satisfying ending. It's a law scripture teaches everywhere — "Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein" (Proverbs 26:27); "His mischief shall return upon his own head" (Psalm 7:16), the very phrase Esther uses of Haman. The trap the wicked set for the righteous tends to snap shut on the one who set it. The Book of Mormon says the same — Nephi saw "that great pit… shall be filled by those who digged it" (1 Nephi 14:3) — and the Prophet Joseph Smith, writing from Liberty Jail while his own enemies seemed to be winning, turned Esther's reversal into a promise: "the Lord so ordered that Haman was hanged upon his own gallows. So shall it come to pass with poor Haman in the last days" (Personal Writings of Joseph Smith, p. 375).

But notice one crucial thing, or you'll misread the book: Esther and Mordecai never build the gallows. They dig no pit and take no revenge. The reversal is done to Haman, not by them. That's what keeps it God's justice instead of human payback. The righteous simply do what's right — Esther risks her life, Mordecai won't bow — and leave the turning around to God. The lesson of venahafoch hu is not that we get to repay evil; it's that we don't have to. "Thou hast turned for me my mourning into dancing" (Psalm 30:11); "ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good" (Genesis 50:20). The God who hides is the God who turns things around — quietly, completely, and right on time.

When History Rhymes ▶︎

Haman is not just any villain — the book gives him a family line, and it matters. It calls him "the Agagite" (3:1), meaning he descended from Agag, king of the Amalekites. The Amalekites were an ancient enemy of Israel. Centuries earlier, God had told King Saul to defeat them completely, and Saul disobeyed and spared their king (1 Samuel 15:8–9). "To obey is better than sacrifice," the prophet Samuel warned him (1 Samuel 15:22), and Saul lost his throne over that disobedience. Now, generations later, a descendant of the enemy Saul let live has risen to power in Persia and is trying to destroy every Jew in the empire. The lesson is sobering: a disobedience left unfinished doesn't simply disappear. It can wait, and come back.

There's a strange detail near the end of the book. After Haman is already dead, Esther asks the king to let Haman's ten sons be hanged the next day as well (9:13–14). Why ask to hang men who are already dead? The Jewish teachers — who count Esther as one of Israel's prophetesses (in the Talmud, Megillah 14a) — read her request as a kind of prophecy: that this same pattern would return one day. And in a way that startled people, it seemed to. On October 16, 1946, ten Nazi leaders were hanged for their crimes after the Nuremberg war-crimes trials. One of them, a man named Julius Streicher, reportedly shouted from the gallows, "Purimfest 1946!" — naming the very festival that celebrates Haman's downfall (TheTorah.com studies the connection; Newsweek reported his words). The point is not that Esther predicted World War II. The point is something scripture teaches better than any other book: history moves in patterns, and the evil a person plots against God's people has a way of returning on his own head. Learning to see the pattern is part of learning to trust God.

And there's one more echo, the most tender of all. In her most frightened moment, Jewish tradition places the words of Psalm 22 on Esther's lips: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" It was a psalm many Jewish mothers taught their children, and some Jewish communities still read it on the fast day before Purim. Those are the exact words the Savior speaks from the cross (Matthew 27:46). Both Esther and Jesus stand alone, feeling abandoned, between a doomed people and death — and both are heard. This is part of why Esther points us toward Christ: like Him, she was willing to die to save her people, and they pray the very same prayer. (For more on all of this, see the culture guide Purim — The Feast of Lots, the article Purim: Hidden Providence, and Seven Sermons from the Cross.)

So when we meet our own "Hamans" — an old mistake we never dealt with, or an enemy who seems to hold all the power — Esther shows us what to do. Do your part with courage and covenant faithfulness. Speak the truth. Then let God do what you cannot. Remember: Esther and Mordecai never built the gallows. Haman built his own.

The Feast of Remembering ▶︎

The book ends by building a memory on purpose. Because the day chosen for destruction became the day of rescue, Mordecai and Esther set up an annual festival called Purim — named "after the name of Pur," the lot Haman cast (9:26) — so that "these days should be remembered and kept throughout every generation" (9:28). And it's a wonderfully ordinary way to remember an extraordinary rescue: not a monument, but "feasting and joy, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor" (9:22).

That fits the book perfectly. Esther has no pillar of fire to point back to, no parted sea — only a string of "coincidences" that, retold every year, become a testimony. And that's the deep lesson of Purim: remembering is how we make the hidden hand visible. You can't see God's providence while you're living through it; you can see it when you look back and tell the story. So the tradition built a festival whose whole purpose is to make people look back once a year and say, out loud, look what God did — even in a story where God is never named.

President Eyring taught the same habit. He described pausing each night to ask himself one question: "Have I seen the hand of God reaching out to touch us or our children or our family today?" And he urged us to "find ways to recognize and remember God's kindness. It will build our testimonies" (O Remember, Remember, October 2007). That's a personal Purim — a way to keep your own quiet rescues from fading. The sacrament is the same instinct at the center of our worship: bread and water taken "in remembrance," so we "always remember him" (Moroni 4:3). A rescue we forget is a rescue half-lost. Esther teaches us to build the memory on purpose.

The Invitation ▶︎

Esther is the book for a silent heaven.

It's the book for everyone who has faced a real crisis with no voice from above and no obvious miracle, and wondered whether God had left the room. Its answer is not that He was gone, but that He was hidden — running every "coincidence," preparing the rescue before the danger, turning the whole thing around at just the right moment. So this week leaves us with a few honest, practical questions:

Can you read your own life the way Esther asks you to read the book — turning around, looking at the whole shape of it, and letting the pile of "coincidences" become a testimony? Where has God placed you "for such a time as this," and what courage is that placement asking of you now? When the frightening decision comes, can you do the right thing and, like Esther, hand the ending to God — "if I perish, I perish"? When someone digs a pit for you, can you leave the turnaround to God instead of building a gallows of your own? And will you build your own "Purim" — some regular habit of remembering — so your quiet rescues are never forgotten?

The name of God is never spoken in Esther. And He is never, for one single moment, absent. "Verily thou art a God that hidest thyself, O God of Israel, the Saviour." He is hidden. He is here. And He has brought you, too, to the kingdom for such a time as this.
