First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week was Isaiah 1–12, and it ended in a song: "Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust, and not be afraid." It began in a courtroom and closed with a man singing.

This week the camera pulls back. The reading opens on the fall of an empire and widens until it is describing the whole earth emptied — and then, on the far side of that, a mountain with a table on it and every nation invited.

There is one more difference worth naming before you start. Last week's chapters ran straight through, one to twelve. This week the reading skips: thirteen and fourteen, then twenty-two, then twenty-four to thirty, then thirty-five. The Overview maps all sixty-six chapters of Isaiah, so you can see exactly where this week's eleven sit and what is around them.

Why These Chapters Exist ▶︎

This is the thing most worth knowing before you open the first page, and it makes the whole week readable.

Isaiah preached while his world was under threat. Assyria deported the northern kingdom during his ministry, and those exiles are not recorded as returning as a people; Judah, next door, had every reason to read that as a warning about itself. Not every chapter here can be tied to a particular crisis with confidence — which is a relief rather than a gap. You do not have to place a chapter on a timeline before it can speak to you; the fear these chapters answer is the same fear whichever decade it was first spoken into. (The sequence is in Historical & Cultural Context, section 1.)

So when these chapters promise that God will gather His people, that is not a pleasant abstraction. It is the specific answer to the specific thing those people were afraid of. And the promise is unexpectedly personal:

"ye shall be gathered one by one, O ye children of Israel" — Isaiah 27:12

Not a crowd. One at a time.

It is also why Nephi read Isaiah aloud to discouraged brothers, telling "a branch who have been broken off" to liken the words to themselves "that ye may have hope" (1 Nephi 19:24). He says plainly what he found there: "he verily saw my Redeemer, even as I have seen him" (2 Nephi 11:2). (The Overview opening sets out the history, and Context, section 6, the records.)

Isaiah is the prophet who told scattered people that scattering is survivable. If you have ever felt outside the thing you belong to, these are chapters worth reading slowly — they were spoken to people in exactly that position, and the gathering they promise counts individuals. (For the empires, the exile and the records, see Historical & Cultural Context.)

When a Mercy Becomes an Assumption ▶︎

Scripture names this danger directly, so we need not guess at it. Jeremiah stood in the temple gate in Lehi's own generation and told the crowd what not to lean on: "Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these" (Jeremiah 7:4). They were not wrong about the temple. They were wrong about what having it guaranteed.

That is what gratitude can do when it is left alone too long: it hardens into expectation, and the expectation attaches itself to the wrong object — a place, a position, a record of past help.

The question Isaiah keeps putting is not whether God has been good to you. It is whether you are still looking at Him, or at the evidence. Chapter 22 finds a city admiring its own walls and water — it had repaired them well, and it "looked not unto the maker thereof" (22:11). Chapter 30 finds a nation counting on Egypt's chariots instead of asking at the Lord's mouth (30:1–2). Both had reasons. Neither looked up.

So it is worth asking, before the reading starts: what has God actually done for me — and have I quietly turned any of it into something I now assume?

The Problem and the Remedy Are in the Same Sentence ▶︎

Isaiah 29:13 describes people who are keeping the practice — drawing near, honouring God with their lips — while something fails to travel the distance: "have removed their heart far from me." Notice what the verse does and does not say: it contrasts what the mouth does with where the heart is, and leaves the inward question where only God can answer it.

Then the very next verse, without a pause: "Therefore, behold, I will proceed to do a marvellous work among this people, even a marvellous work and a wonder."

It is worth being exact about how hard that first half is, because softening it would cost us the second half. When the Savior quoted verse 13 (Matthew 15:7–9) He introduced it with "Ye hypocrites"; when the Lord quoted it to Joseph Smith in the grove in 1820 (Joseph Smith—History 1:19) it came inside a hard answer. Both times it was a rebuke, and both times it was His. This is not a verse about being a bit distracted — and that is precisely why the marvellous work that follows it is worth having. A remedy is only as good as the trouble it is answering.

And yet the sentence does not stop at the rebuke. God says therefore and announces a marvellous work — and the same sentence says what that work costs: "the wisdom of their wise men shall perish, and the understanding of their prudent men shall be hid." So the remedy arrives carrying a judgment — and it is worth being precise about what is judged. The verse says the wisdom of their wise men: wisdom held as a possession, sufficient in itself, with no further need of God. It is not a verdict on learning. The same chapter ends by handing learning back: "they that murmured shall learn doctrine" (29:24), and understanding, hearing and sight all return in verses 18–24. What changes is not how much a person knows but whether they are still listening.

That is why the chapter's word for who receives the joy is meek rather than unlearned: "the meek also shall increase their joy in the LORD" (29:19). Meekness is a posture, and a scholar can hold it as readily as anyone.

That order is the point. The diagnosis is not the end of the paragraph. Whatever distance you have noticed in yourself, the pattern this chapter sets is to let it be named and then look at what God does next.

The Four Most Encouraging Minutes in the Week ▶︎

Read Isaiah 5 and Isaiah 27:2–6 back to back. That is the whole assignment, and it takes about four minutes.

The Overview walks what changed between them. What is worth sitting with here is the posture in 27:3 — "I will water it every moment… I will keep it night and day." Every moment. Night and day. That is not the language of a God who has written anyone off, and it is said about the same vineyard He had once walked away from.

If you have ever assumed God had finished with some part of your life, this is the chapter that argues with you.

What Happens After You Say No ▶︎

Isaiah 30 records a refusal — "and ye would not" (30:15) — and the chapter does not soften who was refusing. (The Overview and Key Passages, passage 7, set out what was offered and what turning back would have cost.)

The part worth carrying into your own week is what comes after the no.

The chapter could have ended at verse 15. Instead it keeps going, and lands on something quieter than a second chance: "thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the way, walk ye in it" (30:21).

A voice behind you, correcting course as you go. Not a voice that stopped speaking when you did not listen the first time.

If you have ever declined something you knew was right and assumed the offer expired, this chapter is evidence to the contrary.

One Clause to Take With You ▶︎

The reading ends on a road, and the Overview follows where it goes. Take one clause from it into the week:

"…and sorrow and sighing shall flee away." — Isaiah 35:10

Not endured. Not carried. Gone the other way.

Read the grammar of it. Sorrow and sighing are the subject of the sentence, and the verb belongs to them: they are the ones who flee. The verse asks nothing of the griever at all — it describes sorrow leaving, while the people it had been following walk on, named ransomed and redeemed.

That is hope held out for what is coming. It is not an instruction to pretend you are not sad now. Isaiah 35 is honest about the desert it is describing; it simply does not believe the desert gets the last word.

A family once carried these chapters across an ocean so their children would still have them. This is the sort of sentence that was worth the trouble.
