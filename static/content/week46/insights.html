First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week Ezekiel was among the exiles in Babylon, by the river Chebar, seeing visions of God's glory far from the temple. This week we meet other exiles in the same empire, in the king's own palace. Daniel and his three friends were taken from Jerusalem as young men, given Babylonian names and a Babylonian education, and expected to become Babylonian. They never did.

The first six chapters are stories, each with a test. The seventh is a vision of beasts, thrones, and one like the Son of Man. The Overview walks every chapter, and Historical & Cultural Context explains the empire they lived in.

A New Field Guide: Persia ▶︎

Daniel lived long enough to see one empire give way to another. He came to Babylon as a young captive, served Nebuchadnezzar, and was still there on the night Belshazzar's kingdom ended (Daniel 5:30–31). Then he served the new rulers too. "So this Daniel prospered in the reign of Darius, and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian" (Daniel 6:28).

Our newest field guide, The Achaemenid Empire: Persia and the Return, tells the story of that second empire. It also explains a few details in Daniel 6 that are easy to read past. When Darius "set over the kingdom an hundred and twenty princes" (Daniel 6:1), Daniel is describing an arrangement much like the one the Persians used across their whole empire, with large provinces each governed by a royal official. And "the law of the Medes and Persians, which altereth not" (Daniel 6:8) is why a king who loved Daniel still could not save him from the lions. The book of Esther turns on the same unchangeable law. How the Empire Worked explains both. Timeline & Kings covers the fall of Babylon to Cyrus, and why the identity of "Darius the Median" (Daniel 5:31) is still uncertain.

The guide carries the story on to Cyrus, who let the exiles go home, and Daniel saw that day come: "Daniel continued even unto the first year of king Cyrus" (Daniel 1:21). If you are coming to the guides for the first time, Babylon: The Empire and the Exile fills in the first half of the week: Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar, and the learning of the Chaldeans that Daniel and his friends were trained in.

"But If Not" ▶︎

There is a kind of faith that says, God will rescue me. It is real faith, and the three young men had it: "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us" (Daniel 3:17).

Then they said three more words that turned their faith into something even stronger: "But if not." Even if He does not, "we will not serve thy gods" (Daniel 3:18).

Most of us have prayed for something that did not come the way we asked: a healing, a reunion, a door that stayed shut. In those moments it is easy to wonder whether our faith was too small. Shadrach, Meshach and Abed-nego show another way to hold it. Their faithfulness was never a bargain with God. It was a decision about whom they belonged to. Sister Joy D. Jones put it in one sentence: "Keeping our covenants is always independent of our situation" (A Sin-Resistant Generation).

And notice what happened next. The Lord did not leave them alone in the furnace. The king looked in and saw four men walking in the fire, "and they have no hurt" (Daniel 3:25). Whatever your furnace is this week, you are not in it alone.

A Habit Already in Place ▶︎

When Daniel heard that praying to anyone but the king could cost him his life, he did not make a speech. He went home, knelt by his open windows toward Jerusalem, and "prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime" (Daniel 6:10).

As he did aforetime. Daniel's courage in the lions' den was not made that day. It was made over years of ordinary mornings, middays and evenings. When the test came, he did not have to decide what kind of man to be. He only had to keep being him.

And look at one word in that verse: thanks. On the day the decree was signed, Daniel still gave thanks.

Try this tomorrow. Pick a time for prayer and keep it all week, whatever the day brings, "morning, mid-day, and evening" (Alma 34:21) if you can. Begin each prayer with one thing you are thankful for, even on the hard days. That is how a habit is built before it is needed.

The Kingdom That Lasts ▶︎

This week is full of kings: Nebuchadnezzar, who built Babylon "by the might of my power" (Daniel 4:30); Belshazzar, who drank from the temple's vessels; Darius, who could not change his own law. And again and again, the kings end up saying the same thing about Daniel's God. Nebuchadnezzar: "his kingdom is an everlasting kingdom" (Daniel 4:3). Darius: "his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed" (Daniel 6:26). In the Aramaic, the words for kingdom and everlasting come together in seven verses of these chapters, spoken by Daniel, by two pagan kings, and by the vision itself (Overview, "Central Insight").

The world's kingdoms in Daniel are statues and beasts: impressive, frightening, and temporary. The kingdom that lasts is a stone cut out without hands, and in the end it is given to "one like the Son of man" (Daniel 7:13). Scripture Helps says it plainly: "The Son of Man refers to Jesus Christ." On the last day of His mortal life, He took Daniel's words to Himself before the council that condemned Him (Mark 14:62).

When the news is full of statues and beasts, Daniel 7 is a good chapter to read slowly. The beasts lose their dominion. His dominion "shall not pass away" (Daniel 7:14).

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

"Therefore I make a decree… because there is no other God that can deliver after this sort." — Daniel 3:29

The man who built the idol said it, after he watched three faithful young men walk out of his fire. Deliver there is nᵊṣal (נְצַל), to rescue, to pull out of danger (Word Studies, study 1). When we read "deliver us from evil" in the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:13), we can recognize that same plea for rescue.

He is able to deliver. He will deliver, in His way and His time. And if not today, He is still the only One worth trusting.
