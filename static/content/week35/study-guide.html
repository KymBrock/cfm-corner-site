1. Week 35: Overview

There is a kind of trouble that ordinary help cannot reach. A man has done something no apology covers. A family learns at a graveside that no amount of money would have bought back the one they loved. A believer lies awake at three in the morning wondering whether God has simply stopped caring. Every psalm you read this week comes from somewhere in that country — and the week takes its title from the prayer of the man who was furthest in.

Psalm 51 is David's prayer after the worst thing he ever did. The prophet Nathan had stood in front of the king and said, "Thou art the man" (2 Samuel 12:7) — Bathsheba, the arranged death of her husband, all of it uncovered. And under the law of Moses, David had nowhere to take it. The offerings on the altar covered sins of ignorance and weakness; for what he had done, the law appointed no sacrifice at all. He knew it — "thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it" (51:16). So he brought the only thing still his to give — "a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise" (51:17) — and asked for something no one in scripture had asked for before: "Create in me a clean heart" (51:10). Not mend. Not improve. Create — the verb of Genesis 1:1, the word scripture uses only when God is the one doing the making, when He organized the heavens and the earth out of chaos. David is not asking to be patched. He is asking to be made new, and he is asking the only One who does that kind of work.

If you have ever done something you could not undo, that prayer is yours — and it is the key to the whole week. Last week you read the psalms people memorize: the shepherd psalm, the two doors of Psalms 1 and 2, the psalm of the cross. This week you read the psalms people reach for when memorizing is not enough. Psalm 49 is a funeral sermon on the one thing money cannot buy. Psalm 69 is prayed by a man in over his head — "the waters are come in unto my soul" — too exhausted from crying to pray well. Psalm 71 is the prayer of a man grown old, asking not for comfort but for time enough to finish bearing his testimony. And Psalm 78 is Asaph — the choirmaster David set over the music of God's house — sitting the children down to tell them their own family history with the failures deliberately left in, because a story with the embarrassing parts removed will not hold the next generation.

These are not the prayers of people having a good week. And God's people have kept them, sung them, and prayed them for three thousand years — which tells you something about what God is willing to carry.

One more thing before you begin, and it is the deepest thing. The Savior knew these psalms and prayed from this book, and He taught that the Psalms testify of Him (Luke 24:44). This week's hardest psalm, Psalm 69, was written a thousand years before Golgotha and reads as though it were written there — the bitter drink, the reproach, the looking for comforters and finding none. When you pray "create in me a clean heart," you are asking for something He made possible. So read this week expecting to find yourself in it. If you have ever been too tired to pray well, Psalm 69 already said it. If you are afraid of running out of time before you have said what you meant to say, Psalm 71 has been waiting for you.

A note on how these psalms address God. You may notice this week's psalms say "God" far more often than "LORD." That is a real feature of Psalms 42–83, and there is a fascinating reason for it — including one place where you can watch it happen, since Psalm 70 is the same poem as Psalm 40:13–17, which you read last week, with the name changed. The full explanation is in Historical & Cultural Context, section 1, along with who Asaph and the sons of Korah were, what the little header lines above each psalm mean, and how the book of Psalms is arranged into five books. None of it is required to pray these psalms. All of it makes them richer.

Before the table, meet the names you will see in it — because this week's psalms are not all David's, and the others belong to two remarkable families of temple singers.

Asaph was the choirmaster David appointed over the music of the house of the Lord (1 Chronicles 16:5), and his psalms have their own sound. Where David mostly prays, Asaph examines: he puts the nation on the witness stand, asks the out-loud questions, teaches the children. You will read three of his this week (50, 77, 78) — and Psalms 73 through 83 are an unbroken run of him.

The sons of Korah carry the best backstory in the hymnbook. Their ancestor Korah led a rebellion against Moses and was swallowed by the earth (Numbers 16) — but the record adds, almost in passing, "Notwithstanding the children of Korah died not" (Numbers 26:11). The rebel's descendants became the singers of the sanctuary. Every psalm with their name on it is proof that a family's story does not have to end at its worst chapter. Their two psalms bracket this week, at 49 and 85.

And one psalm is nobody's. Psalm 71, the prayer of old age, carries no name at all — as though it were left unsigned so that anyone could pray it.

Read Psalm 69 this week slowly, because you are reading the crucifixion.

Not a prophecy in the sense of a prediction with a date on it. Something stranger and more moving: a man in real anguish, writing about his own suffering, whose words the Gospel writers found themselves reaching for again and again as they described what happened to Jesus Christ. Few chapters of the Psalms are carried into the New Testament more often.

Look at what the psalm actually says, and then at what happened at Golgotha:

That last one deserves a moment. A psalmist a thousand years earlier wrote down the bitterness he was given to drink — and on the day it mattered most, soldiers who had never read a psalm handed the Son of God gall and vinegar. This is what the Savior meant when He told the disciples that the Psalms testify of Him (Luke 24:44). He was not pointing at a handful of coded predictions. He was saying: the suffering of the righteous, written down honestly across a thousand years, was always describing what I came to do.

What this asks of you as you read

Psalm 69 is still a psalm you can pray — and that may need explaining. Latter-day Saints do not usually pray words somebody else wrote; we are taught to speak to God in our own. So "praying a psalm" can sound like a foreign practice. But the Lord Himself put it plainly to Emma Smith: "my soul delighteth in the song of the heart; yea, the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me" (D&C 25:12). That is exactly what a psalm is — a song of the heart, which is to say a prayer. They were sung, not recited; the book is Israel's hymnbook before it is anything else. And it works the other way too: our hymnbook is our Psalter. "Where can I turn for peace?" does what Psalm 77 does; "I Need Thee Every Hour" is Psalm 63's thirst; a Primary child singing "I Am a Child of God" is praying Psalm 8. Note the two conditions the Lord attached — the song is of the heart, not of the voice, and He asks that we sing with understanding. Meet those and the promise is unconditional: "it shall be answered with a blessing upon their heads." When you take Psalm 69's words up as your own, you are doing what the Lord said He delights in. That is the point of it being in the Psalter rather than in a prophecy collection. Its writer was drowning — "the waters are come in unto my soul" (69:1) — exhausted from weeping (69:3), estranged from his own family (69:8), and shamed in public. Those are ordinary human sorrows, and they are also the Savior's. When you bring that kind of trouble to God, you are praying words He Himself lived. There are few things in scripture more comforting than that.

And notice where the psalm goes. It does not end in the pit. It turns — "I will praise the name of God with a song" (69:30) — and closes with God saving Zion. The pattern is the pattern of the Atonement itself: down into the depths, and up again. A psalm that describes Good Friday ends the way Sunday ends.

Whose voice is it? A skill worth keeping

One habit will serve you every time a psalm surfaces in the New Testament: notice whose voice it is. Sometimes the Savior quotes the psalm Himself. Sometimes Peter, or Paul, or the disciples do. And sometimes nobody quotes anything — the Gospel writer simply tells you what happened, and a reader who knows the psalm hears it underneath. All three are real. They are just different, the way "he said it" and "it came true" are different — and keeping them apart protects something precious: it means that when we say the Savior said something, He actually said it.

A psalm nobody can afford. Psalm 49 asks a question a funeral makes unavoidable: what would you pay to keep someone? The answer is blunt — "none of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him: for the redemption of their soul is precious" (49:7–8). Precious there means costly: the price is real, and no one has it. Hold that verse next to the Savior's own words: He came "to give his life a ransom for many" (Matthew 20:28; Mark 10:45). Psalm 49 names the debt; the Gospel names who paid it — and it does so in the same vocabulary. The verb Psalm 49 uses for "redeem" is pādâ (פָּדָה), to buy a person out of bondage; the noun for "ransom" is kōp̄er (כֹּפֶר), the price that releases a life otherwise forfeit. When the psalm was translated into Greek, pādâ became lytroō (λυτρόω), and its noun lytron (λύτρον) is the exact word on the Savior's lips. Psalm 49 says no one can pay it. The Gospel says One did. (See Word Studies, study 5.)

The drumbeat the King James muffles. Psalm 62 opens six of its verses with one short Hebrew word, 'ak — "only," "surely," "truly." Hebrew readers hear it drumming. The King James translators, wanting good English, rendered it three different ways — truly, only, surely — and moved it around inside the line: "Truly" (v. 1), "He only" (v. 2), "They only" (v. 4), "wait thou only" (v. 5), "He only" (v. 6), "Surely" (v. 9). Good English, and the drumbeat blurs. Read Psalm 62 aloud putting only at the front of verses 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 9, and the psalm's argument suddenly stands up: only God, only God, only God — and everything else, weighed on the same scales, comes out "lighter than vanity" (62:9). The Hebrew for "vanity" there is hevel (הֶבֶל) — a breath, a puff of vapour, the same word that opens Ecclesiastes.

What David does not ask for. Read Psalm 51 watching for what is missing. It never asks for the consequences to be lifted — and they were not: the child dies, and the sword never departs from David's house. What it asks for instead is stranger and better: "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation" (51:12). Not the throne, not the reputation, not even peace — the joy. Repentance, in this psalm, is not a negotiation over outcomes. It is getting back the one thing sin actually took.

The question Psalm 49 asks, and the answer thirty-seven psalms later. Psalm 49 puts the problem plainly: no one can redeem a brother, and the price of a soul is beyond anybody's reach. Then, in the same psalm, it says who can — "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave: for he shall receive me" (49:15). Hold that open and read to the end of the week. "Great is thy mercy toward me: and thou hast delivered my soul from the lowest hell" (86:13). The same word for the self — nephesh (נֶפֶשׁ), the soul, the life — and the same destination it is rescued from: sheol (שְׁאוֹל), the grave, the pit. Psalm 49 says no man can pay it and God will; Psalm 86 records it done. And the man saying so is not a king or a hero — he calls himself "poor and needy" in his very first verse. The week opens on a debt and closes on a receipt. Read 49:7–9, 49:15 and 86:13 together; they are thirty-seven psalms apart and they are one conversation.

A verse for the nights when prayer stops working. Psalm 77 begins badly. The writer cannot sleep, cannot speak, and refuses to be comforted; then he asks six questions in a row, each one darker than the last — "Is his mercy clean gone for ever? doth his promise fail for evermore?" (77:8). What happens next is one of the most quietly extraordinary turns in scripture. He stops and says: "This is my infirmity" (77:10). He does not answer the questions. He identifies them. They are not conclusions he has reached about God; they are what the trouble sounds like from inside. And then he does four things anyone can do tonight, printed across verses 10 through 12: remember the years of the right hand of the Most High, remember His works, meditate on them, and talk about them — the King James's own word for the last verb. Out loud, to someone. Because the one place the answer is not going to come from just now is his own head. (See Teaching Applications for using this with someone in a hard season.)

A prayer for the second half of life. Psalm 71 is the prayer of someone old, and its request is not what you would expect. It does not ask for comfort, or health, or an easy end. It asks for time — "now also when I am old and grayheaded, O God, forsake me not; until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come" (71:18). The fear is not dying. The fear is stopping before the telling is done. It is one of the few psalms with no header line at all, as though it were left deliberately unsigned so that anyone could pray it.

  • Wealth and sacrifice, put in their place (49–50). Two psalms that take the things people trust in and weigh them. Psalm 49 is a funeral sermon: the rich man's "inward thought is, that their houses shall continue for ever" (49:11), and it does not. Psalm 50 is a courtroom: God summons heaven and earth, and then says the thing nobody expects — "I will take no bullock out of thy house" (50:9). He is not hungry. He never was. What He asks for is thanksgiving and a kept promise (50:14).
  • The broken heart (51). David at his lowest, after Nathan. Watch what he does not offer: "thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it" (51:16). He knows the altar has nothing for him. So he brings "a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise" (51:17) — and asks God to create what he cannot repair.
  • Finding your footing (61–66). A man learning where the ground is. From "the end of the earth" he asks to be led "to the rock that is higher than I" (61:2). In the wilderness of Judah his body itself makes the argument: "my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is" (63:1). And by Psalm 65 the same God who answers prayer is out watering the furrows and crowning "the year with thy goodness" (65:11) — the God of the crisis is the God of the ordinary harvest.
  • The drowning man (69–70). "The waters are come in unto my soul" (69:1) — not a metaphor a person reaches for calmly. Psalm 69 is the psalm the Gospel writers return to again and again at the cross. Psalm 70 follows it with five verses of pure urgency: "make haste to help me, O LORD" (70:1).
  • Two prayers about finishing (71–72). Psalm 71 asks for time enough to testify. Psalm 72 prays for a king, and measures him by an unusual standard — not by armies or borders but by whether "he shall deliver the needy when he crieth; the poor also, and him that hath no helper" (72:12). Then Book Two closes: "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended" (72:20).
  • The sleepless question (77–78). Asaph cannot sleep, and asks it straight out: "Hath God forgotten to be gracious?" (77:9). His answer is not an argument.   • Mercy and truth (85–86). After the exile, the two things that seem to pull against each other arrive together: "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (85:10). Then Psalm 86 asks for something quieter and harder — "unite my heart to fear thy name" (86:11): make the divided heart one.

This is the week for anyone who has ever run out of the ordinary remedies.

Psalm 49 stands at a graveside and says the price of a soul is too high for any man to pay — "none of them can by any means redeem his brother" (49:7). Psalm 51 is prayed by a man the law had nothing left to offer. Psalm 77 is prayed by a man the night had nothing left to offer. The week takes you, on purpose, to the end of what people can arrange for themselves — and there, every one of these psalms turns the same direction. David does not ask to be improved; he asks to be created new. Asaph does not answer his sleepless question with an argument; he answers it by remembering what God has already done. The old man of Psalm 71 does not ask for ease; he asks for time to testify.

And the New Testament tells you where all of it was pointing. Psalm 49 said no man could pay the ransom for a brother's soul. A thousand years later the Savior said He had come "to give his life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45) — and in the Greek Bible of His day, "ransom" (λύτρον, lytron) is the very word Psalm 49's translators had chosen. The debt the psalm declared unpayable was paid. Psalm 51 begged for a clean heart created new; after His Resurrection the Savior named the price of admission to that miracle, and it is exactly what David brought: "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (3 Nephi 9:20). The offering David made when he had nothing else turned out to be the offering the Lord asks of everyone.

So bring Him the week you are actually having. If it holds something you cannot undo, pray Psalm 51 slowly, and mean the verb. If it holds something you cannot carry, Psalm 62 will teach you where the word only belongs. And if it is simply long and tiring, Psalm 69 has already been prayed all the way to the bottom — by its writer, and then in the life of the Son of God — and it does not end in the pit. It ends where this whole week ends: "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (Psalm 85:10). They met in Christ. They still do.

First, what "Book One" means. The Psalms are not one long collection but five, deliberately arranged — an echo of the five books of Moses, and the rabbis said so explicitly. Each division closes with a short burst of praise, and your King James Bible prints the divisions as headings:

This week you cross the seam from Book Two into Book Three — which is why Psalm 72 ends with a line that sounds like the close of everything, "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended" (72:20), when in fact seventy-eight psalms still lie ahead.

Last week's psalms came almost entirely from Book One. This week you cross into different territory, and the difference is visible in the King James text if you know to look for it.

Fifteen of this week's seventeen psalms — every one from 49 through 78 — sit inside Psalms 42–83, and throughout that stretch the collectors preferred the ordinary Hebrew word for God, elohim (אֱלֹהִים), over His personal covenant name. That name is written with four Hebrew consonants, YHVH (יהוה) — scholars call it the Tetragrammaton, "the four letters" — and it is the name God revealed to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14–15). English speakers know it best as Jehovah; it is also written Yahweh. Because Hebrew wrote no vowels, and because Israel eventually stopped saying the name aloud out of reverence, nobody today is certain exactly how it was pronounced. Your King James Bible has a quiet convention for it: wherever you see LORD in small capitals, the Hebrew underneath is the covenant name. Wherever you see plain God, it is elohim. Scholars call Psalms 42–83 the Elohistic Psalter for its habit of preferring the second.

⚠️ One thing to clear up first, because it causes real confusion

In the Church we use these two names in a particular way. Following the First Presidency's 1916 doctrinal exposition The Father and the Son, we use Elohim as a title for God the Father and Jehovah for Jesus Christ in His premortal role. That usage is deliberate, it is authorized, and it is genuinely useful for teaching — it lets us speak precisely about the Father and the Son.

The Hebrew Bible does not use the names that way, and knowing that will save you confusion this week. In the Old Testament both names are used of the same Being, constantly, and often in the same breath:

  • Genesis 2:4 and 2:7 — what your Bible prints as "the LORD God" is, in Hebrew, YHWH ʾelōhîm (יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים). Both names, one being, side by side.
  • Psalm 50:1, in this week's reading — "The mighty God, even the LORD, hath spoken" is ʾĒl ʾelōhîm YHWH (אֵל אֱלֹהִים יְהוָה). Three names for God stacked in a single line.

So when you notice this week's psalms alternating between "God" and "LORD," that is not the Father and the Son alternating. It is one God, addressed by two names — the way you might call the same person by their title in one sentence and their given name in the next.

Why do the names cluster the way they do, then? Nobody is certain, and it is worth knowing what scholars have proposed — partly because you will meet these ideas, and partly because knowing the shape of the argument keeps it from sounding alarming when you do.

The leading academic explanations, none of which the text itself states:

⚠️ And here is the part that matters most

None of this touches what the Church teaches, and it is important to see why.

Every one of these proposals is about how the book was compiled and copied — who gathered which psalms, and which word they wrote. Not one of them is a claim about who God is. The Being addressed in Psalm 51 and the Being addressed in Psalm 70 are the same Lord, prayed to by the same people, in the same book.

Scripture is not made less true by having a history. We already believe our own scriptures came through compilers — Mormon abridged plates, Joseph Smith translated and revised, the Doctrine and Covenants was edited between editions. A book with an editorial history is what revelation looks like when it reaches us through people, and the Book of Mormon says so about itself: "if there are faults they are the mistakes of men" (Title Page).

So take the scholarship as what it is — careful people trying to explain a real pattern in a real text — and hold it loosely, because the pattern is certain and the explanations are not. What the psalms are doing is not in doubt. Somebody is praying, and God is listening. What can be observed is that certain collections of psalms strongly prefer one name, which is what the rest of this section is about. That is a fact about how the book was gathered and copied. It is not a statement about who is being addressed — the same Lord is prayed to throughout.

The proof is one poem preserved twice

Psalm 70 is a near-duplicate of Psalm 40:13–17. Five verses, matching one to one. You read Psalm 40 last week; you read Psalm 70 this week. Psalm 40 sits outside the Elohistic block. Psalm 70 sits inside it. Watch what happens to the divine name:

Psalm 40:13 — "Be pleased, O LORD, to deliver me: O LORD, make haste to help me."

Psalm 70:1 — "Make haste, O God, to deliver me; make haste to help me, O LORD."

Same prayer. Same words, nearly. Different name for God in the opening line.

This is one of the few places in the Bible where you can see an editor's hand without knowing a word of Hebrew. Somebody gathered these psalms into a collection, and that collection had a habit. When the same poem was preserved in two collections, it kept each collection's habit.

Why it matters for reading — and what is not being said. Nothing here means the text was corrupted, and no different God is being addressed: elohim and the covenant name both name the same Lord. What the two copies of one prayer actually show is care. The Psalter — the whole book of Psalms — is not a pile of poems but a book assembled from earlier books, a library bound into one volume with the seams still showing, and when the same poem was preserved in two collections, each kept it in its own accustomed words. Somebody treasured this prayer enough to keep it twice, for two communities. That is the same kind of care that put a Bible on your shelf.

(It also explains something small: Psalm 70 is only five verses, lifted out of a longer psalm. A short poem standing alone has to open and close itself, and Psalm 70 does — which is why it feels tighter than its parent.)

Korah led the rebellion against Moses in Numbers 16 and was swallowed by the earth — but Numbers 26:11 adds, almost in passing, "Notwithstanding the children of Korah died not." And Psalm 71 has no heading at all. In Hebrew manuscripts it follows Psalm 70 with no break, and many readers have taken the two together — a short cry for help, then a long prayer of old age. Read that way, "make haste to help me" is answered by "cast me not off in the time of old age."

The little lines above the psalms are not later decoration. In the Hebrew Bible they are verse 1 — part of the text, counted and preserved. That is why Hebrew and English verse numbers differ in most of this week’s psalms — usually by one, and by two where the heading runs long. Psalm 51’s heading is two Hebrew verses, so "Create in me a clean heart" is verse 10 in your Bible and verse 12 in a Hebrew one.

They carry three kinds of information.

A historical setting. Two this week are specific enough to date:

  • Psalm 51 — "when Nathan the prophet came unto him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba." This is the most precisely situated psalm in the Psalter. It belongs to 2 Samuel 12, in the hours after a prophet said "Thou art the man."
  • Psalm 63 — "when he was in the wilderness of Judah." Written thirsty, and it shows: "my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land."

A musical direction. Several name a tune or an instrument, and nobody today is certain what most of them mean:

  • Neginah (נְגִינָה) (Psalm 61) — a stringed instrument, or music for strings.
  • Shoshannim (שׁוֹשַׁנִּים) (Psalm 69) — literally "lilies," almost certainly the name of a melody, the way a hymn tune has a name of its own.
  • Maschil (מַשְׂכִּיל) (Psalm 78) — a term of instruction or skill; thirteen psalms carry it.
  • "To the chief Musician" — heads eleven of this week's seventeen. These were performance copies.

A dedication or a guild. "For the sons of Korah," "of Asaph," "to Jeduthun."

A caution worth keeping. The Hebrew preposition in these headings — le- — can mean by, for, belonging to, or dedicated to. "A Psalm of David" may mean David wrote it, or that it belongs to a Davidic collection. The tradition has read it both ways for a very long time, and the psalms do not stop being scripture either way.

Psalm 72 is the odd one out this week, and it is placed deliberately. It is a royal psalm — a prayer for the king — and it closes Book Two of the Psalter with the line "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended" (72:20).

Its petitions are almost entirely about how the king treats the powerless:

"He shall judge the poor of the people, he shall save the children of the needy… For he shall deliver the needy when he crieth; the poor also, and him that hath no helper." — Psalm 72:4, 12

A king was measured, in Israel, by what happened to the people who could not defend themselves. That is the standard the psalm asks God to enforce.

And its language outgrew any actual king. "His name shall endure for ever… all nations shall call him blessed" (72:17) was more than Solomon or any son of David delivered. Both Jewish and Christian readers came to hear it as a prayer for a king still to come — which is why this psalm sits behind so much of how the New Testament speaks of Christ's reign.

This week's psalms are not arranged chronologically, but several can be placed:

  • Psalm 51 — Jerusalem, after Bathsheba. The palace, not the battlefield.
  • Psalm 63 — the wilderness of Judah, David a fugitive.
  • Psalm 71 — old age. "Forsake me not when my strength faileth."
  • Psalm 78 — a recital of the exodus, the wilderness and the settlement, aimed at children: "that the generation to come might know them."
  • Psalm 85 — "thou hast brought back the captivity of Jacob." A community returned from exile, asking to be revived again.

Taken together the week spans a whole life and a whole nation's memory: a young man on the run, a king caught in his worst hour, an old man afraid of being discarded, and a people teaching their children what God did before any of them were born.

3. Week 35: Key Passages Study

The Text

10 Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me.

17 The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.

How the Poem Works

Psalm 51 is the most precisely situated poem in the Psalter (another name for the whole book of Psalms). Its heading places it "when Nathan the prophet came unto him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba" — 2 Samuel 12, in the hours after a prophet said "Thou art the man."

The verb in verse 10 is the point. "Create" is bārāʾ (בָּרָא) — the Genesis verb, the making-word the Old Testament reserves for God alone. People make, form and build; only God creates. (The full study of the word, and what happened to it in Greek, is Word Studies, study 1.) A king could restore a reputation, compensate a family, order a cover-up — David had already tried the last of those. What he asks for instead is the one act no earthly power can arrange: he asks God to do to his heart what God once did to the unorganized world.

Verse 17 answers a problem the psalm raised. Two verses earlier David has said that God does not desire sacrifice — "else would I give it" (v. 16). This is not a rejection of the temple system. It is a recognition that his particular sin had no sacrifice appointed for it. The Mosaic law provides offerings for sins of ignorance and inadvertence; for deliberate adultery and arranged murder it provides none. David is a man who has run out of prescribed remedies.

So he offers the only thing left: "a broken and a contrite heart." Not as a lesser substitute, but as what God wanted underneath the offerings all along.

Notice the direction of the whole psalm. It never asks for consequences to be lifted. Bathsheba's husband stays dead; the child dies; the sword never departs from David's house. What he asks for is "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation" (v. 12) — not the throne, not his reputation, not even peace. The joy.

Latter-day Saint Connections

  • "A broken heart and a contrite spirit" is the sacrifice the Savior names in 3 Nephi 9:20 as replacing the offering of blood: "ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit." Psalm 51:17 is where that language begins.
  • Alma 5:14 asks, "have ye spiritually been born of God? Have ye received his image in your countenances?" — the same conviction that the change needed is creation, not correction.
  • Elder Bruce D. Porter of the Seventy built an entire conference address on this verse, "A Broken Heart and a Contrite Spirit" (October 2007). His central claim reframes what the phrase means: "Christ's example teaches us that a broken heart is an eternal attribute of godliness." Not a temporary state to pass through on the way to forgiveness — an attribute of God Himself. He goes further: "Those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit are willing to do anything and everything that God asks of them, without resistance or resentment." (churchofjesuschrist.org)
  • The manual's theme for this week is "Because of the Savior's mercy, I can be forgiven." Psalm 51 is the Old Testament's fullest statement of that mercy, prayed by a man with no claim on it.

  2. Psalm 51 asks for restored joy, not restored circumstances. What does that suggest about what repentance actually returns to us?
  3. What does it mean that the sin David committed had no sacrifice appointed for it?

How the Poem Works

One small Hebrew word opens six verses of this psalm, and an English reader cannot see it.

The word is ak (אַךְ) — a restrictive particle meaning only, surely, nothing but. It stands at the head of verses 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 9. The King James translators, doing their job well, rendered it by sense rather than by rote, so it comes into English six different ways:

The word alternates, and the alternation is the argument. Only is granted to God twice — He only is my rock — and turned against men twice. Between them the psalmist twice steadies himself: my soul, wait thou only. By verse 9 the verdict is in: weighed in the balance, men are "altogether lighter than vanity."

The psalm is not merely saying "trust God." It is saying that the word only belongs to God, and that every rival claim to it collapses when weighed.

Verses 2 and 6 are nearly identical — but not quite. Verse 2 ends "I shall not be greatly moved." Verse 6 drops the qualifier: "I shall not be moved." Between them the psalmist has thought it through, and the hedge is gone. A quiet, honest picture of what happens when someone talks themselves from mostly trusting to trusting.

How the Poem Works

After Psalm 22, Psalm 69 is the psalm the New Testament reaches for most often. It is a lament of a man drowning — not metaphorically distant, but with the specific physical detail of someone who has been crying so long his throat has failed.

One word runs through the psalm like a thread: "reproach" — public shaming, humiliation with an audience. And the psalm's complaint is that it comes because of devotion: "because for thy sake I have borne reproach" (v. 7). The speaker is not suffering despite his faithfulness; he is suffering for it. (The word itself, and where Paul takes it, is Word Studies, study 6.)

Verse 21 is the verse that reaches Golgotha. "They gave me also gall for my meat; and in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink." Matthew records that at the crucifixion "they gave him vinegar to drink mingled with gall" (Matthew 27:34). Be careful how this is described, because it is easy to get wrong. At Golgotha nobody quotes Psalm 69. Matthew simply records what happened, and a reader who knows the psalm hears it. In John, the Savior speaks — "I thirst" — and John tells us He speaks in order to fulfil scripture. But He does not quote the psalm's words. The whole of that sentence is carried by one syllable.

How the Poem Works

Psalm 71 is the only psalm this week with no header line at all — no author, no occasion, no tune. (Those header lines, called superscriptions, are explained in Historical & Cultural Context, section 3.) In Hebrew manuscripts it follows Psalm 70 without a break, and many readers have taken the two together: a short cry, "make haste to help me," answered by a long prayer about growing old.

The psalm opens and closes on the same two things. Verse 1 asks "let me never be put to confusion"; verse 24 reports that the psalmist's adversaries "are confounded… brought unto shame." The same Hebrew root, and the subject has flipped: he asked not to be shamed, and by the end it is his enemies who are. Between those two points, "thy righteousness" appears five times — verses 2, 15, 16, 19 and 24. The psalm is framed by the thing it holds onto.

What it actually asks for in verse 18 is the surprise. An old man afraid of being discarded does not pray for comfort, health or ease. He prays for time — "until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come." He wants to finish telling.

That reframes what a long life is for. The psalm treats old age not as a season of being cared for but as a season with work still in it, and the work is testimony.

How the Poem Works

Psalm 78 is the second-longest psalm in the Psalter, and it is a history lesson aimed at children. Asaph retells the exodus, the wilderness, the wandering and the settlement — and he does not tidy it up. The psalm is largely a catalogue of Israel forgetting, complaining, testing God and turning back.

The stated purpose is in verse 7: so that the next generation "might set their hope in God, and not forget." The psalm is honest about failure precisely because its aim is that the failures not be repeated.

A word runs through the middle of it. ʾAph (אַף) — "anger," and literally the nostril, the flaring of the nose — appears five times (vv. 21, 31, 38, 49, 50). It is the drumbeat of the psalm's account of the wilderness.

And verse 38 is where the drumbeat breaks. "But he, being full of compassion, forgave their iniquity, and destroyed them not: yea, many a time turned he his anger away." The repetition sets up the mercy. You feel the weight of the anger four times so that the turning of it lands.

On verse 2 — read the attribution carefully. Matthew quotes this verse to explain why Jesus taught in parables: "That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet" (Matthew 13:35). That is Matthew's own fulfilment formula, written about the Savior's teaching — not a quotation by Him. Asaph, writing centuries earlier about recounting Israel's history, supplied Matthew the sentence for what the Lord was doing on the shore of Galilee.

Psalm 51:10 (Hebrew 51:12) — "Create in me a clean heart, O God"

לֵב טָהוֹר בְּרָא־לִי אֱלֹהִים

This is the single most important word in the week's reading, and its force is invisible in English because "create" is an ordinary English word and bārāʾ is not an ordinary Hebrew one.

It is the verb of Genesis 1:1 — "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." Across the whole Old Testament, its subject is always God. People make (ʿāśâ, עָשָׂה), form (yāṣar, יָצַר), build (bānâ, בָּנָה) — and those verbs take human subjects constantly. Bārāʾ does not. It names the kind of making only God does.

So David does not ask to be improved, mended, or helped to do better. He asks for the Genesis verb, and he asks for it to be applied to his heart. What he wants is not restoration but origination — something that was not there before.

And the Greek confirms the reading. Where the Hebrew has bārāʾ, the Septuagint translators chose κτίσον (ktison (κτίσον)), from ktizō (κτίζω) — the same verb the New Testament uses of God's creating work, as in "created in Christ Jesus unto good works" (Ephesians 2:10). They heard Genesis in this verse too.

Psalm 51:10 (Hebrew 51:12) — "Create in me a clean heart"

לֵב טָהוֹר

Ṭāhôr is not a word about tidiness. It is the vocabulary of the sanctuary — the word used for what may approach God and what may not. A vessel is ṭāhôr or it cannot be used in the temple. A person is ṭāhôr or cannot enter.

That is what makes David's request so exact. He is not asking to feel better about himself. He is asking to be made fit to approach — restored to the category of things that may come near. Two verses later he prays "Cast me not away from thy presence" (51:11), which is the same request stated as a fear.

Paired with lēb (לֵב), "heart." In Hebrew the heart is not the seat of emotion — that is more often the kidneys or the bowels. The lēb (לֵב) is the seat of thought, will and decision: the place where a person deliberates and chooses. So "a clean heart" is not primarily about feelings. It is about a will that is fit to stand in God's presence.

Psalm 62:2, 6 (Hebrew 62:3, 7) — "He only is my rock and my salvation"

אַךְ־הוּא צוּרִי

Hebrew has two words for rock and they are not interchangeable. Selaʿ (סֶלַע) is a crag — a cliff you hide in, a cleft, somewhere to shelter. Ṣûr is a massif: bedrock, the immovable thing you stand on and build on.

Psalm 62 uses ṣûr, and the choice matches what the psalm says next: "I shall not be moved." This is not the rock you hide behind. It is the rock you are founded on.

A genuine curiosity worth telling a class. The Septuagint does not translate it as "rock" at all. Where the Hebrew says "he is my rock," the Greek says he is "my God and my saviour, my helper" (ἀντιλήμπτωρ, antilēmptōr (ἀντιλήμπτωρ), "one who takes hold in your place"). The translators seem to have avoided calling God a rock in Greek — perhaps wary of how that would sound to readers surrounded by stone gods. Something real is lost, and something real is gained: the Greek names the action, the Hebrew names the foundation.

Psalm 49:7 (Hebrew 49:8) — "None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him"

Both words come from the marketplace, and that is the point.

Pādâ means to buy a person out — the verb used when a slave is bought free or a firstborn is redeemed by payment. Kōp̄er is the price paid to release a life that is otherwise forfeit; in Exodus 21:30 it is the sum an owner pays when his ox has killed a man.

Psalm 49 uses the commercial vocabulary in order to declare the market closed. However rich you are, there is no sum. The Hebrew doubles the verb for emphasis — lōʾ pādōh yipdeh, "he cannot at all redeem" — which the King James renders "can by any means."

And here the Septuagint hands the New Testament its vocabulary. Pādâ becomes λυτρόω (lytroō (λυτρόω)), and the noun λύτρον (lytron (λύτρον)) is the ransom-price. That is the exact word on the Savior's lips: "to give his life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Psalm 49 says no man can pay it. The Gospel says one did.

5. Week 35: Jewish Perspective

The passages below come from midrash (מִדְרָשׁ) — the body of rabbinic commentary on scripture compiled between roughly the third and twelfth centuries. Midrash is not a verse-by-verse commentary in the modern sense. It works by association: a rabbi reads one verse, hears another verse answering it, and sets the two side by side to see what happens.

None of this is offered as a practice to adopt, and none of it is Latter-day Saint doctrine. It is here because these are the readers who lived closest to this text, in its own language, for the longest time — and because they noticed things worth noticing.

How a rabbinic citation works. "Numbers Rabbah 13:6" means the collection (Numbers Rabbah, midrash on the book of Numbers), then the section, then the paragraph. Every citation below links to Sefaria, a free digital library of Jewish texts.

⚠️ A verse-numbering warning you will need. In Hebrew Bibles the psalm's superscription — the little line naming its author or tune — is counted as verse 1. So Hebrew and English verse numbers differ, usually by one and sometimes by two. Where a rabbinic source cites "Psalms 51:19," your Bible reads that at 51:17. Both numbers are given below so you can find the verse.

Psalm 51's most quoted line is "a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise" (51:17 English; 51:19 Hebrew).

Ecclesiastes Rabbah collects it into something larger. Asking what the word heart does across scripture, the midrash builds a list — the heart hardens, softens, is saddened, fears, grows conceited, becomes rebellious — and cites a verse for each. Psalm 51 supplies the entry for breaking:

"The heart breaks, as it is stated: 'A broken and contrite heart' (Psalms 51:19)." — Ecclesiastes Rabbah 1

What the list quietly argues is that a breaking heart is not a malfunction. It sits in the same catalogue as a heart that fears, rejoices or grows proud — one of the ordinary things a heart does. Scripture has a verse for it because it happens.

And in David's case it is the only thing he has left to offer. Two verses earlier he has said that God does not desire sacrifice — "else would I give it" (51:16). He is not downgrading the altar; he is a man whose particular sin had no offering appointed for it.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The Savior makes exactly this exchange explicit in 3 Nephi 9:20: "ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit." The rabbis were reading Psalm 51 as a text about what the heart does; the Book of Mormon reads it as the sacrifice that replaces the sacrifices. Both are hearing the same verse. It may comfort someone in a hard season to know that the breaking of a heart is listed in scripture among the things hearts do — not as failure, but as the beginning of an offering.

Readers often find the last two verses of Psalm 51 jarring. After the most personal prayer in the Psalter — create in me a clean heart — it turns abruptly outward:

"Do good in thy good pleasure unto Zion: build thou the walls of Jerusalem. Then shalt thou be pleased with the sacrifices of righteousness… then shall they offer bullocks upon thine altar." (51:18–19 English; 51:20–21 Hebrew)

Numbers Rabbah reads those verses through the wilderness. Commenting on the small offerings brought at the dedication of the tabernacle — "one silver dish… one ladle… one bull" — the midrash puts a speech in Israel's mouth:

"Master of the universe, we are located in the wilderness, and in accordance with the conditions in the wilderness, we have presented before You. When You enter Your palace, you will see how many offerings and how many bulls we will sacrifice before you." — Numbers Rabbah 13

Then the midrash quotes Psalm 51:20–21 as the fulfilment of that promise, and points at Solomon's dedication, where the offering was no longer one bull but twenty-two thousand.

Read that way, the psalm's ending is not a change of subject. David is a man in his own wilderness, with nothing adequate to bring. What he offers now is a broken heart, because it is the wilderness and that is what he has. The last verses are him saying: and when this is over, there will be more.

Latter-day Saint Connection

This is the pattern of a repentant person promising future service they cannot yet render — Alma the Younger "labouring without ceasing" after his three days, Paul preaching the faith he once destroyed. It also sits close to how we speak of consecration: we bring what we have now, in the circumstances we are actually in, and commit the rest. A convert who cannot yet pay a full tithe, a member returning after years away — Psalm 51 ends by saying the wilderness offering is accepted and that it is not the last word.

Psalm 63 opens: "O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is" (63:1 English; 63:2 Hebrew). Its heading places David "in the wilderness of Judah."

Genesis Rabbah takes that verse and reads it into an entirely different scene — Jacob at Bethel, asleep on a stone with the ladder above him:

Rabbi Yosei bar Zimra began: "My flesh yearns for You, in a parched and thirsty land without water" (Psalms 63:2 [63:1 in your Bible]). — Genesis Rabbah 69

This is midrash doing what midrash does. Jacob is a fugitive sleeping rough, far from home, with nothing; David is a fugitive in the wilderness of Judah with nothing. The rabbi hears one in the other, and opens the Jacob story with David's line.

The move is worth learning from. It reads a psalm not as a diary entry belonging to one man but as language given to anyone in that position. That is, in fact, how the Psalter has always been used.

Latter-day Saint Connection

We do the same thing whenever a hymn written for someone else's grief becomes ours. The Prophet Joseph in Liberty Jail reaching for scripture written centuries before him — "O God, where art thou?" — is this exact motion. The Psalms are the church's loaned language: written by one person in one wilderness, and handed to everyone who ends up in a wilderness of their own.

Psalm 78 is Asaph's long retelling of the exodus and the wilderness, and it says plainly why: "that the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born" (78:6).

The rabbis treat this psalm as a source of historical detail, not merely of praise. In Tanchuma, discussing what happened at the sea, the midrash reaches for Psalm 78:9 — "The children of Ephraim, being armed, and carrying bows, turned back in the day of battle" — and reads it alongside Psalm 140 to reconstruct the day itself (Tanchuma, Shoftim).

The instinct there is that a psalm can be evidence. Asaph is not just praising; he is reporting, and the report can be cross-examined against other passages.

That fits the psalm's own claim about itself. It opens "I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old" (78:2) — the word for parable, māshāl (מָשָׁל), meaning something closer to instruction by comparison than fiction.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The manual's teaching theme for children this week is "I will remember the works of the Lord." Psalm 78 is the Old Testament's fullest instruction on how that remembering is transmitted: deliberately, out loud, from one generation to the next, and without editing out the failures. Most of Psalm 78 is Israel behaving badly. Asaph tells it anyway, because a sanitised history teaches nothing.

The Book of Mormon works the same way — Nephi records his brothers' rebellions, Mormon records his own people's destruction. "Remember, remember" is the refrain of a people who knew exactly how easy forgetting is.

One thing worth understanding about how these psalms have been used: in Jewish practice the Psalter is not primarily a book to study. It is a book to say.

Psalms are recited daily in the siddur (סִדּוּר) — the Jewish prayer book — and in many communities the entire Psalter is read through on a schedule. Psalm 51's "O Lord, open thou my lips; and my mouth shall shew forth thy praise" (51:15) is said before the Amidah (עֲמִידָה), the central standing prayer, every single day. A verse David prayed after the worst act of his life became the sentence with which an entire people opens its mouth to pray.

Latter-day Saint Connection

We have something close to this in the sacrament prayers — fixed words, said the same way each week, that do not become less sincere for being repeated. The instinct that a borrowed sentence can carry a personal prayer is one of the oldest instincts in worship. And Psalm 51:15 is a good place to notice it: the line is not asking for eloquence. It is asking for the ability to speak at all — which is exactly where a person stands after the kind of failure that psalm was written in.

The first scholars of the Bible were called soferim (סוֹפְרִים) — literally "the counters" — because they counted. Not skimmed, not summarised: counted, letter by letter, to find the exact middle of a book. The Talmud preserves their results, and one of them is worth a Latter-day Saint reader's attention this week.

The verse: "But He, being full of compassion, forgives iniquity" (Psalms 78:38), is the midpoint of verses in the book of Psalms. — Kiddushin 30a

Psalm 78:38 is in this week's reading. It sits in the middle of Asaph's long, unsparing history of a nation that kept forgetting — seventy-two verses of failure — and it stops to say that mercy kept interrupting.

Two honest notes, because a reader may go and count. Our Bibles do not divide the verses the way theirs did, so counting a King James Psalter lands a little further on. And the Talmud says so itself, on the same page: when the Sages proposed settling a similar question by fetching a scroll and counting, Rav Yosef answered, "We are not experts about verses either" — Israel and Babylonia already divided some verses differently.

So this is not arithmetic to check. It is a claim about what belongs at the centre, made by men who had read the book more carefully than we have. They went looking for the heart of the book of praises, and they said it was the verse where God forgives and does not destroy.

LDS Connection

We do the same thing without counting. Ask a Latter-day Saint for the centre of the Book of Mormon and you will not get a verse number — you will get "O Jesus, thou Son of God, have mercy on me" (Alma 36:18), the turn at the middle of Alma's chiasmus, which is also a cry for mercy at a structural centre. Two traditions, two ways of reading, and both put the same thing in the middle. Whatever the counting, that instinct is sound: mercy is not an episode in the story of God and His people. It is the hinge the whole thing turns on.

6. Week 35: Teaching Applications

Seven settings, and you are not meant to use all of them. Each takes one idea from the week and does something with it. Nothing here needs preparation beyond the study guide itself — no handouts, no slides, and in most cases nothing you have to buy. Pick the one that matches who is in front of you.

The word you cannot see. Read Psalm 62:1–9 together and have someone circle every place the King James says Truly, only, or Surely. There are six, and in Hebrew they are all the same word. Then ask what changes when you notice that: who gets the word only, and who has it taken away from them?

For a family with young children, the manual's theme does the work: Jesus Christ is my rock. Take them somewhere with actual bedrock — a boulder, a stone wall, a cliff face — and let them push on it. Then ask what Psalm 62 means by "I shall not be moved."

A quieter option for a hard week. Psalm 69:3 says "I am weary of my crying: my throat is dried." That is not despair, it is exhaustion — and it is in scripture. Some families need a night when the lesson is simply: the Psalms have words for this, and you are allowed to use them.

Open with the doublet. Put Psalm 40:13–17 and Psalm 70 side by side on the board — the class read Psalm 40 last week. Let them find the differences themselves before you explain anything. When someone notices that "LORD" became "God," you can tell them why: they have just found an editor's fingerprint, and Psalms 42–83 has that habit throughout.

Then the question that carries the hour: what does it mean that the same prayer was worth keeping twice?

A second route, if the class is ready for it. Psalm 51:16 says God does not desire sacrifice — "else would I give it." Ask why David says that when the law he lived under prescribed sacrifices constantly. The answer is in the situation: for what he had done, the law appointed no offering. He had run out of prescribed remedies, and Psalm 51 is what a person brings when the ordinary means are gone.

Teach "Jesus Christ is my rock" with two rocks. Bring a small crumbly stone and something solid. Build a tower on each. Psalm 62 says "He only is my rock… I shall not be moved."

Teach Psalm 78 with a family story. The psalm says the works of God only reach the next generation if somebody tells them: "that the generation to come might know them" (78:6). Have each child ask a parent or grandparent for one story about God helping their family — then bring it back and tell it. That is Psalm 78, done rather than discussed.

Keep Psalm 51 simple. Create in me a clean heart — God can make our hearts new, the way He made the world. Children understand "make new" better than "forgive," and it is closer to the Hebrew.

The honest psalm. Psalm 77 asks, out loud and in scripture, "Hath God forgotten to be gracious?" (77:9). Youth often assume that questions like that are evidence of weak faith. Show them the question is in the Bible, printed in the Psalms — and then show them Asaph's answer, which is not an argument but a decision: "I will remember the works of the LORD" (77:11).

Psalm 51 without flinching. David's sin is adultery and arranged murder. Youth already know that; sanding it down costs credibility. The point worth making is what he asks for — not to be let off, not for consequences to lift, but "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation." Repentance restores the joy, not the circumstances.

A discussion question that usually goes somewhere: Psalm 62 says everything except God, weighed in the balance, is "lighter than vanity." What in your life have you given the word only to?

Why these psalms say "God" more than "LORD." Start with what the class can see for themselves: the King James Bible uses LORD in small capitals for God's personal covenant name, and plain God for the ordinary Hebrew word for deity. Then give them the counts — 93 to 13 inside Psalms 42–83, roughly even outside — and the pair of Psalm 70 and Psalm 40:13–17, the same poem preserved twice with the names swapped. Let them work out what it implies about how the book of Psalms was gathered. Scholars call that preferring block the Elohistic Psalter, from elohim, the ordinary word for God; the term is worth giving them once they have seen the thing it names. This is a chance to show that noticing the hand of those who collected and copied scripture is not a threat to it — it is evidence of the care with which it was kept.

Psalm 71, and how not to over-read a pattern. The psalm ends where it began. Have the class read only its opening and its closing line:

71:1–2 — "let me never be put to confusion… Deliver me in thy righteousness"

71:24 — "My tongue also shall talk of thy righteousness… they are confounded… that seek my hurt"

The same two ideas, shame and God's righteousness, open the psalm and close it — and in between, "thy righteousness" recurs five times (vv. 2, 15, 16, 19, 24). Poets call a poem that closes the circle it opened a ring.

Then use it to teach restraint, which is the more valuable lesson. It is tempting to call this a chiasmus — the mirror pattern (A-B-C-B-A) that Latter-day Saints know from Alma 36, where the turning point sits at the exact centre. This is not that. Psalm 71's high point is verses 18–19, near the end rather than in the middle, and its repeated terms do not mirror inward in order. It is a ring, not a chiasm. The difference matters, because a real pattern given the wrong name trains a reader to see patterns that are not there.

Psalm 49 and the ransom. Psalm 49:7–8 says no one can buy a brother back from death: the price is real and nobody has it. Trace the vocabulary — Hebrew pādâ (to buy a person out of bondage) becomes Greek lytron (λύτρον, the ransom-price) when the psalm is translated, and lytron is the exact word the Savior uses of Himself: "to give his life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Psalm 49 states the debt and says it cannot be paid. The Gospel names who paid it.

Psalm 51 is the repentance lesson. It contains no theory. It is a man in the worst hour of his life asking God to create a clean heart in him — using bārāʾ (בָּרָא), the verb of Genesis 1:1, which in the Old Testament never has a subject other than God. He does not ask to be improved. He asks for the one act only God performs. Anyone who will teach repentance should know what David actually asks for, and what he pointedly does not.

Psalm 66:16 is a teaching model: "Come and hear, all ye that fear God, and I will declare what he hath done for my soul." Not what he believes, not what he has concluded — what has happened to him. That is the shape of a testimony that travels.

Psalm 78 is the argument for teaching at all. The works of God reach the next generation only because someone deliberately says them out loud — and the psalm includes the failures. A missionary who has learned that from Asaph will not be thrown by teaching people whose history is messy.

Read Psalm 62 six times, once for each only. Ask each time who the word is being given to.

Sit with Psalm 71 if you are past fifty, and notice what it asks for. Not comfort, not health, not rest: "until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come" (71:18). It asks for time to finish telling. That is a question worth answering personally — what have you not yet finished telling?

Try Psalm 51:15 as a way to begin prayer: "O Lord, open thou my lips; and my mouth shall shew forth thy praise." Jewish practice has opened its central daily prayer with that line for many centuries. It does not ask for eloquence. It asks for the ability to speak at all — which is exactly where a person stands after the kind of failure that psalm came out of.

And if this is a hard season: Psalm 69 and Psalm 77 are both in scripture, both unresolved for most of their length, and both honest about fatigue. You are not required to arrive at the last verse today.

7. Week 35: Study Questions

Seven questions. They are meant to be sat with rather than answered quickly, and several have no settled answer.

1. Why does David ask God to create?

Psalm 51:10 uses bārāʾ (בָּרָא) — the verb of Genesis 1:1, whose subject in the Old Testament is always God.

What is the difference between asking God to fix something and asking Him to make something that was not there before? And why might a king — a man who could restore a reputation, compensate a family, command almost anything — ask for the second?

2. What does it mean that his sin had no offering appointed for it?

The law of Moses prescribed offerings for sins of ignorance and weakness; for what David had done, it prescribed none. That is why he says "thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it" (51:16).

What does a person bring when the ordinary remedies do not reach? And what does it say that the answer — "a broken and a contrite heart" — turned out to be what God wanted underneath the offerings all along?

3. Psalm 51 asks for restored joy, not restored circumstances. Why?

Bathsheba's husband stays dead. The child dies. The sword never departs from David's house. He asks for none of that to be undone. What he asks for is "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation" (51:12).

What does that suggest repentance actually returns to us? And is that a smaller thing than we usually hope for, or a larger one?

4. What have you leaned on that did not hold your weight?

Psalm 62 spends six verses on one small word. Twice it is granted to God — He only is my rock. Twice it is turned against men. By verse 9 everything else, weighed in the balance, is "lighter than vanity."

What in your life currently carries the weight that word implies? And notice that verse 2 says "I shall not be greatly moved" while verse 6 drops the qualifier — what happens to a person between those two verses?

5. Is it faithful to ask "Hath God forgotten to be gracious?"

Asaph asks it in Psalm 77:9, out loud, in scripture. He does not answer it with an argument. He answers it with a decision: "I will remember the works of the LORD" (77:11).

What is the difference between a question that damages faith and a question that scripture models? And what does it mean that remembering — not reasoning — is what Asaph reaches for?

6. What have you not yet finished telling?

Psalm 71 is the prayer of an old man afraid of being discarded. What he asks for is not comfort or relief but time: "until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come" (71:18).

If old age has work still in it, what is the work? And who, in your own life, taught you "from your youth" in the way verse 17 describes?

7. Why does a psalm meant to build faith in children spend most of its length on failure?

Psalm 78 exists so "that the generation to come might know" (78:6) — and most of its seventy-two verses recount Israel forgetting, complaining, testing God and turning back. Only at verse 38 does the drumbeat break: "But he, being full of compassion, forgave their iniquity."

What is lost when a family or a people tells only the tidy version of its history? And why might the mercy in verse 38 land harder because four accounts of anger came first?

A last one, if you want it. Psalm 85:10 says "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other." Those pairs are usually in tension — mercy against truth, righteousness against peace. Where have you seen them meet, and what did it cost?
