First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week the Psalter opened, and it was lovely. Blessed is the man. The heavens declare the glory of God. The Lord is my shepherd. If you had only ever read Psalms 1 through 46, you might come away thinking this is a book of praise with some sadness in it.

This week the floor gives out.

"Save me, O God; for the waters are come in unto my soul. I sink in deep mire, where there is no standing." That is Psalm 69, and it does not resolve for nearly thirty verses. "Will the Lord cast off for ever? and will he be favourable no more? Is his mercy clean gone for ever?" That is Psalm 77, and those are not rhetorical questions — the man asking them cannot sleep and cannot speak. And in the middle of it all sits Psalm 51, written by a man who has done something no apology covers.

Here is the thing worth knowing before you start: this is not a detour. Roughly a third of the Psalms are laments, and they are the single largest kind of psalm in the book. The Psalter is called Tehillim — Praises — and yet it spends more time crying than singing. Not because Israel was gloomy, but because the book was assembled by people who knew that most prayer does not begin in gratitude. It begins in trouble, and it has to be allowed to.

So this is the week the book earns its reputation. Anyone can sing Psalm 23. This week asks what you do with the other nights.

A Letter to Fellow Students ▶︎

There is a phrase that runs through any study of this book, and it can stop a Latter-day Saint reader cold: praying a psalm.

We are not raised on memorized prayers. We are taught from Primary to speak to God in our own words, out of our own week — which is one of the loveliest things about how we pray. It also means that taking up somebody else's words can feel like putting on a borrowed coat. If Psalm 51 is David's prayer, in David's crisis, what business do I have praying it?

The Lord answered that question in our own dispensation, and He said it to Emma Smith while she was preparing a hymnbook:

"For my soul delighteth in the song of the heart; yea, the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me, and it shall be answered with a blessing upon their heads." — Doctrine and Covenants 25:12

Read that beside the Psalter and something clicks into place. These are not poems that were later set to music. They are songs — and the Lord has told us plainly what a song of the heart is. It is a prayer. Israel did not memorize the Psalms instead of praying. They sang them, and the singing was the praying.

Which means we have been doing this every Sunday of our lives and calling it something else. Our hymnbook is our Psalter. When a congregation sings "Where Can I Turn for Peace?" it is doing exactly what Psalm 77 does — naming the trouble out loud in God's hearing and refusing to stop asking. "How Firm a Foundation" is Psalm 62's rock. "I Need Thee Every Hour" is Psalm 63's thirst. A Primary child singing "I Am a Child of God" is praying Psalm 8 without knowing the number. Those are our psalms, given to us in our own tongue, and the promise attached to them is the very same promise: it shall be answered with a blessing upon their heads.

Two words in that verse are worth carrying through this whole week. The song is of the heart — not of the voice, and not of the memory. This is open to someone who cannot hold a tune, and closed to no one. And the Lord asks that we sing with understanding, which is to say knowing what we are saying and meaning it. Meet those two, and the blessing is not rationed.

So when you come to "Create in me a clean heart, O God" this week, do not read past it. Say it. It was written down, set to music, and placed in a book precisely so that other people, in their own trouble, could take it up. That is what these words are for. You are not borrowing a coat. You are joining a choir that has been singing for three thousand years, and the Lord has said He delights in the sound.

A note on the superscriptions ▶︎

Several psalms this week carry a header line — "To the chief Musician," "A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet came unto him," "upon Shoshannim." In Hebrew Bibles that line is counted as verse 1, which is why a rabbinic source will cite Psalm 63:2 for what your Bible prints as 63:1. Where the study guide quotes a Jewish source, both numbers are given. It is worth knowing before you go looking something up and land a verse off.

"I Will Take No Bullock Out of Thy House" ▶︎

Before Psalm 51 asks for a clean heart, Psalm 50 explains why nothing else will do — and the two belong together so tightly that reading either one alone costs you half of it.

Psalm 50 opens like a courtroom. God summons heaven and earth as witnesses, gathers His covenant people, and then — this is the surprise — brings a charge against them. Not against the nations. Against the congregation. "Hear, O my people, and I will speak" (50:7).

And the charge is not that they had stopped sacrificing. They had not. The animals were arriving on schedule. The charge is that they had begun to imagine God needed them:

"I will take no bullock out of thy house, nor he goats out of thy folds… If I were hungry, I would not tell thee: for the world is mine, and the fulness thereof." (50:9, 12)

There is something almost amused in that line. The offering was never a transaction. God is not short of cattle, and He was never being fed. The sacrifice was for them — a weekly, bodily, expensive act of turning toward Him, and we tend to love whatever we sacrifice for.

Then comes the verse that names the real problem, and it is the sharpest sentence in the psalm:

"Thou thoughtest that I was altogether such an one as thyself." (50:21)

That is what had gone wrong. They had made God in their own image — a being who could be managed, whose favour could be purchased, who wanted what they wanted and could be paid off in livestock. And a God like that cannot forgive anything, because a God like that is not there.

Now read Psalm 51. Two psalms later, a man with no offering appointed for his sin — the law prescribed no sacrifice for adultery or for murder — says: "For thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it: thou delightest not in burnt offering. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit" (51:16–17). He has heard Psalm 50. He is not trying to pay. He is bringing the only thing God ever actually asked for.

Look at what the Savior said of the same shift, speaking to a people whose temple sacrifices had just ended: "ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (3 Nephi 9:20). Same requirement, both sides of the Atonement. The animals were always pointing at the heart.

The Prayer That Asks for the Wrong Thing ▶︎

Psalm 51 is the center of the week, and its most famous line is the one we say fastest.

"Create in me a clean heart, O God."

Look at the verb. It is the verb from Genesis 1. Bārāʾ — the word used when God created the heavens and the earth, and used in the Hebrew Bible only ever with God as its subject. Nobody else creates in this sense. Human beings shape, build, form, assemble; God alone creates.

Which means David is not asking to be repaired. He has been caught in adultery and in arranging a man's death, and every instinct we would have in his place is to ask for a fix — a second chance, a clean record, an opportunity to make it up. He asks for none of those. He asks to be made new, by the only one who can do it.

That is a strange prayer, and it is strange in a way worth sitting with. Most of our prayers ask God to change the situation. This one says: the situation is me.

Find what is missing. Read the psalm again watching for what David never asks for. He never asks for the consequences to be lifted — and they were not. The child dies. The sword does not depart from his house. What he asks for instead is stranger and better: "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation" (51:12). Not the throne, not the reputation, not even peace. The joy. Repentance here is not a negotiation over outcomes. It is asking back the one thing the sin actually took.

And the psalm comes with a companion phrase we have half-domesticated. "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart." We hear broken and think shattered, ruined, past mending. The Hebrew word is crushed — and here is one way to hold that: a seed has to break open before anything can grow out of it. Broken soil is soil that can finally receive something. A broken heart in Psalm 51 is not a heart that has failed. It is a heart that has stopped defending itself, and is at last able to take something in.

There is one more layer. "Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean." Hyssop is a small, scrubby plant, and if you trace it back through the law of Moses you land in Numbers 19, where you find what it was for: to cleanse someone who had touched a corpse. Not someone dirty — someone who had been in contact with death. David is not asking to be tidied up. He is asking to be brought back from the dead.

"This Is My Infirmity" ▶︎

If Psalm 51 is the week's most famous psalm, Psalm 77 may be its most useful.

It opens badly and gets worse. "I am so troubled that I cannot speak." Then six questions in a row, each one darker than the last: has God forgotten to be gracious? is his mercy clean gone for ever? has he in anger shut up his tender mercies? These are not the words of a man being faithless. They are the words of a man in the dark, saying exactly what the dark says.

And then comes verse 10, one of the most quietly remarkable sentences in scripture:

"And I said, This is my infirmity."

He stops and names it. Not God has abandoned me — the thought that God has abandoned me is a symptom. He is not arguing with the despair, and he is not pretending it isn't there. He is identifying it, the way you might say: this is the fever talking.

Then he does four things, and they are printed right there in verses 10 through 12. Any of us can do them tonight:

  1. Remember the years of the right hand of the Most High — go looking in your own past for the times you knew He was there.
  2. Remember His works — and if you cannot find them in your own life just now, look at what He has done for someone else. He is no respecter of persons.
  3. Meditate on it — not a glance. A stay.
  4. Talk about it — the King James's own word for the fourth verb. Out loud, to someone. Get it out of your own head, which is not a reliable narrator just now.

That is a psalm doing something a sermon cannot. It does not tell a suffering person to feel differently. It hands them a sequence.

And when the psalm turns, look at what it reaches for: not an argument, but a memory of water — "The waters saw thee, O God, the waters saw thee; they were afraid" (77:16). The Red Sea. He is troubled by deep water, so he remembers the night deep water was troubled by God.

Count Them ▶︎

Here is an experiment you can run in ten minutes, in your own Bible.

Open Psalm 62 and read it aloud — but put a small stress on the word only each time it appears. It is there six times: verses 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 9. The Hebrew word is 'ak, a short particle that lands like a drumbeat at the head of the line. The King James translators, wanting good English, moved it around and rendered it three different ways — truly, only, surely — so the drum goes quiet in translation.

Truly my soul waiteth upon God… He only is my rock… They only consult to cast him down… wait thou only upon God… He only is my rock and my salvation… Surely men of low degree are vanity.

Two things surface. The first is obvious once you hear it: the psalm is systematically removing every other place you might put your weight. Not God and savings. Not God and reputation. Only God.

The second is easy to miss, and better. Verse 1 is a statement. Verse 5 is a command. He begins by saying my soul waits upon God — and four verses later he has to tell himself to do it: my soul, wait thou only upon God. Somewhere inside his own psalm he lost it and had to say it again on purpose.

Which is worth knowing, because that is exactly what most of us take for a failure of faith. It is in the text. Trust was not a state he reached in verse 1 and held. He chose it, drifted, and chose it again — inside eight verses.

Whose Voice Is It? ▶︎

Psalm 69 is carried into the New Testament more than almost any other psalm, and reading it after Gethsemane is difficult to do without hearing Him: the deep waters, the reproach that broke his heart, the gall and the vinegar, they that hate me without a cause.

That instinct is right. What the psalm is comes first: a man drowning — "the waters are come in unto my soul" — worn out with weeping, estranged from his own brothers, shamed in public. Those are ordinary human sorrows. They are also the Savior's. When you bring that kind of trouble to God, you are praying words He Himself lived, and there are few things in scripture more comforting than that.

And it deserves one piece of care, because the care protects the comfort.

The New Testament carries this psalm in three different ways. Sometimes He quotes it — "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25). Sometimes someone else quotes it about Him — Paul in Romans 15:3, Peter in Acts 1:20 speaking of Judas. And sometimes the scene simply fulfils it while nobody quotes anything at all: the vinegar at the cross in Matthew 27:34 is the narrator telling us what happened, not a citation.

Of the twelve psalm quotations traced through this week's reading, only two are the Savior's own words — and only one of them comes from Psalm 69.

This is not pedantry, and it is not a reason to hold the psalm at arm's length. It is the opposite. When we say "Jesus said" about something He did not say, we have put words in His mouth — and the words He did say lose a little of their weight, because now they are one item in a long list instead of the one place He reached into this psalm and made it His own. The precision is what keeps the moment sharp.

One more echo, and it is the one that catches people. In the wilderness, Psalm 91 is quoted — "He shall give his angels charge over thee" — and the speaker is Satan. Knowing who is talking changes the whole scene. This is why it is worth asking, every time.

The Prayer of the Second Half of Life ▶︎

Tucked between the great psalms of this week is one that almost nobody quotes, and it may be the one you need most in twenty years.

Psalm 71 is the prayer of someone old. It is one of the few psalms with no header line at all — no author, no occasion, no tune — as though it were left deliberately unsigned so that anyone arriving at that stage of life could pick it up and find their own name missing from it.

And its request is not what you would expect. It does not ask for comfort. It does not ask for health, or for an easy end, or even for the strength to endure. It asks for time:

"Now also when I am old and grayheaded, O God, forsake me not; until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come." (71:18)

The fear in that verse is not dying. The fear is stopping before the telling is done.

Read it and notice how far back he starts: "thou art my trust from my youth… by thee have I been holden up from the womb" (71:5–6). This is a person taking the long view of a whole life with God in it, and concluding that the point of having been carried is to say so out loud while there is still someone to hear.

That connects straight to the psalm that follows two weeks of this reading — Asaph's long history in Psalm 78, which opens by naming the same duty: tell the next generation, "that the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children" (78:6). And it is unsparing about what it hands over. Psalm 78 tells the children the failures too. It does not sand the story down.

Here is something you can do with that. Most of us testify readily about the obvious deliverances — the job that came through, the health that returned, the child who came home. We are far quieter about the slow ones: the repentance that took years, the doubt that resolved into something sturdier, the marriage that was rebuilt. Those are also the works of the Lord, and Psalm 71's petition is for time enough to say so. You do not have to be grayheaded to start.

The Debt and the Receipt ▶︎

Psalm 49 asks a question that a funeral makes unavoidable: what would you pay to keep someone?

The answer is blunt. "None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him: for the redemption of their soul is precious" (49:7–8). Precious there carries the sense of costly — the price is real, and no one has it. Then, in the same psalm, it says who does: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave: for he shall receive me" (49:15).

Hold that open and read to the end of the week. "Great is thy mercy toward me: and thou hast delivered my soul from the lowest hell" (86:13). The same word for the self — nephesh, the whole living person — and the same place it is rescued from. Psalm 49 says no man can pay it and God will. Psalm 86 records it done. And the man saying so is no king or hero; he calls himself "poor and needy" in his very first verse.

They are thirty-seven psalms apart and they are one conversation. The week opens on a debt and closes on a receipt.

Between them sits Psalm 78 — seventy-two verses of a nation that kept forgetting — which stops in the middle of its own catalogue of failure to say: "But he, being full of compassion, forgave their iniquity, and destroyed them not" (78:38). Mercy kept interrupting.

And centuries later the Savior said of Himself that He came "to give his life a ransom for many." In the Greek Bible of His day, that word ransom is the very one Psalm 49's translators had reached for.

The Invitation ▶︎

The Psalms are the book for whatever this week has done to your heart — and this week's psalms are for the parts you would rather not say out loud.

If you are carrying something you cannot undo, Psalm 51 was written by a man in exactly that position, and it does not ask you to feel better first. If you are awake at three in the morning wondering whether God has stopped caring, Psalm 77 has already said it, in scripture, without rebuke — and then hands you four things to do about it. If you are tired of counting on things that keep giving way, read Psalm 62 aloud and count the onlys. If you are in a dry season, Psalm 63 is a man in a wilderness telling God he is thirsty.

There is a detail in this week's reading that I keep returning to. Two of these psalms — the one that opens the week and one that nearly closes it — are headed "for the sons of Korah." Korah was the man who led a rebellion against Moses and was swallowed by the earth. And Numbers 26 adds, almost in passing: "Notwithstanding the children of Korah died not."

The rebel's descendants became the singers in the house of the Lord.

That is what this week is about. Not that the failure did not happen — it did, and the earth opened. But that it did not get the last word about the family, and it does not get the last word about you.

So here is the invitation, and it is the oldest one there is. Take one of these psalms this week and pray it — out loud, in your own voice, meaning it. Sing the hymn that says the same thing if the words come easier that way; the Lord has told us that counts, and that it will be answered with a blessing upon your head. Create in me a clean heart, O God. He is the only one who can, He has said He is willing, and that is the whole reason we still open a book of praises that spends most of its time crying.
