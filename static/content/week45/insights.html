First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week ended in the ruins of Jerusalem. Lamentations opens on an empty city, "How doth the city sit solitary, that was full of people!" (Lamentations 1:1), and yet in the middle of its grief it says, "his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness" (Lamentations 3:22–23).

This week we hear from the other side of that disaster. Ezekiel was one of the people already carried away to Babylon, years before the city fell. A psalm remembers that captivity: "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion" (Psalm 137:1). Ezekiel lived by one of those rivers. His book is what the Lord said to people who were sitting down and weeping.

The reading jumps around (chapters 1–3, then 33–34, 36–37, and 47), but the Overview walks all forty-eight chapters in order, so you can see what happens in between.

God Came to Where They Were ▶︎

The book does not open in Jerusalem. It opens in exile. The Lord does not wait for His people to return to the temple to meet Him there. His glory appears to them "among the captives by the river of Chebar" (Ezekiel 1:1), in a foreign land, far from the temple where most of them believed He was to be found. His prophet's first act matches it: before saying a word, Ezekiel sits among the exiles for seven days (Ezekiel 3:15). The Overview opens with that scene.

If you have ever been in a hard place and assumed God was somewhere else, Ezekiel begins where you are.

"How Should We Then Live?" ▶︎

At the turning point of the book, the exiles finally say what they feel: their sins are upon them, they "pine away in them," and they ask, "how should we then live?" (Ezekiel 33:10).

It is the question of anyone who has made mistakes they cannot undo and has started to believe the mistakes are the whole story. Notice that the Lord does not scold the question. He answers it with an oath, "As I live," and with His own heart: He has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked," and He pleads, "turn ye, turn ye… for why will ye die?" (Ezekiel 33:11).

Then He makes a promise about the past that is easy to miss: when a person turns, "None of his sins that he hath committed shall be mentioned unto him" (Ezekiel 33:16). Modern revelation says the same: "I, the Lord, remember them no more" (Doctrine and Covenants 58:42). Key Passages, passage 2, quotes the verses and reads the chapter closely.

So it is worth asking this week: where have I been pining away, when the Lord is asking me only to turn?

The Shepherd Who Goes Looking ▶︎

Chapter 34 begins with leaders who failed their people. They fed themselves and not the flock, and they did not strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the broken, bring back the driven away, or seek the lost (Ezekiel 34:2–4).

Then the Lord says something remarkable. He does not appoint a better committee. He comes Himself: "I, even I, will both search my sheep" (Ezekiel 34:11). And He takes the failed shepherds' list and promises to do every item Himself: seek the lost, bring back the driven away, bind up the broken, strengthen the sick (Ezekiel 34:16).

The Come, Follow Me manual asks readers to notice the difference between a sheep that is lost and one that is driven away. A lost sheep wandered. A driven-away sheep was pushed. The Lord goes after both. Centuries later the Savior said, "I am the good shepherd, and know my sheep, and am known of mine" (John 10:14). Key Passages, passage 3, quotes the shepherd verses and reads the chapter with John 10.

If you have ever felt rejected, neglected, or abandoned by a person or institution you looked to for guidance or care, this promise offers comfort and hope. Human failure does not change the Savior’s love for His sheep or His knowledge of them.

When the arm of the flesh fails, we can turn to the arm of the Lord. Through His Atonement, He understands our pain personally and completely. He has descended below all things (Doctrine and Covenants 88:6) and knows how to succor His people in their suffering (Alma 7:11–12). He invites us to take His hand and accept His help. We can bring Him our hurt and resentment, asking Him to soften our hearts and help us receive His comfort.

A Heart That Can Be Moved ▶︎

The title of this week comes from Ezekiel 36:26: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh."

A stone does not respond. You can press it, speak to it, or warm it, and it stays the same. Flesh is alive. It feels, heals and changes. In Hebrew the heart is not only where we feel; it is also where we think and choose (Word Studies, study 3). So the promise is not just new feelings. It is a new inner life that can be moved, shaped, and transformed by God.

Earlier in the book the Lord had commanded a new heart (Ezekiel 18:31); here He promises to give one. Key Passages, passage 4, takes up how the two fit together. The Book of Mormon calls the change "a mighty change in us, or in our hearts" (Mosiah 5:2).

"O Lord GOD, Thou Knowest" ▶︎

When the Lord sets Ezekiel down in the valley of dry bones and asks, "Son of man, can these bones live?", Ezekiel does not say yes and he does not say no. He says, "O Lord GOD, thou knowest" (Ezekiel 37:3).

That may be the most useful prayer in the book. There are situations in every life that may sometimes look like that valley: a marriage, a circumstance, a faith, a hope that seems impossible, "very dry" (Ezekiel 37:2). We cannot honestly say we know it will live and be viable. We can honestly give the question back to the One who does know.

Then watch what happens. The bones come together, but "there was no breath in them" (Ezekiel 37:8), until the Lord's breath comes. In Hebrew, breath, wind and spirit in this chapter are all one word (Word Studies, study 4). Life comes when His Spirit enters. And the vision ends with a promise spoken straight to people who had said "our hope is lost" (Ezekiel 37:11): "I will open your graves… And shall put my spirit in you, and ye shall live" (Ezekiel 37:12–14).

Where the River Goes ▶︎

The last assigned chapter shows water coming out from under the door of the Lord's house (Ezekiel 47:1). It starts as a trickle and keeps growing, though Ezekiel mentions no other stream joining it: ankle-deep, knee-deep, waist-deep, then "a river that I could not pass over" (Ezekiel 47:5).

Then notice where it goes. It flows straight into the Dead Sea, the one place in the land so salty that, as the manual notes, fish and plants cannot live in it (Historical & Cultural Context, section 8). The river does not go around the dead place. It goes into it, and "the waters shall be healed" (Ezekiel 47:8). "Every thing shall live whither the river cometh" (Ezekiel 47:9). Trees grow on its banks, "and the leaf thereof for medicine" (Ezekiel 47:12).

My husband and I recently attended the funeral of Bishop J.D. Banasky, one of the bishops who served in the young single adult stake where we met. We both loved J.D. and looked up to him so much. He was one of our matchmakers and blessed our lives in so many ways.

J.D. was constantly giving. His home was open to students at all hours of the day and night. He was always cooking and kept his kitchen stocked for hungry college students. Those meals often came with warm, wise counsel.

A few weeks before our wedding, J.D.’s wife passed away unexpectedly, just days after giving birth to their daughter. She had fallen asleep with their newborn on her chest and did not wake up. My husband spent his “bachelor’s party” with J.D., rocking the baby so J.D. could get some rest.

J.D. continued to give through his grief. At his memorial, his children recounted memory after memory of his daily acts of service. Even while living in a care center and facing debilitating health issues, he kept finding ways to care for others.

The opening song at the funeral was “Give, Said the Little Stream.” I had never heard that song sung at a funeral before, but it captured the way J.D. had lived. He kept giving, even when it might have seemed there was nothing more to give. His generosity became living water to so many people, and giving seemed to sustain him too.

As I read Ezekiel’s vision this week, I thought about J.D. and how I could better follow his example. Through his love and generosity, I learned to recognize more clearly the love and generosity of my Savior. J.D.’s life helps me picture what it can mean for the Lord’s living water to reach others through us.

Imagine if each of us, as recipients of God’s generosity, lived more like J.D.—continually sharing the living water we have received. As that kindness passes from one person to another, imagine how far it could reach: a growing river of generosity, bringing comfort, hope and healing wherever it flows and gathers.

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

The book of Ezekiel begins with the glory of the Lord appearing among exiles and ends with the name of a city:

"And the name of the city from that day shall be, The LORD is there." — Ezekiel 48:35

A people who thought God had left them are promised a home whose very name says He has not. Wherever you are sitting this week, by whatever river, that is the promise: The LORD is there.
