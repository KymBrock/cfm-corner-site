First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

For once, there is no jump. This week begins mid-sentence.

Last week, 2 Chronicles ended on a cliffhanger. A Persian king named Cyrus gives an order — God "hath charged me to build him an house in Jerusalem… let him go up" (2 Chronicles 36:23) — and the book simply stops there, on the word up. (Chronicles is the last book of the Hebrew Bible, so that unfinished invitation is quite literally where the Hebrew scriptures end.) This week's reading opens by finishing that very sentence: "let him go up to Jerusalem… and build the house of the Lord" (Ezra 1:3). The seam is deliberate. Everything we have read for months has been waiting for that phrase. Now it comes — the exile ends, right on the schedule Jeremiah had promised (Jeremiah 29:10).

Two things will make the week easier to follow.

First, Ezra and Nehemiah are really one book — a single story of the return home — and our lesson lands on its high points rather than reading straight through. So the reading can feel like it skips around; it does, and it is helpful to recognize that.

Second, the story stretches across about a hundred years and three separate homecomings. First a wave of exiles rebuilds the altar and, eventually, the temple. Then — after a long gap of nearly sixty years — Ezra the scribe arrives to teach the law. A little later, Nehemiah arrives to rebuild the city wall. (Next week's book, Esther, actually takes place inside that long gap — the story of the Jews who stayed behind in Persia.) Keep those three arrivals in mind — temple, teacher, wall — and the week holds together.

A Letter to Fellow Students ▶︎

If you want a single thread to hold onto this week, here it is: rise up, and build. The whole story is people being stirred to their feet and put to work — with God's hand steadying the work the entire way.

It begins with the unlikeliest missionary in scripture: a Persian emperor. "The Lord stirred up the spirit of Cyrus," the text says (Ezra 1:1) — a king who did not worship Israel's God, moved by that God to send the exiles home. And the same stirring reaches the ordinary people, those "whose spirit God had raised" to go up (Ezra 1:5). The return begins, from the throne room down, as God waking people up.

Back in the land, the first thing they rebuild is not a wall or even the temple — it is an altar, so that worship can resume before the walls are even safe (Ezra 3:2–3). When the temple's foundation is finally laid, the sound that goes up is unforgettable: the young shout for joy while the old men who remembered the first temple weep aloud, until "the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping" (Ezra 3:12–13). If you have ever rebuilt anything after a loss, you know that sound — grief and gladness in the same breath.

Then opposition arrives, and it does not leave. Neighbors who are refused a share in the work set out to discourage and delay it, and the building stops for years (Ezra 4:4–5, 24). What finally restarts it is not a better permit but prophets — Haggai and Zechariah — stirring the people back to their feet (Ezra 5:1–2). The temple is finished at last (Ezra 6:15), protected, of all things, by a Persian king's archives.

Two generations later comes Ezra, "a ready scribe in the law of Moses" (Ezra 7:6) — the first great leader of God's people who is neither king nor warrior but a man whose power is simply that he knows the book. The verse that defines him could define the week: he "prepared his heart to seek the law of the Lord, and to do it, and to teach in Israel" (Ezra 7:10) — seek, do, teach, in that order.

Then Nehemiah, the king's cupbearer, who hears that Jerusalem still lies in ruins and rises to rebuild its wall. He gives us the week's motto — "Let us rise up and build" (Nehemiah 2:18) — and, under mockery and threats, the line the lesson is named for: "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down" (Nehemiah 6:3). The wall goes up in just fifty-two days (Nehemiah 6:15).

And the week ends not with stone but with an open book. The people gather at the Water Gate, Ezra reads the law aloud, and the Levites "gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). The people weep as they finally grasp it — and their leaders gently redirect the tears: "the joy of the Lord is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:10).

Rise, build, and let God steady your hands. That is the shape of the week — and the study guide slows down on each turn of it.

The Wall That Was Built, and the Wall Christ Broke ▶︎

There is a quiet thread running under this week's reading that most of us pass right over — and it turns out to be one of the deep roots of the entire New Testament. To see it, you have to slow down at a verse that looks like bookkeeping.

Start with who these people were. The community that came home from Babylon was small, and it was a community of survivors. Their grandparents had watched Jerusalem burn. Their nation had been swallowed whole by an empire, and everything that had made them a people — land, temple, throne — had been taken away. Only one thing had survived the exile intact: their identity as Israel, carried in memory, in law, and in family lines. So when they returned, they rebuilt more than a temple and a wall. They rebuilt a boundary. A people once dissolved was determined never to dissolve again — and it drew its lines by blood.

You can watch the line being drawn in the register of the returnees. Certain priests "sought their register among those that were reckoned by genealogy, but they were not found: therefore were they, as polluted, put from the priesthood" (Ezra 2:62). Read that slowly. These men may well have been true sons of Aaron; they simply could not prove it on paper. And in the new community, the paper was the point. They were barred from the holy things until a day when "there stood up a priest with Urim and with Thummim" (Ezra 2:63) — that is, until a priest could inquire of God directly and settle what no genealogy could. It was an honest deferral: we cannot decide this; someone greater will have to. But no one in that generation could fill the office. So the question was left open — a door left ajar in the very first chapter of the return.

And the boundary did not stop at the priesthood. Ezra pressed the same rule of descent onto marriage itself: the "holy seed" must not "mingle… with the people of those lands" (Ezra 9:2). Nehemiah shut the congregation to the Ammonite and Moabite "for ever" (Nehemiah 13:1). The line of blood had become the line of belonging.

Before we judge them, be fair to them. Every survival instinct they had said hold the line. In their prophets' telling, it was precisely mingling — foreign wives, foreign gods, borrowed worship — that had cost them the first temple. A tiny remnant, surrounded by larger peoples, with no army and no king, had exactly one wall left that Babylon could not breach: the wall of who counts as us. The boundary was built to save a people. And in a real sense, it did.

But watch where it goes.

The first crack ran through Israel's own family. In Ezra 4, the neighboring peoples come with what sounds like an olive branch: "we seek your God, as ye do; and we do sacrifice unto him since the days of Esar-haddon king of Assur, which brought us up hither" (Ezra 4:2). These were the peoples Assyria had resettled in the north generations earlier — the mixed-worship community we met two weeks ago. And the answer they receive is the boundary, spoken aloud: "Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God" (Ezra 4:3). The historian Josephus knew these people well — "called in the Hebrew tongue Cutheans, but in the Greek tongue Samaritans" (Antiquities 9.14.3) — and he traces the breach to its bitter end: when a brother of the high priest married the daughter of Sanballat the Samaritan governor, Jerusalem's elders forced him to choose — "divorce his wife, or not to approach the altar" — and Sanballat answered by building a rival temple on Mount Gerizim (Antiquities 11.7–8). The refusal never healed. By the time a tired traveler sat down at a Samaritan well five centuries later, the breach was simply a fact of life, so ordinary that a Gospel writer states it as common knowledge: "the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans" (John 4:9).

And the principle became a wall of stone. By Jesus' day, the boundary Ezra had drawn in a register was built into Herod's temple as an actual, physical barrier — a low stone partition called the soreg, ringing the inner courts, posted with signs "in Greek, and some in Roman letters, that no foreigner should go within that sanctuary" (Josephus, Wars of the Jews 5.5.2), the prohibition backed "under pain of death." This is not a legend or an exaggeration. One of those very signs was dug out of the ground in 1871, and you can still read it: "No stranger is to enter within the balustrade round the temple and enclosure. Whoever is caught will be himself responsible for his ensuing death." Stand in front of that sentence for a moment. A rule that began as a grieving community's attempt to stay alive had become a wall a man could die for crossing.

Meanwhile, the office stayed empty. Remember the door left ajar in Ezra 2 — the priest "with Urim and with Thummim" who would one day stand up and resolve what the registers could not? He never came. Through the whole life of the Second Temple, that office went unfilled. The rabbis themselves said so: the Talmud counts the Urim and Thummim among the very things the returned temple lacked — along with the Ark, the heavenly fire, and the Divine Presence (Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 21b). Let that sink in: by the rabbis' own accounting, the Second Temple outwardly stood but inwardly waited. The wall grew higher and higher around a house still waiting for someone.

Then One walked in and opened the gate. In a Nazareth synagogue, Jesus stood up to read Isaiah's promise — "the acceptable year of the Lord," "deliverance to the captives" — and sat down and said, "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears" (Luke 4:18–21). So far, so good; the hometown crowd is pleased. Then he keeps talking. He reminds them that in the days of Elijah and Elisha, when Israel was full of widows and lepers, God sent his prophets to exactly two people — a Phoenician widow of Sidon, and Naaman the Syrian (Luke 4:25–27). Two foreigners. Two people from the wrong side of the wall. And the congregation that had just admired him "were filled with wrath," rose up, and moved to throw him off a cliff (Luke 4:28–29). It was the first sermon of his ministry, and it nearly got him killed — for suggesting that God's grace had never respected the boundary.

And it was the pattern of his whole ministry, start to finish: Gentile wise men at his cradle (Matthew 2), a Samaritan woman at a well (John 4), a Samaritan leper singled out for thanks (Luke 17:16), a Canaanite mother praised for "great faith" (Matthew 15:28), a Samaritan cast as the hero of his best-known parable (Luke 10:33). Over and over, the people the wall shut out are the ones he seeks out.

And it is worth pausing here, with real tenderness, over who those outsiders were — because the wall did not only shut out strangers. It shut out family. It was not only priests who came home unable to prove their line; ordinary Israelite households returned so long-scattered that they could not show "whether they were of Israel" (Ezra 2:59–62). The northern people we so quickly file as "the adversaries" first came asking to help — "we seek your God, as ye do" (Ezra 4:2) — a mixed people, part Israelite by blood, remnants of the northern tribes Assyria had scattered, wanting back in. And the scattering had reached further still. Two generations in Babylon and Persia had made God-fearers and friends among the nations — and remember that Daniel himself had been set "chief of the governors over all the wise men of Babylon" (Daniel 2:48), the very kind of court sage the New Testament will later call the Magi. It is at least possible — and let us hold this as a thought, not a claim of the text — that when "wise men from the east" came seeking "the King of the Jews," having read his coming in a star (Matthew 2:1–2), they came as heirs of a witness a faithful exile had planted among them generations before. If so, they too were people the wall would have kept outside. These were not villains. They were the fallout of catastrophe — the scattered, trying to find their way home.

And gathering them was the whole point of what Christ came to do. He called himself the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to go after the one that is lost. "Other sheep I have," he said, "which are not of this fold: them also I must bring" (John 10:16). He would die, John tells us, "not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad" (John 11:52) — the very promise God had made through Ezekiel to a people in exile: "so will I seek out my sheep, and will deliver them out of all places where they have been scattered" (Ezekiel 34:12). The wall was built by a scattered people desperate to survive. The gathering was God's answer to the scattering itself.

Now for the bookend that ties the whole arc together. When a mob finally seized Paul in that temple, the charge was that he had brought "Greeks also into the temple" and so had "polluted this holy place" (Acts 21:28). Polluted — the very word the King James translators had used at Ezra 2:62 for the priests struck from the rolls. The story that opened with men called polluted for lacking a genealogy closes with a mob screaming polluted at the gentiles' apostle. Five centuries, one word. And by the time that mob formed, the gospel's answer was already written: Christ "hath broken down the middle wall of partition," so that the outsider is "no more" a stranger and foreigner but a "fellowcitizen" (Ephesians 2:14, 19). Paul, who nearly died at the soreg, is the one who tells us it has already fallen.

Here is a reflection worth holding gently — and let us be exact about what it is: a devotional reading, not doctrine, and not scholarship. Ezra's awaited "priest with Urim and Thummim" is never named — not by the text, not by the rabbis, not by any authority this guide can cite. But it is hard, having walked this arc, not to see Him. The true High Priest who inquires of God directly. The one who reopens what bloodline had closed. The one who stands up in a synagogue and reads the acceptable year to the very people the wall shut out — and is eventually killed for it. The office Ezra left open, a Galilean carpenter walked in and filled. Hold that thought the way the returned exiles held the question itself: reverently, and without forcing it. But hold it.

The boundary was built to save a people. It ended by shutting out the world. And then Someone tore it down — and it cost Him everything.

The Weapon Was Money ▶︎

There is one more thread in this week's story, and it hides in the driest verses of all — the ones about taxes. But follow the money, and you find the machinery underneath the whole fight.

Start with a fact that is easy to miss: the returning exiles could not have rebuilt anything without the Persian treasury. When the work first stalls and then restarts, what protects and pays for it is a royal decree — Darius orders the temple funded "of the king's goods" (Ezra 6:8). And when Ezra comes up, he carries a remarkable privilege: the king declares that "it shall not be lawful to impose toll, tribute, or custom" on the priests, Levites, and temple servants (Ezra 7:24). The people who served the house of God were, by imperial law, exempt from taxes.

This was not special favoritism for the Jews. It was simply how Persia ran an empire — kings routinely shielded temples and their sacred staff from taxes and forced labor. (We even have a surviving royal letter, preserved in a later copy, in which a Persian king rebukes one of his own officials for daring to tax a temple's sacred workers.) To Persia, funding a religion was cheaper than fighting one.

Now watch that same machinery become a weapon. When the neighboring peoples want the rebuilding stopped, they do not make a religious argument — they make a financial one. They warn the king that a rebuilt Jerusalem "will not pay toll, tribute, and custom, and so thou shalt endamage the revenue of the kings" (Ezra 4:13). Threaten an empire's income and it listens: the king orders the work halted (Ezra 4:21). The single most effective attack on the temple in this whole book is not an army. It is an argument about lost tax revenue.

It is tempting — standing back — to see how tightly money and the bloodline-boundary might have been wound together. That tax-free status belonged to a class you had to prove you were part of; loosen the proof, and in theory anyone could claim a seat at the exempt table. Whether the community ever actually reasoned that way, no source we can find says outright — so hold this one as a thought, not a fact. But the shape of that world is unmistakable: holiness and revenue were filed in the same drawer.

Which is why a scene five centuries later should sound familiar. When Jesus' opponents set the trap designed to destroy him, they reach for exactly this weapon. Pharisees and pro-Roman Herodians — natural enemies, briefly allied — send men to ask, sweetly, "Is it lawful to give tribute unto Caesar, or not?" (Matthew 22:17). It is a perfect snare: answer no, and Rome hears treason; answer yes, and the crowd hears betrayal. The Gospel says plainly what they were doing — they came to "entangle him in his talk" (Matthew 22:15). The same lever that once halted the temple — a tax question, sprung as a legal trap — is now aimed at the temple's true builder. And he slips it without denying either claim: "Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's" (Matthew 22:21).

The wall in this week's story was defended with money as much as with stone. And the One who came to take it down was met with the very same weapon — and would not be entangled.

"I Am Doing a Great Work": The Art of Not Coming Down ▶︎

Nehemiah's enemies tried three tactics, and it is worth telling them apart, because you will meet all three.

First came ridicule — "will they fortify themselves?" — which the builders answered by building. Then came threats of attack, which they answered with prayer and a watch. But the third tactic was the subtlest, because it did not look like opposition at all. It looked like an invitation: "come, let us meet together" (Nehemiah 6:2). Four times they sent it, and Nehemiah's reply is one sentence long: "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down: why should the work cease, whilst I leave it, and come down to you?" (Nehemiah 6:3).

Notice what he does not say. He does not say the meeting is evil. He says the work is great — and that leaving it has a cost. Some things that pull us off the wall are enemies; most are merely meetings. The discipline of the week is learning to tell opposition you must endure from distraction you must simply decline. Nehemiah had decided in advance what his great work was, which is why he could answer in one sentence.

And then the rabbis add a footnote that keeps the whole thing honest. The Talmud asks why the book of this heroic builder was not, in their tradition, named for him — and answers that Nehemiah "took credit for himself and boasted about his good deeds," citing his refrain, "Remember me, God, for good" (Nehemiah 13:31; Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 93b). Whether or not one accepts the verdict, the caution lands: even a man who prays and builds and reforms can blur his witness by keeping one eye on his own credit. Do the great work. Refuse to come down. And leave the remembering to God.

Prayer in One Hand, a Trowel in the Other ▶︎

If you want the week's theology of work in a single verse, it is this one: "Nevertheless we made our prayer unto our God, and set a watch against them day and night" (Nehemiah 4:9).

Prayer and a watch. Not prayer instead of a watch, as though trusting God excused the labor; not a watch instead of prayer, as though the labor could carry itself. The builders on the wall are the picture of it — each with a tool in one of his hands while the other "held a weapon" (Nehemiah 4:17). Nehemiah prays before he speaks to the king (Nehemiah 2:4) and rides out at night to survey the ruins. The pattern never breaks.

The week's word study on yad, "hand," shows why. The phrase "the good hand of my God upon me" (Nehemiah 2:8) is Nehemiah's explanation for everything — the king's favor, the timber, the timing. And yet the theme verse ends with a different set of hands: "So they strengthened their hands for this good work" (Nehemiah 2:18). God's hand makes the work possible; the workers' hands take it up. Two hands, one work. "Remember that it is upon the rock of our Redeemer… that ye must build your foundation" (Helaman 5:12) — but even on that rock, somebody has to lay stone.

The Joy of the Lord Is Your Strength ▶︎

The climax of this week is a congregation in tears — and two leaders telling them to stop crying.

At the Water Gate, the people hear the law read and explained — the Levites "gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8) — and understanding breaks them. They weep, presumably because they finally see the distance between the law and their lives. (The craft behind "gave the sense" is richer than it looks: the rabbis read the phrase as the whole layered art of public reading — Scripture, its Aramaic Targum for a people who had largely lost Hebrew in exile, and the chant that makes meaning land. The study guide's Jewish Perspective traces it, and even carries the thread forward to Jesus reading in the Nazareth synagogue. But the heart of it is plain: the word was made understandable.) And Nehemiah and Ezra, remarkably, refuse the tears: "mourn not, nor weep… for the joy of the Lord is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:9–10). Guilt, it turns out, is not the goal of scripture. Understanding is — and understanding, rightly finished, lands in joy. The word torah itself says so: from a root meaning to point or direct, it is not a code to condemn but an instruction to aim a life. An arrow is not aimed in order to shame it.

The rabbis remembered this assembly as a beginning, not a one-time event: tradition credits Ezra with instituting the regular public reading of the Torah — on Shabbat afternoon, and every Monday and Thursday (Babylonian Talmud, Bava Kamma 82a) — a rhythm of returning to the word that has shaped Jewish life for two and a half millennia. What Nehemiah 8 narrates once, Ezra made a habit. That is the same instinct behind a curriculum like the one that brought you here: a covenant people stays covenant by returning to the word on a fixed and frequent rhythm — and by pressing past reading to the sense.

And the chapter ends where the week began, with joy displacing grief. In Ezra 3, joy and weeping were indistinguishable in one roar. In Nehemiah 8, the people keep the Feast of Tabernacles with a gladness unmatched "since the days of Jeshua the son of Nun" (Nehemiah 8:17). The arc of restoration runs from tears you cannot tell from shouting — to joy that has become strength.

The Invitation ▶︎

Week 31 asks a builder's questions — and they are wonderfully practical.

What is the great work God has put in front of you right now? Name it, specifically. Then notice the "come, let us meet together" invitations that most reliably pull you away from it — the ones that are not evil, just not it. Nehemiah could answer in a single sentence because he had already decided what his great work was.

Notice the order of your building, too. The exiles raised the altar before the walls — worship before defenses. Is yours in that order? And when the work stalls, as it did for years between Ezra 4 and Ezra 5, do you wait for easier conditions, or for a prophetic word to stir you back to your feet? Is your prayer paired with a watch, your faith with a trowel? And when you finally understand the word deeply enough to weep over it — will you let the weeping finish as joy?

One last question, from the heart of the week. Every community that survives a catastrophe builds boundaries, and some of them are holy and right. But the arc from Ezra's register, to the cliff at Nazareth, to the broken middle wall asks each of us to inspect our own masonry: which of the walls in your life guard something sacred — and which have quietly become a soreg, keeping out the very people the Lord of the house is trying to bring in?

"Arise and shine forth, that thy light may be a standard for the nations" (D&C 115:5). The rubble does not get the last word. The last word is an open book, a people who understand it, and the joy of the Lord — which is, and always was, their strength.

Let us rise up and build.
