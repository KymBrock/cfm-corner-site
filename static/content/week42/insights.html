First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week was Isaiah 50–57. It sat with the suffering Servant of chapter 53, then opened the door to the foreigner and the eunuch, and in its last chapter heard the high and lofty One say He dwells "with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit."

This week is the end of the book: Isaiah 58 to 66, nine chapters, all assigned, read straight through. The Overview walks through it chapter by chapter. Here we stop at a few places where the reading turns toward you.

Repairer of the Breach ▶︎

Near the middle of Isaiah 58 there is a promise that is easy to read past:

"And they that shall be of thee shall build the old waste places: thou shalt raise up the foundations of many generations; and thou shalt be called, The repairer of the breach, The restorer of paths to dwell in." — Isaiah 58:12

It is a name, and it is given to a particular person: the one who keeps the fast the Lord has chosen, who loosens burdens, shares bread and does not hide from people in need. A breach was a gap in a city wall, and the first hearers were living beside broken walls (Historical & Cultural Context, section 1, describes their situation). Isaiah promises that the person who lifts others will become someone who mends.

Most of us know where the breaches are near us. A relationship that has gone cold. A neighbour nobody visits. A family where something broke a generation ago and nobody has tried to repair it. The promise of 58:12 is not that we will fix everything. It is that the Lord gives this name to people who start, one burden at a time.

Is there a breach near you that you could begin to repair this week? It may be small. Isaiah calls it restoring "paths to dwell in": making a way for someone to come home.

Your Old Names Are Not Your Last Names ▶︎

Chapter 62 does something tender with names. Zion has been called "Forsaken" and her land "Desolate," and then the Lord renames her:

"Thou shalt no more be termed Forsaken; neither shall thy land any more be termed Desolate: but thou shalt be called Hephzi-bah, and thy land Beulah: for the LORD delighteth in thee." — Isaiah 62:4

(Word Studies, study 5, explains what the two names mean.) And at the end of the chapter comes the last new name, the one many of us most need to hear: "Sought out, A city not forsaken" (62:12).

The name Zion is not being rejected. Forsaken and Desolate describe the loss the city and its people had experienced; the promised names express God's delight and their restored relationship with Him. We may also carry painful labels, given by others or by ourselves: failure, too late, unwanted, the one who left. A label may recall a real event without telling the whole truth about a person. Isaiah's promise invites us to look beyond loss toward the belonging God offers.

How might remembering that you are loved by God change the way you speak about yourself—or someone else—this week?

Comfort for All That Mourn ▶︎

Isaiah 61 sends someone "to comfort all that mourn" (61:2). The Savior read that chapter aloud in the synagogue at Nazareth and told the people it was fulfilled that day (Key Passages, the passage on 61:1–3, reads the scene, including how the congregation answered Him).

The people Isaiah 61 sends good news to are the same people who "shall build the old wastes" (61:4). Those who are comforted become the rebuilders, like the repairer of the breach in 58:12.

Near the end of the book, the comfort comes in God's own voice:

"As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you; and ye shall be comforted in Jerusalem." — Isaiah 66:13

Isaiah places that comfort "in Jerusalem," the holy mountain to which the scattered are brought home seven verses later (66:20). The comfort and the gathering belong to one promise: God comforts His people as He brings them home. If you are grieving this week, the promise is His, "so will I comfort you," and it does not wait until your grief is finished.

A House of Prayer for All People ▶︎

At Nazareth, Jesus recalled God's mercy to a widow in Sidon and to Naaman the Syrian—examples from Israel's sacred history of help reaching beyond Israel (Luke 4:25–27). Isaiah had already described a wide welcome. Israel knew scattering, and Isaiah 56 records the Lord's promise to gather "the outcasts of Israel." There was also the fear of being shut out of the Lord's house. That same chapter, last week's reading, answers the stranger who feared "The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people." Strangers and eunuchs who hold to the covenant will be brought to His holy mountain, "for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people." And the Lord adds, "Yet will I gather others to him" (Isaiah 56:3–8). Isaiah's book closes on the same wide circle. God sends messengers to "the isles afar off, that have not heard my fame" to declare His glory "among the Gentiles," and Israel's own scattered "brethren" are brought home "out of all nations" (66:19–20).

The Savior carried that welcome in person. At Nazareth His hearers first "wondered at the gracious words" He spoke. They were filled with wrath when He reminded them that God's mercy had once gone to a widow of Sidon and to Naaman the Syrian (Luke 4:22–28). In Samaria He crossed an old dividing line. As John's Gospel puts it, "the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans" (John 4:9). The woman at the well, who spoke of "our father Jacob," raised the question that divided their peoples: "Our fathers worshipped in this mountain; and ye say, that in Jerusalem is the place where men ought to worship." He was plain about the difference ("salvation is of the Jews," He said), and He told her of an hour when "the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:12, 20–23). Many in her city came to say, "this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world" (John 4:39–42).

Where have you seen God's welcome reach someone you did not expect it to reach?

One Verse to Take With You ▶︎

The week's title, from the middle of the reading:

"And the Redeemer shall come to Zion, and unto them that turn from transgression in Jacob, saith the LORD." — Isaiah 59:20

The word for Redeemer is gōʾēl (גֹּאֵל), and in Israel's law the redeemer was family, a near relative who bought back what had been lost (Word Studies, study 2, explains the law behind it).

That is how the New Testament speaks of Jesus Christ. He took part of "flesh and blood" like ours, and "he is not ashamed to call them brethren" (Hebrews 2:11, 14). He is the near kinsman who buys back what we lost and could not recover ourselves.

So when this week's reading feels heavy with ruins and confessions, remember who is coming to Zion. Not a distant official, but family. This week, let Him come close: take one step toward Him, and trust that He is already on the road.
