First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

For weeks now the Old Testament has been telling the story of a people — kings and exiles, temples built and cities rebuilt, a nation carried off to Babylon and Persia and brought home again. This week the camera turns all the way around. Job is not the story of a nation. It is the story of one man, on one ash-heap, asking one question: why?

And the man is not even an Israelite. Job lives "in the land of Uz" (Job 1:1), somewhere east of Israel, outside the covenant nation, and the book never bothers to place him on Israel's map or in Israel's history. That is on purpose. The Bible Project's overview of the book notes that this placement is "intentional," so that we "focus simply on the story of Job and on the questions raised by his experience of suffering" (Book of Job Summary, BibleProject). Stripped of nation, temple, and law, Job stands for something bigger than any one people: the human being caught in undeserved pain — which is why his book has spoken to sufferers of every nation ever since.

The reading also changes shape. Up to now we have mostly read narrative — history told as story. Job belongs to what scholars call the Old Testament's "wisdom literature" (along with Proverbs and Ecclesiastes) — books that wrestle less with Israel's history and more with how life actually works. And Job wrestles with the hardest question wisdom ever faces: when a genuinely good person suffers terribly and undeservedly, what do we make of God?

A Letter to Fellow Students ▶︎

Job is one of the most honest books in all of scripture, because it refuses to give a cheap answer.

Most of us were handed a comfortable theory somewhere along the way: good people are blessed, bad people are punished, so if life falls apart, someone must have done something wrong. Job takes that theory, holds it up to the light of a good man's ruin, and breaks it in half. Job has not earned his suffering — the book tells us so from the first verse, and at the end God Himself says so (Job 42:7). And yet everything is taken from him anyway: his wealth, his ten children, his health, and very nearly his hope.

Watch the arc of the man across the reading. He begins on the summit of faith — "the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord" (Job 1:21). Then the same wholehearted man breaks open and curses the day he was born (Job 3). He argues, he complains, he insists he is innocent, he begs for a hearing before a God who feels absent — "Oh that I knew where I might find him!" (Job 23:3). And out of that very darkness, mid-argument and abandoned by his friends, comes the single most famous line in the book: "I know that my redeemer liveth" (Job 19:25). At the end, when God finally speaks, He does not explain a thing — and it turns out Job did not need an explanation. He needed a Person: "now mine eye seeth thee" (Job 42:5).

The book of Job does not answer why we suffer. It answers a deeper and better question: whether God can still be trusted when we do not know why. That is what we will sit with this week.

The Question the Book Will Not Answer ▶︎

Here is the first thing to make peace with: Job never gets told why.

We, the readers, are let in on a secret Job never learns. The book opens by lifting a curtain on a scene in heaven — a kind of heavenly court where "the sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord" (Job 1:6). Among them comes a figure the Hebrew calls ha-satan — literally "the satan," with the word "the" attached. That little word matters. It tells us this is a title, not yet a personal name: it means "the accuser" or "the adversary," a prosecutor's role. The Bible Project overview renders it "the Accuser" or "the Prosecutor" (BibleProject).

And the accuser's challenge is not about Job's behavior but about his motive. Does Job "fear God for nought" (Job 1:9) — that is, for nothing, for no payoff? Take away the blessings, the accuser wagers, and the worship will vanish with them. So the hedge of protection comes down, and Job loses everything.

Now here is the thing to hold onto: Job never sees that opening scene. He never finds out about the wager, never learns there was a reason, never gets the explanation we were given on page one. He suffers in the dark. And that is deliberate, because it makes Job a mirror for us. We, too, live on the near side of a curtain. We rarely get to see the "why" of our hardest days. Job's whole book is written for people who are suffering without the backstory — which is to say, for all of us.

One caution the Latter-day Saint seminary manual adds is worth repeating, so we don't misread that heavenly scene: "the Lord does not really make agreements with Satan… the Lord has power over Satan and has no need to bargain with him" (Old Testament Seminary Teacher Manual, Lesson 107). The opening is a way of putting a real question into story form — is love of God possible for God's own sake? — not a report that God gambles with the devil.

It helps to remember what kind of figure is asking that question. Whatever else we say about the adversary, his role in this scene is a prosecutor's — and a prosecutor's whole job is to test motives: to press the case, to ask whether a person's faith is the real thing or just something the blessings paid for. And that testing is not a strange cruelty tacked onto life. It is very close to the point of life. This world is a proving ground — "we will prove them herewith, to see if they will do all things whatsoever the Lord their God shall command them" (Abraham 3:25). The plan of salvation is, in part, exactly this kind of test: a place to learn and to grow, and to show whether we can be trusted with what a loving Father longs to give His children — in the end, all that He has. Job's trial is that proving pressed to its very hardest point.

And the test runs in more than one direction. It asks whether God can trust us — and, just as much, whether we will learn to trust Him, and even to trust that our own devotion is real, once every visible reward has been stripped away. The adversary is real, but he is on a leash; nothing he does touches the faithful except by permission and for a purpose — and the purpose, in the end, is us.

"Though He Slay Me" ▶︎

Strip away the payoff, and what is left of faith? That is the accuser's experiment. And Job answers it with one of the boldest sentences a believer has ever spoken: "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him" (Job 13:15).

Sit with how extreme that is. Job does not say, "I'll trust God because I'm sure He'll fix this." He says he will trust God even if the worst happens — even if God never rescues him at all. This is faith with the safety net cut away: not faith that God will remove the trial, but faith in God whether or not He does.

The Jewish teachers saw exactly this in the line, and it became their highest praise of Job. They taught that Job "served the Holy One, Blessed be He, only out of love, as it is stated: 'Though He will slay me, still I will trust in Him'" (Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 31a, courtesy of Sefaria, CC BY-NC). Their point is a distinction worth keeping: you can serve God out of fear — because you dread the punishment — or out of love — because of who He is, no matter what He gives or withholds. Job, they said, served from love. And love is the kind of faith that survives when the blessings are gone, because it was never about the blessings.

President James E. Faust reached for this very verse to describe standing firm when everything is against you: "Said he, speaking of the Lord, 'Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him'" ("Where Do I Make My Stand?," October 2004). That is the question Job forces on each of us: is my devotion tied to God's gifts — or to God? What would be left of my worship if the gifts were taken away?

"I Know That My Redeemer Liveth" ▶︎

Everything in the book bends toward one sentence, and it comes from the bottom of the pit — not from the calm Job of chapter 1, but from the ruined Job of the ash-heap, mid-argument, deserted by his friends:

"For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth: And though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God." (Job 19:25–26)

To feel the force of it, you have to know the word behind "redeemer." In Hebrew it is go'el — the kinsman-redeemer. In ancient Israel the go'el was a specific, concrete person: your nearest relative, the one whose family duty it was to buy you back if you fell into debt or slavery, to reclaim lost family land, or to defend you when you could not defend yourself. (It is the whole plot of the book of Ruth: Boaz is Ruth's go'el, the kinsman who steps in — "the LORD… hath not left thee this day without a kinsman," Ruth 4:14.)

Now hear Job again. He has lost every earthly advocate — his children dead, his wealth gone, his wife in despair, his friends turned prosecutor. He has no one left to step in for him. And into that total absence he reaches, past all of them, to a living go'el: a Kinsman-Redeemer who "liveth," who "shall stand at the latter day upon the earth," and who will bring Job through death itself — "in my flesh shall I see God." Out of the ash-heap comes a flash of resurrection hope: a living Redeemer, a body remade, God seen with Job's own eyes.

The Interpreter Foundation scholar Mack Stirling notes that the Hebrew hints at even more than the English shows — "that the Redeemer/Advocate will be God Himself" (Mack C. Stirling, "Job: An LDS Reading," Interpreter Foundation). The Kinsman Job longed for is the One whose empty tomb would answer his question forever. President Joseph Fielding Smith made Job's confession his own: "I feel to say with Job of old, whose knowledge came from the same source from which mine has come: 'For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth,' and that 'in my flesh shall I see God'" ("I Know That My Redeemer Liveth," October 1971).

And this is why so many readers have seen the shape of Christ in Job himself. A blameless man, brought unjustly to the depths, who at the end will pray for the very friends who wounded him — Stirling writes that "many have rightly considered Job to be a type of Christ," his "bitter experiences" answering to Christ drinking the bitter cup, his "role as mediator for the friends" a picture of "Christ's as mediator for mankind" (Stirling, Interpreter Foundation). Job foresaw dimly what we have been given plainly: a living Redeemer who "will take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people… that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people" (Alma 7:11–12). Job's Redeemer did not stay behind the curtain. He came down into the ash-heap Himself.

The Voice from the Whirlwind ▶︎

For thirty-five chapters, human beings argue about God. Then God finally speaks — "out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1) — and here is the surprise: He never answers the question.

Instead, He asks questions of His own. Dozens of them, one after another, a sweeping tour of a universe far larger than Job's suffering: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?… When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" (Job 38:4, 7). Where were you when I set the sea its limits, when I taught the hawk to fly, when I fed the young lions? On and on, wonder after wonder.

At first this can sound like God dodging — changing the subject when a hurting man asks "why?" But look closer. God is not changing the subject; He is changing the scale. Job has been staring into the small, sealed room of his own pain until it filled his whole horizon. God flings open the windows onto a cosmos of unimaginable wisdom and beauty and asks, in effect, do you trust the One who runs all of this? The turning point in Job is not an answer; it is an enlargement. His question was never going to be settled by information. It is dissolved by encounter. President Gordon B. Hinckley pointed to this same whirlwind speech as a word for our anxious age ("The Dawning of a Brighter Day," April 2004).

And tucked inside verse 7 is a gift Latter-day Saint readers should not miss. "When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy" — before the earth was even founded, there was a joyful assembly. Jonn Claybaugh's Interpreter study aid reads it the way our doctrine does: "We all 'shouted for joy' at the prospect of mortality, when presented to us in the council in heaven (38:7)." Which means the mortal life that now feels unbearable to Job — the very life that has brought him such loss — is the estate the "sons of God" once shouted to receive (compare Abraham 3:22). We wanted this. We cheered for it. Not for the pain, but for the whole mortal journey that the pain is part of. Remembering that premortal "yes" does not erase a single grief, but it changes how we carry it.

"Now Mine Eye Seeth Thee" ▶︎

Job's last words are not an argument won. They are a Person met:

"I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee." (Job 42:5)

His faith was real before — but it had been secondhand, a religion of report, of things he had been told. Now it is firsthand. The hearing of the ear has become the seeing of the eye. And this, it turns out, is often what suffering does when we let it drive us toward God instead of away: it turns a faith we were handed into a faith we have met.

Two details in the final chapter are easy to rush past, and both are worth slowing down for.

First, Job's next line — "wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6) — is not self-hatred, and it is not Job finally admitting his friends were right that he must have sinned. The seminary manual is careful here: "Job did not hate himself. Rather, he humbly acknowledged his weaknesses, sins, and limitations before the Lord" (Old Testament Seminary Teacher Manual, Lesson 109). It is simply the natural smallness a soul feels in the presence of God — the same "dust and ashes" with which Abraham once dared to speak to the Lord (Genesis 18:27).

Second, and more tender: Job's restoration begins the moment "he prayed for his friends" (Job 42:10) — the very men who had spent the whole book wounding him with their accusations. His own healing is bound up with forgiving them. And the "twice as much" that God then gives him is not a paycheck for good behavior. The Bible Project overview calls the restoration "a generous gift, not a reward," and Stirling reads the whole epilogue as "a this-worldly analogue of eternal life" (Stirling, Interpreter Foundation) — a small picture, drawn in cattle and children and long life, of the far greater restoration God gives to all who endure. President Henry B. Eyring saw the miracle in it plainly: "The story of Job ends with this miracle: 'So the Lord blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning'" ("The Comforter," April 2015).

A Kindness the Jewish Teachers Saw ▶︎

Because the rabbis wrestled with Job so honestly, they left behind two insights that belong in any week spent with this book.

The first is a mercy. They noticed that Job, in his agony, says things that are wrong — bold, near-bitter complaints against God's justice. And instead of condemning him for it, they concluded: "a person is not held responsible for what he says when he is in distress… Although Job uttered certain words that were wrong and inappropriate, he was not punished for them because he said them at a time of pain and hardship" (Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 16b, courtesy of Sefaria, CC BY-NC). That is grace in a single sentence — and it is God's own verdict, for at the end He tells the friends that Job "hath spoken of me the thing that is right" (Job 42:7). The God who "descended below them all" (D&C 122:8) is not scandalized by the honest cry of the afflicted. Job gives us permission to bring God our grief unpolished.

The second is about the friends. For all their bad theology, the rabbis honored them for one thing: they came. They traveled great distances the moment they heard, and before they said a single unhelpful word they "sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him" (Job 2:13). The rabbis coined a proverb from it: "Either a friend like the friends of Job or death" (Bava Batra 16b, Sefaria, CC BY-NC). And yet the same friends became "miserable comforters" (Job 16:2) the instant they opened their mouths to explain Job's pain and pin it on his sin. That is the whole ministering lesson in one story: their presence was a treasure; their explanations were a wound. To "mourn with those that mourn" (Mosiah 18:9) is a call to sit, not to solve.

"Thou Art Not Yet as Job" ▶︎

Latter-day Saints have a companion text to Job that no one else has. When Joseph Smith was suffering in a Missouri dungeon — cold, betrayed, his people driven out — the Lord comforted him with Job's own name: "Thou art not yet as Job; thy friends do not contend against thee… as they did Job" (D&C 121:10). And then came the promise that could stand as the moral of Job's entire book:

"all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good. The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?" (D&C 122:7–8)

There is the answer Job could only glimpse, spoken to us plainly. Why trust a God who does not always explain? Because that God has Himself gone lower than we will ever be asked to go. Job cried "I know that my redeemer liveth" into the dark, not yet knowing His name. We know His name. And we know that He did not stay above the suffering He allows — He "descended below them all," down into the deepest ash-heap there is, so that no grief we bring Him is one He has not already carried.

The Invitation ▶︎

Job is the book for a heaven that will not explain itself.

It is the book for everyone who has done their honest best, kept their covenants, loved God — and then watched their life come apart anyway, with no voice from above telling them why. Its answer is not a reason. Its answer is a Redeemer who lives, a God who can be trusted in the dark, and an encounter that turns out to be better than any explanation. So this week leaves us with a few honest questions:

Is my faith tied to God's blessings — or to God? Could I say, with Job, "though he slay me, yet will I trust in him"? When I cannot find God and cannot see the reason, can I still believe what Job believed — that "he knoweth the way that I take" (Job 23:10), even when I cannot find Him? Can I let my hardest days drive me toward God until a faith I was told becomes a faith I have met — "now mine eye seeth thee"? And when someone I love is on their own ash-heap, will I be the friend of the first seven days who simply sits — or the friend of the speeches who tries to explain the pain away?

Job never learned the reason. But he met the Redeemer. And at the end, the man who had lost everything could say the truest thing in the book: "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee." For I know that my Redeemer liveth — and He has descended below them all, that in our own flesh we too might one day see God.
