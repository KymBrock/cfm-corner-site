1. Leviticus Overview

Leviticus is the book most readers skip — and the one that may matter most for understanding how Israel lived in God's presence. If Exodus ends with God's glory filling the Tabernacle, Leviticus answers the question that glory immediately raises: Now what? How do sinful people live alongside a holy God without being consumed?

The answer is an entire system of approach: sacrifices, priesthood, purity, festivals, and ethical holiness — all designed not to keep people away from God but to make nearness possible. The Hebrew name for the book is Vayikra — "And He called" — because it opens with God calling to Moses from the Tabernacle. The English title "Leviticus" comes from the Latin Leviticus (via the Greek Λευιτικόν, Leuitikon), meaning "pertaining to the Levites" — because the tribe of Levi was set apart to officiate in the priesthood ordinances and sacrificial worship that the book describes. The Hebrew name tells us who speaks (God calls); the later title tells us who serves (the Levites carry it out). This is not a book of arbitrary rules. It is a book about a God who has moved in and now teaches His people how to draw close without being destroyed.

"And the LORD called unto Moses, and spake unto him out of the tabernacle of the congregation." (Leviticus 1:1)

God doesn't wait for Moses to approach. He calls. The entire book flows from this initiative — God reaching out from the place He has chosen to dwell, instructing His people in the art of sacred nearness. Leviticus is God's manual for living with Him.

1. Drawing Near Through Offering

The Hebrew word for sacrifice — korban — doesn't mean "slaughter." It comes from the root qarav, "to draw near." Every offering in Leviticus is an act of approach: the burnt offering (olah) ascends entirely to God; the peace offering (shelamim) creates communion; the sin and guilt offerings restore what sin has broken. Sacrifice is how Israel drew close.

2. Holiness as Divine Identity and Human Calling

"Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy" (Lev 19:2). Holiness (kedushah) isn't merely moral purity — it is the distinguishing character of God, and it becomes the covenant identity of His people. Israel is to be set apart as God is set apart. This is both gift and demand.

3. The Priesthood as Bridge

Aaron and his sons stand between God and Israel — offering sacrifice, pronouncing blessing, discerning clean from unclean. The High Priest alone enters the Holy of Holies once a year, bearing the sins of the nation. The priesthood makes access possible for the whole community.

4. Sin, Impurity, and Restoration

Leviticus takes sin and impurity seriously — not to condemn but to provide pathways back. The sin offering (chatat) addresses unintentional sin. The guilt offering (asham) covers cases requiring restitution. The Day of Atonement provides annual, comprehensive cleansing. Nothing is beyond repair.

5. Holiness in Daily Life

Leviticus 19 — the heart of the book — takes holiness out of the sanctuary and into the field, the marketplace, and the neighborhood. Leave gleanings for the poor. Pay workers on time. Don't gossip. Don't bear grudges. Love your neighbor as yourself. Holiness is relational, not merely ritual.

6. Christ in the Offerings

Every sacrifice, every priestly act, every element of Yom Kippur points forward to Christ. He is the unblemished offering, the High Priest who enters God's presence, the scapegoat who bears sins away, and the one whose blood speaks "a better word than the blood of Abel" (Hebrews 12:24).

The arc moves from God's presence → to access → to mediation → to protection → to restoration → to transformation. Leviticus is the operating manual for covenant life with God.

  1. Leviticus isn't punishment — it's invitation: Every law serves the purpose of enabling nearness to God
  2. We see Christ on every page: The offerings, the priesthood, and Yom Kippur are the grammar of the Atonement
  3. We see temple patterns: Washing, anointing, clothing, sacred space, veils, and divine presence echo through Latter-day Saint worship
  4. We see ethical holiness: "Love thy neighbour as thyself" originates here, not in the New Testament
  5. We understand the whole Bible better: Without Leviticus, Hebrews, Romans, and the Gospels lose their sacrificial vocabulary

Leviticus opens with God calling — reaching out from the Tabernacle to teach His people how to draw near. If sacrifice means "drawing close," what does your own approach to God look like? And what would it mean to take holiness out of the sanctuary and into your daily relationships — your workplace, your neighborhood, your home?

2. Leviticus Overview: Historical and Cultural Context

The entire book of Leviticus takes place at Sinai — Israel has not moved. The Tabernacle is built, God's glory has filled it, and now He speaks from within it. Leviticus covers roughly one month of narrative time, but its instructions shape centuries of Israelite worship, ethics, and identity. The book presupposes the Exodus: everything here flows from the reality that God has rescued His people and now dwells among them.

Hebrew Title

The Hebrew name Vayikra (וַיִּקְרָא) comes from the first word of the book: "And He called." Jewish tradition emphasizes this: God doesn't command coldly from a distance — He calls, personally and intimately. The Midrash notes that the calling always preceded the speaking; God gave Moses time to prepare before delivering instruction.

Greek/English Title

"Leviticus" comes from the Greek Leuitikon (pertaining to the Levites), though the book is addressed primarily to all Israel, not just the priestly tribe. The name is somewhat misleading — this is a book for the entire community.

Israel Was Not Unique in Offering Sacrifice

Every culture surrounding Israel practiced animal sacrifice — Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Canaanite. What made Israelite sacrifice distinctive was not the act but the theology behind it.

The Korban Revolution

The Hebrew word for offering — korban (קָרְבָּן) — comes from qarav, "to draw near." This reframes sacrifice entirely. The offering is not payment or appeasement. It is an act of approach — the worshiper drawing near to God. This distinction is fundamental to understanding Leviticus.

A System, Not Random Acts

Leviticus 1–7 presents five offerings in deliberate order. Together they address the full range of human need before God:

The Logic of the Sequence

The order is not accidental. It moves from:

  • Consecration (olah — total self-giving)
  • to Gratitude (minchah — tribute from daily life)
  • to Communion (shelamim — shared meal with God)
  • to Purification (chatat — dealing with sin's contamination)
  • to Restitution (asham — making things right)

The first three are voluntary acts of devotion. The last two address brokenness and restoration. Together they form a complete theology of approach.

Not About Hygiene

Modern readers often assume the clean/unclean categories (Leviticus 11–15) are primitive hygiene rules. While some overlap with health, the system is fundamentally about holiness — maintaining the symbolic boundaries that protect sacred space.

The Basic Categories

  • Clean (tahor): Normal state; eligible to approach the sanctuary
  • Unclean (tameh): Temporary state requiring purification before approaching sacred space
  • Holy (kadosh): Set apart for God's purposes

What Caused Impurity

Most sources of impurity relate to the boundary between life and death: birth, skin diseases (associated with decay), bodily discharges, and contact with death. The principle: God is the God of life, and anything associated with death's domain must be addressed before entering His presence.

Impurity Was Not Sin

Being unclean was often unavoidable — childbirth, menstruation, burying the dead. These were normal human experiences, not moral failures. The system provided a process for returning to a state where one could approach God. The problem was not impurity itself but refusing to address it.

Latter-day Saint Resonance

The principle of spiritual preparation before entering sacred space echoes in temple worship. The temple recommend interview, personal worthiness, and the act of changing into white clothing all reflect Levitical patterns of purification before approaching God's presence.

Aaron's Consecration (Leviticus 8–9)

The consecration of Aaron and his sons follows the pattern given in Exodus 29:

  • Washed with water (Lev 8:6)
  • Clothed in sacred garments (Lev 8:7-9)
  • Anointed with oil (Lev 8:12)
  • Sanctified with blood on ear, thumb, and toe (Lev 8:23-24)

Each element carries meaning: washing = purification; clothing = new identity; anointing = empowerment by the Spirit; blood on ear, thumb, toe = consecration of hearing, doing, and walking.

Nadab and Abihu (Leviticus 10)

Aaron's sons offer "strange fire" and are consumed. The episode is jarring — but it establishes a principle that runs through the entire book: God's holiness is not casual. Approach must be on His terms. This isn't arbitrary cruelty; it's the seriousness with which God treats His own presence among His people.

The Priestly Role

Priests served as the bridge between God and Israel:

  • Offering sacrifices on Israel's behalf
  • Pronouncing blessings (Num 6:22-27)
  • Teaching Torah — distinguishing holy from common, clean from unclean (Lev 10:10-11)
  • Entering God's presence so the people didn't have to risk it

The Most Sacred Day

Yom Kippur was the theological center of Israel's year — the one day when accumulated sin and impurity were comprehensively addressed. It occurred on the 10th of Tishrei (seventh month), five days before the Feast of Tabernacles.

The Ritual Sequence

  1. The High Priest bathes and dresses in plain white linen (not the ornate garments of office) — humility before God
  2. Offers a bull for his own sin — even the High Priest needs atonement
  3. Enters the Holy of Holies with incense — the smoke screens God's glory so the priest can survive
  4. Sprinkles blood on the Mercy Seat — the bull's blood for himself, then the goat's blood for Israel
  5. The scapegoat receives confession — Aaron lays both hands on it and confesses all Israel's sins
  6. The scapegoat is led into the wilderness — carried to "a land cut off" (eretz gezerah), never to return

Two Goats, One Atonement

The two goats represent the full scope of atonement:

  • The slaughtered goat: Blood pays the debt of sin — atonement through death
  • The scapegoat (Azazel): Bears sins away — removal and elimination

Sin is both covered and carried away. Christ fulfills both roles: His blood atones, and He bears our sins "as far as the east is from the west" (Psalm 103:12).

Temple Connection

The Yom Kippur pattern — passing through veils, into God's immediate presence, bearing the names of the covenant people — resonates profoundly with Latter-day Saint temple experience. The High Priest entered a space no one else could enter, on behalf of the entire community.

A Distinctive Section

Scholars call Leviticus 17–26 the "Holiness Code" (Heiligkeitsgesetz) because of its repeated refrain: "I am the LORD" and "Be holy, for I am holy." This section moves holiness from the sanctuary into every arena of life.

What the Holiness Code Covers

  • Food and blood (Lev 17): The sacredness of life/blood
  • Sexual ethics (Lev 18): Boundaries protecting family and community
  • Daily holiness (Lev 19): The ethical core — gleaning laws, honest dealing, neighbor-love
  • Penalties (Lev 20): Consequences for violations
  • Priestly standards (Lev 21–22): Higher expectations for those who serve at the altar
  • Sacred calendar (Lev 23): The appointed times (moedim) — Sabbath, Passover, Shavuot, Yom Kippur, Sukkot
  • Sabbatical and Jubilee (Lev 25): Economic rest and restoration every 7th and 50th year
  • Blessings and curses (Lev 26): Covenant consequences

The Heart of the Code: Leviticus 19

Leviticus 19 is addressed to "all the congregation" — not priests, not elders, everyone. Its commands are overwhelmingly ethical and relational:

  • Leave gleanings for the poor (19:9-10)
  • Don't steal, lie, or defraud (19:11-13)
  • Don't curse the deaf or trip the blind (19:14)
  • Judge justly (19:15)
  • Don't gossip (19:16)
  • Don't hate in your heart (19:17)
  • Love your neighbor as yourself (19:18)

The chapter ends with: "I am the LORD." Every ethical command is anchored in God's character, not social utility.

These festivals are not merely commemorative. They are called miqra'ei kodesh — "holy convocations" or "sacred rehearsals." Each one rehearses a dimension of God's redemptive work and points toward its ultimate fulfillment in Christ.

Leviticus sits at the center of the Torah — literally and theologically. It addresses the most practical question the Exodus raises: How do sinful, mortal people live in the presence of a holy God?

The answer involves:

  • Sacrifice as the means of drawing near
  • Priesthood as the bridge between divine holiness and human frailty
  • Purity as the boundary that protects sacred space
  • Atonement as the comprehensive solution to accumulated sin
  • Holiness as both ritual separation and ethical transformation
  • Festivals as the sacred rhythm that keeps covenant memory alive

Far from being an outdated manual of ancient ritual, Leviticus provides the theological vocabulary for the Atonement, the patterns for temple worship, and the ethical vision that Jesus inherited and intensified. Without it, the New Testament's language of blood, sacrifice, priesthood, and holiness has no foundation.

3. Leviticus Overview: Key Passages Study

The Text

"And the LORD called unto Moses, and spake unto him out of the tabernacle of the congregation, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, If any man of you bring an offering unto the LORD, ye shall bring your offering of the cattle, even of the herd, and of the flock. If his offering be a burnt sacrifice of the herd, let him offer a male without blemish: he shall offer it of his own voluntary will at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation before the LORD. And he shall put his hand upon the head of the burnt offering; and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him."

Key Hebrew

  • vayikra (וַיִּקְרָא) — "And He called" — intimate address, not distant command
  • korban (קָרְבָּן) — offering — from qarav, "to draw near"
  • olah (עֹלָה) — burnt offering — from alah, "to ascend, go up"
  • tamim (תָּמִים) — without blemish — whole, complete, perfect
  • lirtzono (לִרְצֹנוֹ) — "of his own voluntary will" — or "for his acceptance/favor"
  • samakh (סָמַךְ) — to lean, press heavily — hand laid on the animal's head

What It Meant in Context

The book opens with God calling — not Moses approaching, but God initiating. The first instruction is about korban, drawing near through offering. The burnt offering (olah) was the most comprehensive sacrifice: entirely consumed, nothing kept back. The worshiper chose the animal, brought it voluntarily, pressed his hand on its head (an act of identification — "this represents me"), and watched it ascend in smoke and fire.

The word lirtzono can mean both "of his own free will" and "for his acceptance." Both readings work: the offering is voluntary, and its purpose is to make the worshiper acceptable before God. Sacrifice is not coercion — it is willing approach.

Theological Significance

  1. God initiates: The first word is God calling. Sacrifice isn't humanity reaching up; it's God teaching us how to come close.
  2. Without blemish: The offering must be tamim — whole, complete. You don't give God your leftovers.
  3. Identification: The hand-pressing (semikhah) creates connection between worshiper and animal. The animal's fate represents the worshiper's self-offering.
  4. Complete surrender: The olah ascends entirely. Nothing is retained. This is total consecration.

Christ-Centered Reading

Christ is the ultimate olah — without blemish (tamim), offered voluntarily (lirtzono), ascending entirely to God. The hand-pressing foreshadows our identification with Christ: He takes our place; we participate in His offering. "He hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin" (2 Cor 5:21). The olah principle — holding nothing back — is what Christ did perfectly and what He invites us to do: "Offer your whole souls as an offering unto him" (Omni 1:26).

Modern Application

What does it mean to hold nothing back from God? The olah asks: Is there an area of your life you've kept off the altar — a relationship, an ambition, a habit? Complete consecration doesn't mean self-destruction; it means nothing withheld.

The Text

"And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, If a soul shall sin through ignorance against any of the commandments of the LORD concerning things which ought not to be done, and shall do against any of them..." (4:1-2)

"And if any one of the common people sin through ignorance, while he doeth somewhat against any of the commandments of the LORD... then he shall bring his offering, a kid of the goats, a female without blemish, for his sin which he hath sinned. And he shall lay his hand upon the head of the sin offering... And the priest shall make an atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him." (4:27-31)

Key Hebrew

  • chatat (חַטָּאת) — sin offering — from root chata', "to miss the mark, go wrong"
  • bishgagah (בִשְׁגָגָה) — through ignorance, unintentionally — not willful defiance
  • kipper (כִּפֶּר) — to atone, make atonement — to cover, purge, ransom
  • venislach (וְנִסְלַח) — "and it shall be forgiven" — passive: forgiveness comes from God

What It Meant in Context

The sin offering addressed unintentional sin — what happens when someone violates a commandment without realizing it. The Hebrew bishgagah covers errors, negligence, and ignorance. The offering varied by the offender's status: a bull for the High Priest (whose sin affects the whole sanctuary), a male goat for the community, a male goat for a ruler, a female goat or lamb for an ordinary person.

The key action: the priest applied the blood to purify sacred objects — the horns of the altar, the veil, the Mercy Seat. This reveals something remarkable: sin doesn't just damage the sinner; it contaminates sacred space. The chatat purifies the sanctuary so God can continue to dwell among His people.

Theological Significance

  1. Unintentional sin still matters: Ignorance doesn't neutralize the effect of sin. Even unknown violations create real consequences that require real atonement.
  2. Blood purifies sacred space: Sin's pollution reaches God's dwelling. The chatat cleanses what sin has contaminated.
  3. Graduated responsibility: The higher the offender's status, the greater the required offering. Leaders bear more responsibility for sin's communal impact.
  4. "It shall be forgiven him": The passive voice matters — forgiveness comes from God, not from the ritual itself. The sacrifice is the vehicle; God is the source.

Christ-Centered Reading

Paul writes that God made Christ "to be sin for us" (2 Cor 5:21) — the Greek echoes the Septuagint language for the chatat. Christ became the sin offering. His blood doesn't merely cover individual guilt; it purifies the heavenly sanctuary itself (Hebrews 9:23-24). And the promise still holds: "It shall be forgiven."

The chatat also addresses a pastoral reality: we sin without knowing it. We carry blind spots, cultural assumptions, inherited attitudes that offend God's holiness. The sin offering provides for what we can't see in ourselves — and Christ's atonement covers what we don't yet recognize.

Modern Application

Are there sins you don't know about? Attitudes, prejudices, neglects you haven't yet seen? Leviticus 4 teaches that God provides atonement even for what we're unaware of. This isn't license for carelessness — it's assurance that God's mercy is wider than our self-awareness.

The Text

"And the LORD said unto Moses, Speak unto Aaron thy brother, that he come not at all times into the holy place within the veil before the mercy seat, which is upon the ark; that he die not: for I will appear in the cloud upon the mercy seat." (16:2)

"And he shall take the two goats, and present them before the LORD at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation. And Aaron shall cast lots upon the two goats; one lot for the LORD, and the other lot for the scapegoat." (16:7-8)

"And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness: And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited." (16:21-22)

"For on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before the LORD." (16:30)

Key Hebrew

  • kapporet (כַּפֹּרֶת) — Mercy Seat — from kipper, "to atone"
  • la'Azazel (לַעֲזָאזֵל) — for Azazel / the scapegoat — meaning debated
  • eretz gezerah (אֶרֶץ גְּזֵרָה) — "a land cut off" — the wilderness of no return
  • Shabbat Shabbaton (שַׁבַּת שַׁבָּתוֹן) — Sabbath of complete rest — the most emphatic Sabbath language in Torah
  • innu et nafshoteikhem (עִנּוּ אֶת נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם) — "afflict your souls" — deep self-examination, traditionally fasting

What It Meant in Context

Yom Kippur was the theological center of Israel's year. Once annually, accumulated sin and impurity received comprehensive treatment. The High Priest — and only the High Priest — entered the Holy of Holies. He changed from his ornate garments into plain white linen: before God, rank is stripped away.

The two goats represent the full scope of atonement:

  • The LORD's goat: Slaughtered; its blood sprinkled on the Mercy Seat and the altar. Blood pays the debt.
  • The Azazel goat (scapegoat): Receives the confession of all Israel's sins through the laying on of hands, then is sent into the wilderness to "a land cut off." Sin is removed — carried where it can never return.

Together: sin is both atoned for and eliminated. The debt is paid AND the burden is carried away.

The people fasted and did no work — Shabbat Shabbaton, the most intense rest of the year. They could contribute nothing. Atonement was God's work through the priest.

Theological Significance

  1. Only the High Priest enters: No one else can approach the Mercy Seat. Access to God's immediate presence requires a consecrated mediator.
  2. White linen, not glory garments: Before God, the High Priest strips away status. Humility is the prerequisite for atonement.
  3. Two goats, one atonement: The dual ritual means sin is both paid for (blood) and removed (scapegoat). Incomplete atonement — paying without removing, or removing without paying — is insufficient.
  4. "A land not inhabited": Sin is sent where no one lives. It doesn't come back. It doesn't get recycled. It's gone.
  5. The people do nothing: They fast and rest. Atonement is received, not achieved.

Christ-Centered Reading

The book of Hebrews reads Yom Kippur as the blueprint for Christ's work:

  • Christ is both the High Priest who enters and the sacrifice whose blood is offered (Heb 9:11-12)
  • He enters the heavenly Holy of Holies "once for all" — not annually, but permanently (Heb 9:24-26)
  • He fulfills both goats: the blood that atones and the one who bears sins away (Heb 9:28; Isa 53:4-6)

The scapegoat carried sins to eretz gezerah — the land cut off. Christ carried our sins to a place no one else could go: Gethsemane and Golgotha, the cut-off land.

Modern Application

Do you believe your sins are actually gone — carried to a land not inhabited — or do you keep retrieving them from the wilderness? The scapegoat didn't come back. Christ's atonement is complete. Yom Kippur teaches: you can stop carrying what has already been carried away.

The Text

"And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Speak unto all the congregation of the children of Israel, and say unto them, Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy." (19:1-2)

"And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleanings of thy harvest... thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger: I am the LORD your God." (19:9-10)

"Thou shalt not defraud thy neighbour, neither rob him: the wages of him that is hired shall not abide with thee all night until the morning." (19:13)

"Thou shalt not curse the deaf, nor put a stumblingblock before the blind, but shalt fear thy God: I am the LORD." (19:14)

"Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD." (19:18)

Key Hebrew

  • kedoshim tihyu (קְדֹשִׁים תִּהְיוּ) — "You shall be holy" — plural, addressed to the entire community
  • pe'ah (פֵּאָה) — corner — the field-edges left for the poor
  • leqet (לֶקֶט) — gleanings — fallen grain left on the ground
  • ve'ahavta lere'akha kamokha (וְאָהַבְתָּ לְרֵעֲךָ כָּמוֹךָ) — "love your neighbor as yourself"
  • ani YHWH (אֲנִי יְהוָה) — "I am the LORD" — appears 16 times in this chapter

What It Meant in Context

Leviticus 19 is addressed to "all the congregation" — not to priests, not to leaders, but to every Israelite. The command "Be holy" is immediately followed not by more ritual law but by ethics: honor parents, keep the Sabbath, don't make idols (19:3-4). Then it moves into economic justice, social care, honest dealing, and neighbor-love.

The gleaning law (19:9-10) is practical holiness: don't harvest to the edges of your field; leave the corners and dropped grain for the poor and the immigrant. This is the law that brings Ruth to Boaz's field (Ruth 2) and changes the course of redemptive history.

The repeated refrain "I am the LORD" (16 times in the chapter) anchors every command in God's identity. You don't leave gleanings because it's good economics. You don't pay workers on time because of labor law. You do these things because I am the LORD — and holiness means reflecting His character in every relationship.

The climax — "love thy neighbour as thyself" (19:18) — comes after a sequence that prohibits hatred in the heart (19:17), vengeance, and grudges. Love isn't sentiment. It is the culmination of a whole pattern of not hating, not exploiting, not ignoring — active, practical, daily regard for the other person.

Theological Significance

  1. Holiness is democratized: Not a priestly specialty but a communal calling
  2. Holiness is ethical: The content is overwhelmingly about relationships, economics, and justice — not ritual performance
  3. Holiness is grounded in God's character: "Be holy because I am holy" — not "because it's useful" or "because society functions better"
  4. Interior and exterior together: Don't hate in your heart (19:17) AND don't defraud your neighbor (19:13). Holiness addresses both hidden thoughts and visible actions.

Christ-Centered Reading

Jesus quotes Leviticus 19:18 as the second great commandment (Matt 22:39). He didn't invent neighbor-love — He inherited and universalized it. The command "Be ye therefore perfect" (Matt 5:48) echoes kedoshim tihyu. The Sermon on the Mount is Leviticus 19 intensified and expanded.

The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) is Jesus' commentary on "Who is my neighbor?" — answering the question already latent in Leviticus 19:34, which extends neighbor-love to the stranger: "thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt."

Modern Application

Leviticus 19 asks: What does holiness look like not in the sanctuary but in your daily life? In your field (do you leave margins for others?), your business (do you pay fairly and promptly?), your speech (do you gossip?), your heart (do you harbor grudges?). Holiness isn't a Sunday concept. It's a Monday-through-Saturday reality.

The Texts

"For on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before the LORD." (16:30)

"Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy." (19:2)

What These Verses Represent Together

These two verses anchor the two great movements of Leviticus: cleansing and calling.

Leviticus 16:30 promises comprehensive purification — you may be clean from all your sins. Not some. Not the small ones. All.

Leviticus 19:2 issues the most demanding call in Scripture — be holy as God is holy. Not merely acceptable. Holy.

Together they define the covenant life: God cleanses what we cannot clean, and then calls us into a holiness we could never generate alone. Atonement makes holiness possible. Holiness is the purpose of atonement.

Modern Application

We often emphasize one at the expense of the other. Cleansing without calling produces cheap grace — forgiveness with no transformation. Calling without cleansing produces despair — a standard we can never reach. Leviticus holds both: you are forgiven AND you are called to be holy. The two are inseparable.

4. Leviticus Overview: Word Studies

Root: קרב (qarav), "to draw near, approach"

Appears: Leviticus 1:2, 3, 10, 14; 2:1; 3:1; and throughout

Meaning

Korban is the general word for an offering brought to God. It does not mean "sacrifice" in the sense of destruction or loss. It means "that which is brought near" — or more precisely, the vehicle by which the worshiper draws near to God. The root qarav appears over 280 times in the Hebrew Bible, always carrying the sense of approach, proximity, or closeness.

Why This Word Matters

The entire sacrificial system of Leviticus is reframed by this single word. When we read "sacrifice" in English, we hear loss, pain, giving up. When an ancient Israelite heard korban, they heard coming close. Every offering — whether burnt, grain, peace, sin, or guilt — was fundamentally an act of approach. The animal wasn't the point. Drawing near to God was the point.

Old Testament Echoes

  • Psalm 73:28: "It is good for me to draw near (qarov) to God"
  • Isaiah 58:2: "They seek me daily, and delight to know my ways... they take delight in approaching (qarav) God"
  • Ezekiel 44:15: The faithful priests "shall come near (qarav) to me to minister unto me"

Theological Significance

  1. Sacrifice is invitation, not punishment: God designed the system to bring people closer, not to keep them at bay
  2. The worshiper moves toward God: Korban implies action, will, and desire — not passive compliance
  3. Every offering is relational: The goal isn't transaction but connection

LDS Application

Temple worship follows the same logic: we don't go to the temple to perform rituals but to draw near to God. Sacrament, prayer, and covenant-making are all forms of korban — vehicles of approach. The question isn't "What am I giving up?" but "How close am I drawing?"

Root: עלה (alah), "to go up, ascend"

Appears: Leviticus 1:3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 13, 14, 17; 6:9-13

Meaning

The olah is the burnt offering — entirely consumed by fire on the altar. Unlike other offerings where portions went to the priest or worshiper, the olah ascended completely. Nothing was retained. The smoke rising from the altar represented the offering going up to God.

The Perpetual Fire

"The fire shall ever be burning upon the altar; it shall never go out" (Lev 6:13). The olah was offered every morning and evening (tamid — the "continual" offering), creating a perpetual connection between Israel and God. The fire that consumed the olah was never allowed to die.

Theological Significance

  1. Total consecration: The olah holds nothing back. It is the sacrifice of complete surrender.
  2. Ascending to God: The directional language matters — the offering goes up. It moves toward God.
  3. Voluntary: "Of his own voluntary will" (lirtzono, Lev 1:3). Complete surrender cannot be coerced.
  4. Continual connection: The perpetual fire represents unbroken covenant relationship — "always having the Spirit."

LDS Application

The olah principle undergirds the covenant of consecration. We offer not 10% or 90% but everything — time, talent, means, will. "Offer your whole souls as an offering unto him" (Omni 1:26). The perpetual fire echoes the sacrament prayer's promise: "always remember him... always have his Spirit."

Root: חטא (chata'), "to miss, go wrong, sin"

Appears: Leviticus 4:3, 8, 14, 20, 21, 24, 25, 26, 28, 29, 32, 33, 34, 35

Meaning

Chatat means both "sin" and "sin offering" — the same word for the disease and the cure. The root chata' literally means "to miss the mark" — like an archer whose arrow goes wide. Sin in Hebrew isn't primarily rebellion (though that exists); it's going off course, failing to hit the target of God's will.

The Purification Function

Modern scholarship increasingly translates chatat as "purification offering" rather than "sin offering," because the blood was applied not to the sinner but to sacred objects — the altar, the veil, the Mercy Seat. The chatat purified the sanctuary from the contamination that sin causes. Sin doesn't just wound the sinner; it pollutes God's dwelling place.

Theological Significance

  1. Sin has spatial consequences: It contaminates sacred space, threatening God's willingness to dwell among Israel
  2. Blood purifies: The blood of the chatat cleanses what sin has defiled — restoring the conditions for God's presence
  3. Unintentional sin still matters: The chatat addresses bishgagah — sins committed in ignorance. Unknown sin has real effects.
  4. The word is both problem and solution: Chatat means sin AND the offering that addresses it. God names the cure with the same word as the disease.

LDS Application

The principle that sin contaminates sacred space resonates with temple worthiness. We prepare spiritually before entering the temple not because God is arbitrary but because sin and holiness cannot coexist unchecked. The sacrament functions as a weekly chatat — purification that restores our covenant standing and keeps our personal sacred space clean.

Root: כפר (kaphar) — possibly related to "cover" or "ransom"

Related: כַּפֹּרֶת (kapporet) — Mercy Seat; כִּפּוּר (kippur) — atonement (as in Yom Kippur)

Appears: Leviticus 1:4; 4:20, 26, 31, 35; 5:6, 10, 13, 16, 18; 16:6, 10, 11, 16, 17, 18, 24, 27, 30, 32, 33, 34

Meaning

Kipper is the central verb of atonement in the Hebrew Bible. Its meaning is debated — "to cover," "to ransom," "to purge/wipe clean" — but all three dimensions are present in Leviticus. Atonement covers sin from God's sight, ransoms the sinner from death's claim, and purges the sanctuary from contamination.

The Frequency

Kipper appears nearly 50 times in Leviticus alone — more than any other book. Leviticus is saturated with atonement because the problem it addresses (sinful people living with a holy God) demands constant, comprehensive covering.

The Mercy Seat Connection

The kapporet — the golden lid of the Ark — gets its name from this root. The place where God meets Israel (Ex 25:22) is literally the "place of atonement." God's meeting-point with humanity is defined by mercy, not judgment.

Theological Significance

  1. Atonement is multi-dimensional: Not merely forgiveness, but covering, ransoming, and purifying — all at once
  2. God provides the means: The sacrificial system isn't human invention but divine gift
  3. The Mercy Seat defines the meeting place: Where God and humanity connect, atonement is the foundation
  4. It's God's most-used verb in Leviticus: Atonement isn't one theme among many — it's the infrastructure

LDS Application

"The Atonement of Jesus Christ" preserves this Hebrew richness: Christ covers, ransoms, and purges. He doesn't merely forgive — He addresses sin from every angle. The word at-one-ment captures the relational goal: making one what sin has divided.

Root: קדש (qadash), "to be holy, set apart, consecrate"

Related: קֶדֶשׁ (qodesh) — holiness, holy thing; מִקְדָּשׁ (miqdash) — sanctuary

Appears: Leviticus 11:44, 45; 19:2; 20:7, 26; 21:8; and throughout

Meaning

Kadosh means "set apart, distinct, other." God is kadosh — fundamentally different from everything created. When Israel is called to be kadosh, they are called to share in God's distinctiveness, to be set apart as He is set apart.

The Leviticus Refrain

"Ye shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" appears in Leviticus 11:44-45, 19:2, 20:7, and 20:26. It frames the purity laws (ch. 11), the ethical code (ch. 19), and the community standards (ch. 20). Holiness is the thread that connects ritual, ethics, and identity.

What Holiness Is NOT

Holiness in Leviticus is not:

  • Merely moral perfection (though it includes ethics)
  • Withdrawal from the world (Israel lives among nations)
  • Grim asceticism (the peace offering is a joyful shared meal)

Holiness IS: reflecting God's character in every dimension of life — worship, work, relationships, food, sexuality, economics, speech.

Theological Significance

  1. God's holiness is the standard: "Be holy because I am holy" — not "because it's virtuous" or "because it's useful"
  2. Holiness is communal: Kedoshim tihyu is plural — addressed to the entire congregation, not individuals
  3. Holiness is both gift and demand: God sets Israel apart (gift) and then calls them to live accordingly (demand)
  4. Holiness connects ritual and ethics: The same word covers Tabernacle sanctity and neighbor-love

LDS Application

"Holiness to the Lord" — inscribed on every Latter-day Saint temple — is Kodesh l'YHWH, the same phrase engraved on the High Priest's golden plate (Ex 28:36). The temple declares in Hebrew what Leviticus demands in practice: everything set apart for God. Holiness in Latter-day Saint theology is not an abstract ideal but an active covenant — sanctification through the Spirit, lived out in daily discipleship.

Root: Debated — possibly עז (ez, goat) + אזל (azal, to go away); or a proper name

Appears: Leviticus 16:8, 10 (twice), 26

Meaning

Azazel is one of the most debated words in the Hebrew Bible. Three major interpretations exist:

  1. Rashi: A rocky cliff in the wilderness where the goat was sent
  2. Ibn Ezra / Nahmanides: A wilderness entity or force representing the realm of chaos and sin — the goat is sent back to the domain of disorder
  3. Etymological: "The goat that goes away" — from ez (goat) + azal (to depart)

The Scapegoat Ritual

The Azazel goat receives the confession of all Israel's sins through the High Priest's hand-pressing. It is then led into the wilderness — to eretz gezerah ("a land cut off"). The goat bears the sins away and does not return.

Theological Significance

  1. Sin is removed, not just forgiven: The scapegoat carries sin where it cannot come back
  2. Comprehensive transfer: "All the iniquities... all their transgressions in all their sins" — the language is exhaustive
  3. Directional theology: Sin goes away from God's presence, away from the camp, away from the people — into nothingness

LDS Application

The scapegoat answers the question every penitent asks: "Are my sins really gone?" Leviticus says yes — carried to a place of no return. "As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us" (Psalm 103:12). Christ carries what the Azazel goat symbolized — sin borne away permanently. Remembering past sins after repentance is, in a sense, chasing the scapegoat into the wilderness to bring it back.

5. Leviticus Overview: Jewish Perspective

Jewish tradition has a surprising relationship with Leviticus. The book that many modern readers find most difficult was, according to the Midrash, the first book taught to children. The Talmud records: "Why do young children begin their Torah study with Torat Kohanim (Leviticus)? Because children are pure, and the sacrifices are pure — let the pure come and occupy themselves with the pure" (Leviticus Rabbah 7:3). Far from being obscure or unapproachable, Leviticus was considered the foundation of Jewish education precisely because its themes — purity, holiness, and drawing near to God — are the most essential.

This section explores how Jewish tradition reads Leviticus, especially its theology of sacrifice, atonement, and holiness.

A Scribal Tradition

In Torah scrolls, the first word of Leviticus — וַיִּקְרָא (Vayikra) — is traditionally written with a small aleph (א). Several explanations are offered:

Humility of Moses: Moses, in his humility, wanted to write Vayikar ("and He happened upon," as with Balaam in Numbers 23:4) instead of Vayikra ("and He called"), to avoid suggesting a special relationship with God. God insisted on the full word; Moses made the aleph small as a compromise.

Theological Reading: The small aleph teaches that God's calling is gentle, not overwhelming. He calls in a way that can be heard without being crushed. The voice that filled the Tabernacle stopped at its walls — Israel outside didn't hear it. God modulates His revelation to what we can bear.

LDS Resonance

The "still small voice" (1 Kings 19:12; 1 Nephi 17:45) resonates with this tradition. God's most important communications often come quietly, calibrated to the hearer's capacity.

Maimonides: Accommodation

Maimonides (Rambam, 12th century) argued that sacrifice was a divine concession. Israel, having lived in Egypt for generations, knew no worship without animal offering. God met them where they were, redirecting what they already understood toward proper worship of YHWH. Sacrifice was, in Maimonides' view, a transitional institution — necessary at Sinai, ultimately superseded by prayer.

Nachmanides: Intrinsic Meaning

Nachmanides (Ramban, 13th century) disagreed sharply. Sacrifice wasn't concession but revelation. The act of laying hands on the animal, watching it die, seeing the blood — this was meant to impress on the sinner: "This should have been me." The substitutionary logic was the point, not an accommodation. Each element carried inherent symbolic weight.

The Tension

The debate between Maimonides and Nachmanides shapes Jewish thought to this day. Was sacrifice a temporary pedagogical tool, or did it carry eternal theological meaning? Most traditional authorities lean toward Nachmanides — sacrifice was always meaningful, and its loss (with the Temple's destruction) is genuinely mourned.

LDS Resonance

Latter-day Saint theology affirms the Nachmanides position: sacrifice pointed to Christ and carried real, typological meaning (Alma 34:14: "the whole meaning of the law, every whit pointing to that great and last sacrifice"). The sacrificial system wasn't a placeholder but a prophecy enacted.

The Crisis of 70 CE

When the Romans destroyed the Second Temple, the entire sacrificial system ended. No altar, no priests officiating, no korbanot. The rabbis faced an existential question: How do we draw near to God without sacrifice?

The Rabbinic Solution

The Talmud records Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai's response: "We have another atonement equal to sacrifice — acts of lovingkindness (gemilut chasadim)" (Avot de-Rabbi Natan 4:5).

The synagogue became a mikdash me'at — "a small sanctuary" (from Ezekiel 11:16). The three daily prayer services were structured to correspond to the three daily sacrificial times:

  • Shacharit (morning prayer) = morning tamid offering
  • Mincha (afternoon prayer) = afternoon tamid offering
  • Ma'ariv (evening prayer) = the burning of fats and limbs through the night

The Siddur and Leviticus

To this day, traditional Jewish prayer books include recitation of the sacrificial portions of Leviticus as part of the morning service. Jews literally read Leviticus as prayer — keeping the memory of sacrifice alive and expressing longing for its restoration.

LDS Resonance

The Latter-day Saint understanding echoes both positions: sacrifice pointed to Christ (past), and prayer and sacrament continue the pattern of korban (present). The sacrament table functions as a kind of altar, the sacrament prayers as a form of sacrificial liturgy, and the covenant renewal they effect mirrors what the tamid and festival offerings accomplished.

The Holiest Day

Yom Kippur remains the most sacred day in the Jewish calendar — even for secular Jews, it carries enormous cultural and spiritual weight. It is the one day when even non-observant Jews often fast and attend synagogue.

The Avodah Service

The central liturgical moment of Yom Kippur is the Avodah — a detailed recitation of the High Priest's service from Leviticus 16. The congregation relives the entire ritual: the white linen, the bull for the priest's sin, the two goats, the entry into the Holy of Holies, the sprinkling of blood, the confession over the scapegoat.

At the moment in the recitation when the High Priest pronounced the divine name (YHWH) — which happened only on Yom Kippur, only in the Holy of Holies — the congregation prostrates. This is one of the only times Jews bow fully in worship (apart from Rosh Hashanah).

Teshuvah — Return

The Hebrew word for repentance — teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה) — means "return." It's not self-flagellation but homecoming. The ten days between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur (the Yamim Nora'im, "Days of Awe") are dedicated to self-examination, apology, reconciliation, and return to God.

A critical principle: Yom Kippur atones for sins between humans and God, but NOT for sins between one person and another — until the offended party has been appeased. You cannot fast your way out of an unresolved wrong against your neighbor. This links Leviticus 16 directly to Leviticus 19.

LDS Resonance

The Latter-day Saint repentance process reflects this: confession to God addresses vertical sins, but reconciliation with others is required for horizontal ones. The Savior's teaching — "first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift" (Matt 5:24) — is Yom Kippur ethics in Gospel language.

The Great Principle of Torah

Rabbi Akiva (2nd century CE) identified Leviticus 19:18 — "Love thy neighbour as thyself" — as klal gadol ba-Torah, "the great principle of the Torah." Ben Azzai countered that Genesis 5:1 — "This is the book of the generations of Adam... in the likeness of God made He him" — was even greater, because neighbor-love based on God's image includes everyone, not just those we naturally call "neighbor."

Ramban's Warning

Nachmanides offered a famous reading of Leviticus 19:2: without the general command "Be holy," a person could technically observe every specific law while remaining "a scoundrel with the Torah's permission" (naval birshut ha-Torah). One could eat only kosher food — gluttonously. One could avoid forbidden relationships — while being crude and exploitative in permitted ones. "Be holy" is the umbrella demand that no technical compliance can satisfy without genuine moral transformation.

The Structure of Leviticus 19

Jewish commentators note that Leviticus 19 mirrors the Ten Commandments:

LDS Resonance

The temple covenant of consecration — giving all that we have and are to the building of the kingdom — is the institutional expression of kedoshim tihyu. Holiness isn't a feeling; it's a covenant commitment to live differently in every dimension.

The Ultimate Paradox

While the red heifer ritual appears in Numbers 19, it belongs theologically to Leviticus's purity system. The paradox: the person who prepares the purification water becomes impure, while the impure person who receives it becomes clean. Even Solomon reportedly said, "I have labored to understand the Torah and have succeeded — except for the red heifer."

Rabbinic Acceptance

The rabbis classified the red heifer as a chok — a statute whose reason is beyond human understanding. Rashi: God says, "I have enacted a statute, I have decreed a decree, and you are not permitted to transgress it." Some commandments serve purposes we can fully understand; others ask for trust.

LDS Resonance

Some elements of covenant worship — including temple ordinances — carry meaning that deepens over time and may not be fully understood at first encounter. The willingness to participate faithfully before understanding completely is itself an act of holiness.

Jewish tradition finds in Leviticus:

  1. The foundation of education: Pure children study the pure book — Leviticus is where Torah learning begins
  2. Sacrifice as deep theology: Whether accommodation (Maimonides) or revelation (Nachmanides), sacrifice carries real meaning
  3. Prayer as continuity: When the Temple fell, prayer inherited the sacrificial structure
  4. Yom Kippur as the hinge of the year: Comprehensive atonement, requiring both divine mercy and human reconciliation
  5. Holiness as total life transformation: Not technical compliance but genuine moral change
  6. Mystery as trust: Some commands ask for obedience before understanding

The Jewish reading of Leviticus corrects two common Christian misreadings: that the law was merely burdensome, and that sacrifice was merely primitive. For Jewish tradition, Leviticus is the book of sacred nearness — God teaching His people how to live in His presence without being consumed.

6. Leviticus Overview: Teaching Applications

Leviticus can feel inaccessible — ancient ritual, unfamiliar vocabulary, detailed procedure. The teaching goal is to help readers and students see what lies beneath the surface: a God who wants to be near, a system designed to make that nearness possible, and a vision of holiness that extends into every corner of life. Approach Leviticus not as a book of rules but as a book of relationship.

For Young Children (Ages 3–7)

Focus: God wants to be close to us

Story (simplified): "After God came to live in the special tent with His people, He taught them how to visit Him. He said, 'Bring Me a gift — not because I need it, but because it's how you come close to Me.' The people brought animals and bread and oil. Every gift was a way of saying, 'I want to be near You, God.' And God said, 'I want you to be near Me, too.'"

Object Lesson: Use a night-light. Turn off the lights. "God is like this light. He wants us close. But we have to walk toward the light — we have to choose to come near. The offerings in Leviticus were like taking steps toward God's light."

Song: "I Love to See the Temple" (CS 95)

Testimony: Heavenly Father doesn't want to be far away. Everything He teaches us — prayer, church, the temple — is His way of helping us come closer.

For Older Children (Ages 8��11)

Focus: Saying sorry and making things right

Read: Leviticus 4:27-31 (summarized): "If someone did something wrong without meaning to, they could bring an offering and the priest would help make things right with God."

Discussion:

  1. Have you ever done something wrong without realizing it? How did you find out?
  2. What does it mean that God provided a way to fix mistakes — even ones we didn't know about?
  3. How do we "make things right" today? (Prayer, repentance, the sacrament, apologizing)

Activity: Write a "making things right" list: someone to apologize to, something to fix, a way to be kinder this week.

Connection: God always provides a way back. The sin offering in Leviticus and the sacrament today serve the same purpose — restoring our relationship with God.

For Youth/Teens

Focus: What does holiness look like on a Tuesday?

Read: Leviticus 19:9-18

Discussion:

  1. This is God's definition of "holiness." What surprises you about it?
  2. Which of these commands is hardest for you personally? (Not gossiping? Not holding grudges? Leaving "margins" for others?)
  3. Leviticus 19 says "I am the LORD" after almost every command. Why?
  4. How does "love thy neighbour as thyself" connect to holiness?

Challenge: Pick one command from Leviticus 19 and live it deliberately this week. Not gossip (19:16)? Pay someone fairly (19:13)? Don't hold a grudge (19:18)? Report back next week.

Lesson Approach 1: Sacrifice as Drawing Near

Opening Question: "What comes to mind when you hear the word 'sacrifice'? Now — what if the word actually means 'drawing near'?"

Key Scripture: Leviticus 1:1-4

Background: Explain korban (from qarav, to draw near). Sacrifice in Leviticus wasn't appeasement — it was approach. Every offering was a vehicle for the worshiper to come closer to God.

Discussion:

  1. How does reframing "sacrifice" as "drawing near" change how you read Leviticus?
  2. What are our modern korbanot — the things we bring to God to draw near? (Prayer, temple worship, sacrament, service, tithing)
  3. The burnt offering (olah) was entirely consumed — nothing held back. What would total consecration look like in your life?
  4. Leviticus 1:3 says "of his own voluntary will." Why does God insist that drawing near must be voluntary?

Application: Every act of worship is korban — not loss, but approach. This week, reframe one act of "sacrifice" (tithing, service, temple attendance) as an act of drawing near.

Testimony: God designed an entire system to make closeness possible. He wants us near — and He provides the way.

Lesson Approach 2: The Day of Atonement and Christ

Read together: Leviticus 16:7-10, 21-22, 30

Key Point: Two goats, one atonement. The first goat's blood pays the debt; the second goat carries sins away to a land of no return.

Discussion:

  1. Why two goats? What would be missing if there were only one?
  2. The scapegoat was sent to "a land not inhabited" — sins carried where they can never return. Do you live as if your sins are truly gone?
  3. The High Priest wore plain white linen — not his glory garments. What does this teach about approaching God?
  4. The people fasted and did no work. Atonement was received, not earned. How does this connect to grace?

Read: Hebrews 9:11-12, 24-26. Christ fulfills both goats: the blood that atones and the one who bears sins away — once for all.

Application: If the scapegoat didn't come back, why do we keep retrieving our forgiven sins from the wilderness? Christ's atonement is complete. Believe it.

Lesson Approach 3: Holiness in Daily Life

Opening Question: "If someone asked you to define holiness, what would you say? Now let's see what God says."

Read together: Leviticus 19:2-4, 9-11, 13-14, 17-18

Key Point: God's definition of holiness is overwhelmingly ethical and relational — not ritual performance.

Discussion:

  1. How much of Leviticus 19 is about ritual, and how much is about relationships?
  2. Verse 14: "Don't curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind." What does this mean beyond the literal? (Don't exploit people's vulnerabilities)
  3. Verse 17: "Don't hate thy brother in thine heart." Why does God address the interior life alongside the exterior?
  4. "I am the LORD" appears 16 times. What's God saying by repeating that after every command?
  5. How does "love thy neighbour as thyself" serve as the summary of everything before it?

Application: Holiness isn't a temple-only concept. It's how you treat your coworker, your neighbor, the person who wronged you. Pick one relationship this week and apply Leviticus 19 to it.

7. Leviticus Overview: Study Questions

Drawing Near: The Offerings (Leviticus 1–7)

  1. The Hebrew word for offering — korban — means "drawing near." How does this change the way you think about sacrifice in your own life?
  1. The burnt offering (olah) was entirely consumed — nothing held back. What area of your life have you kept off the altar?
  1. The sin offering (Leviticus 4) addressed unintentional sin — wrongs the person didn't even know about. What does God's provision for unseen sin teach you about His mercy?
  1. The peace offering (shelamim) was a shared meal — God, priest, and worshiper eating together. How is communion with God part of your worship life?

The Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16)

  1. On Yom Kippur, the High Priest changed from ornate garments into plain white linen. What does this teach about approaching God?
  1. The scapegoat carried Israel's sins to "a land not inhabited" — a place of no return. Do you live as if your repented sins are truly gone? What would change if you did?
  1. The people fasted and did no work on Yom Kippur. Atonement was entirely God's work through the priest. How comfortable are you receiving grace you didn't earn?
  1. Leviticus 16:30 promises cleansing "from all your sins." Not some. Not the respectable ones. All. What sin do you struggle to believe Christ can actually carry away?

Holiness in Daily Life (Leviticus 19)

  1. Leviticus 19:2 says "Be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy." This is addressed to the whole congregation — not just priests. What does it mean that holiness is everyone's calling?
  1. Read Leviticus 19:9-10 (gleaning laws). What are the "corners of your field" — the margins you leave for others in your time, money, and attention?
  1. Leviticus 19:14 says not to curse the deaf or trip the blind. Beyond the literal meaning, how do we exploit people's vulnerabilities today?
  1. Leviticus 19:17 prohibits hating "in thine heart." Is there someone you treat politely while harboring resentment? What would Leviticus 19 say about that?
  1. "Love thy neighbour as thyself" (Lev 19:18) comes from Leviticus, not the New Testament. How does knowing this change how you read the Old Testament?
  1. Read Leviticus 1:1-2 together. God called to Moses from the Tabernacle. He didn't wait for Moses to come to Him — He spoke first. What does this teach about who God is?
  1. **Explain the word korban (offering = "drawing near").** What are our family's ways of drawing near to God? (Prayer, scriptures, temple, church, service) Which one means the most to our family right now?
  1. Read Leviticus 16:21-22 (the scapegoat). The goat carried the people's sins far away — to a place they could never come back from. Who carries our sins away? (Jesus) How does it feel to know our sins are really gone after repentance?
  1. Read Leviticus 19:9-10 together. God told farmers to leave the edges of their fields for the poor. What are ways our family can "leave corners" for others — sharing what we have?
  1. Read Leviticus 19:18. "Love thy neighbour as thyself." Who is one "neighbor" our family can show extra love to this week? Make a plan together.

Theological Exploration

  1. Leviticus presents five distinct offerings (burnt, grain, peace, sin, guilt). How does each one address a different dimension of the God-human relationship? What would be missing if any one were absent?
  1. The sin offering (chatat) purified the sanctuary, not just the sinner. What does it mean that human sin contaminates sacred space? How does this principle inform our understanding of temple worthiness?
  1. Compare the two goats of Yom Kippur (Leviticus 16:7-22) with Isaiah 53:4-6 and Hebrews 9:11-28. How does Christ fulfill both goats — the one whose blood atones and the one who bears sins away?
  1. Leviticus 19:2 says "Be holy because I am holy." How is this command both gift and demand? How does the Atonement make an impossible standard into a real possibility?
  1. Trace the phrase "I am the LORD" through Leviticus 19. It appears 16 times. What is its rhetorical function? Why does God anchor every ethical command in His identity rather than in social utility?

Historical and Comparative

  1. Maimonides argued sacrifice was a divine accommodation to Israel's Egyptian background. Nachmanides argued it carried intrinsic theological meaning. Which position do you find more compelling, and why? How does Alma 34:14 inform a Latter-day Saint reading?
  1. Jewish tradition teaches that children began Torah study with Leviticus because "the pure should study the pure." What does this tradition reveal about how ancient Jews valued the book modern readers most often skip?
  1. After the Temple's destruction in 70 CE, prayer replaced sacrifice. How does the structure of Latter-day Saint worship (sacrament, temple, prayer) continue the sacrificial patterns of Leviticus?
  1. Leviticus 19 has been called a re-presentation of the Ten Commandments in ethical, daily-life form. Compare the two lists. Where do you see parallels? What does Leviticus 19 add that the Ten Commandments do not?
  1. What is the single most important insight you gained from studying Leviticus?
  1. How has your understanding of Christ's Atonement deepened through Leviticus's sacrificial vocabulary — korban, olah, chatat, kipper, Azazel?
  1. What specific action will you take this week because of what you've studied?
  1. What question about Leviticus do you still want to explore further?
