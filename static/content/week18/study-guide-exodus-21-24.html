1. Week 18: Exodus 21–24 — Overview

God cares about everything. Week 18 moves from the Ten Commandments (Week 17) to their application in daily life. The Covenant Code (Ex 21-23) shows how foundational principles work out in specific cases: how to treat servants, resolve disputes, protect the vulnerable, observe Sabbath rest, and celebrate annual festivals. Then Exodus 24 ratifies this covenant in blood—anticipating Christ's blood that establishes the new covenant. This week reveals that God governs not just hearts but also households, marketplaces, and courtrooms.

  • Exodus 21: Laws about servants, violence, injuries, and liability
  • Exodus 22: Laws about property, theft, social responsibility, and justice
  • Exodus 23: Impartial justice, Sabbath rest, annual festivals, and conquest promises
  • Exodus 24: Covenant ratification in blood; elders see God and eat in His presence

2. Protection for the Vulnerable

God repeatedly commands protection for those society often overlooks:

"Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child. If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry" (Ex 22:22-23).

Three groups receive special mention:

  • Strangers/Foreigners (ger) — "for ye were strangers in Egypt"
  • Widows — without husband's protection
  • Orphans — without parental provision

3. Proportionate Justice (Lex Talionis)

"Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot" (Ex 21:24).

This isn't vengeance—it's limitation on vengeance. In a world where insults led to murders and murders led to clan warfare, "eye for eye" means the penalty must fit the crime—no more, no less.

4. Sabbath as Social Justice

"The seventh day thou shalt rest: that thine ox and thine ass may rest, and the son of thy handmaid, and the stranger, may be refreshed" (Ex 23:12).

Sabbath isn't just personal piety—it's justice. Everyone rests: landowners, servants, foreigners, even animals. Sabbath equalizes.

5. Covenant Sealed in Blood

"Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, Behold the blood of the covenant" (Ex 24:8).

Blood on the altar (God's side) and blood on the people (Israel's side) binds both parties in life-and-death commitment. Jesus echoes this directly: "This is my blood of the new covenant" (Matt 26:28).

6. The Preparatory Gospel — Why Case Law?

Latter-day Saint scripture provides a deeper explanation for why Israel received detailed case law rather than the higher principles God originally intended. D&C 84 teaches:

"Now this Moses plainly taught to the children of Israel in the wilderness, and sought diligently to sanctify his people that they might behold the face of God; But they hardened their hearts and could not endure his presence; therefore, the Lord in his wrath, for his anger was kindled against them, swore that they should not enter into his rest while in the wilderness, which rest is the fulness of his glory. Therefore, he took Moses out of their midst, and the Holy Priesthood also; And the lesser priesthood continued, which priesthood holdeth the key of the ministering of angels and the preparatory gospel; Which gospel is the gospel of repentance and of baptism, and the remission of sins, and the law of carnal commandments." (D&C 84:23-27)

The Covenant Code is not simply "practical application of principles" — it is a gracious divine accommodation to a people who were not yet ready for what God wanted to give them. The JST of Exodus 34:1-2 further clarifies this: when Moses returned with the second set of tablets, the Lord said the words of the law would be written "according as they were written at the first on the tables which thou brakest" — the same law, the same words. But then: "it shall not be according to the first, for I will take away the priesthood out of their midst; therefore my holy order, and the ordinances thereof, shall not go before them."

The law itself didn't change — what changed was Israel's access to the priesthood and holy order that gave the law its deeper meaning. Without the Melchizedek Priesthood and its ordinances going before them, the same words became "the law of carnal commandments" — not because the text was different, but because the people now lacked the spiritual framework to understand it as covenant principles. The words were identical; the lens through which Israel could read them was diminished.

This makes the mishpatim an act of mercy, not merely legislation. God gives what we are ready to receive — not by changing the message, but by meeting us where we are. The detailed case laws of Exodus 21-23 become a schoolmaster — teaching justice, compassion, and covenant faithfulness at a level Israel could bear — until the Lawgiver Himself would come to restore the priesthood, fulfill the law, and elevate their understanding.

2. Week 18: Historical and Cultural Context

The Covenant Code in Context

The laws in Exodus 21-23 are often called the "Covenant Code" or "Book of the Covenant" (Ex 24:7). They represent one of the oldest collections of biblical law and show how the Ten Commandments apply to specific situations.

Structure:

  • Exodus 21:1-11 — Laws about Hebrew servants
  • Exodus 21:12-36 — Laws about violence, injury, and liability
  • Exodus 22:1-15 — Laws about property and theft
  • Exodus 22:16-31 — Social and religious responsibilities
  • Exodus 23:1-9 — Justice in courts
  • Exodus 23:10-19 — Sabbath, sabbatical year, and festivals
  • Exodus 23:20-33 — Conquest promises

Understanding the Principle

"Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot" (Ex 21:24).

Common Misunderstanding: This promotes vengeance and brutality.

Actual Purpose: This limits vengeance to proportionate justice.

Historical Context:

  • In ANE cultures, revenge often escalated wildly
  • Lamech boasted: "I have slain a man to my wounding... If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold" (Gen 4:23-24)
  • "Eye for eye" stops escalation: penalty must match offense—no more, no less

Implementation

Most scholars believe lex talionis was usually applied as monetary compensation, not literal mutilation:

  • Exodus 21:30 shows ransom payment for capital offense
  • Rabbinic interpretation (Mishnah Bava Kamma 83b-84a) requires monetary damages

The principle is equivalent restitution, not physical retaliation.

A Difficult Topic

Exodus 21 regulates slavery rather than abolishing it. This is difficult for modern readers. Rather than trying to justify or debate its moral significance, we should simply present what the record actually says, recognizing that this was a very different time and culture.

How widespread was slavery?

Slavery was not a fringe practice — it was woven into the fabric of nearly every ancient society:

How did people become slaves?

Unlike American slavery, ancient slavery was not based on skin color. Anyone of any ethnicity could become a slave. The most common pathways were:

  • War and military defeat — the most common source; entire conquered populations could be enslaved
  • Debt — families or individuals who couldn't pay debts sold themselves or their children into bondage
  • Birth — children of enslaved mothers were born into slavery
  • Crime or legal punishment — enslavement as a judicial sentence
  • Child abandonment — exposed or abandoned children could be claimed as slaves
  • Kidnapping and piracy — common throughout the Mediterranean world
  • Self-sale — people in desperate poverty sold themselves to secure food and shelter

Slavery was not limited to the lower classes. Royalty, scholars, and people of high birth were regularly enslaved — especially through war and political misfortune:

  • Joseph — son of a patriarch, sold by his own brothers into slavery in Egypt
  • Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah — likely from noble or priestly families ("of the king's seed, and of the princes," Daniel 1:3), carried into Babylonian captivity
  • The Israelites as a nation — an entire people enslaved in Egypt
  • Plato — sold into slavery by the tyrant Dionysius I of Syracuse after criticizing his rule; had to be ransomed by friends
  • Pythagoras — tradition holds he was enslaved during Cambyses' conquest of Egypt
  • Diogenes the Cynic — captured by pirates and sold; when asked his trade, he replied, "I know how to rule free men"
  • Epictetus — born into slavery in Rome, became one of the most influential Stoic philosophers
  • Aesop — the famous fabulist, was a slave on the island of Samos

What the biblical text says:

Slavery was a common and deeply entrenched institution throughout the ancient world, and the Israelites fully participated in it. The Torah draws explicit distinctions based on ethnicity and gender:

- Hebrew male servants were released after six years (21:2)

- Hebrew women were not given the same release: "she shall not go out as the menservants do" (21:7)

- Foreign slaves could be held permanently as property and inherited by children (Leviticus 25:44-46)

This reflects the social reality of a different time and demonstrates how God works with people within the context of their environments, whether those environments are ideal or not.

Key Provisions in Covenant Code:

  • Six-year limit for male Hebrew servants: Hebrew male servants freed in seventh year (21:2)
  • Voluntary permanence: A male servant could choose to stay permanently (21:5-6)
  • Women did not receive the same release: "If a man sell his daughter to be a maidservant, she shall not go out as the menservants do" (21:7). The provisions in 21:7-11 address marriage obligations, not freedom — female slaves were often taken as concubines. A woman could go free only if the master failed to fulfill his marital commitment to her or his son. This is a different and unequal standard.
  • Injury compensation: If a master destroyed a servant's eye or knocked out a tooth, the servant went free (21:26-27). The text uses the generic "manservant" (ebed) and "maidservant" (amah) — applying to both men and women. Whether this extended to foreign slaves is debated; some rabbinic sources (Kiddushin 24a-25a) read this provision as specifically protecting foreign slaves, since Hebrew servants already had a release date.

Theological Movement:

The Law plants seeds of freedom that would eventually flower, but these seeds would not grow overnight. God works within human cultures and conditions to move them toward justice progressively.

Brief Mention (Exodus 21:13)

"If a man lie not in wait, but God deliver him into his hand; then I will appoint thee a place whither he shall flee."

In the ancient Near East, when someone was killed — even accidentally — the victim's family had a culturally recognized right to avenge the death. A close male relative known as the "avenger of blood" (goel ha-dam) was expected to hunt down and kill the person responsible, regardless of whether the death was intentional or accidental. This created a cycle of blood vengeance that could escalate into clan warfare.

The cities of refuge were God's intervention into this cycle. They were designated cities — eventually six, spread across the land of Israel — where a person who had killed someone accidentally (without premeditation or malice) could flee for protection. Once inside the city, the manslayer was safe from the avenger of blood and was entitled to a trial before the congregation to determine whether the killing was truly accidental or deliberate.

  • If the killing was judged accidental, the manslayer remained in the city of refuge, protected, until the death of the current high priest — at which point they could return home freely.
  • If the killing was judged intentional murder, the manslayer was handed over to the avenger of blood for execution.

This system established a critical legal principle: accidental killing is not murder, and the accused deserves protection and a fair hearing before punishment. It inserted due process into a culture of blood vengeance.

Developed Later:

  • Numbers 35:9-34 — Six cities designated with detailed regulations
  • Deuteronomy 19:1-13 — Further provisions, including the requirement that roads to the cities be maintained so the manslayer could reach them quickly
  • Joshua 20 — The six cities formally established after the conquest

Christ Connection:

Christ is our refuge from the justice we deserve. We flee to Him for mercy, and He provides sanctuary. Just as the manslayer was freed at the death of the high priest, we are freed through the death of our great High Priest.

What Are the Moedim?

The Hebrew word moed (מועד, plural moedim) is typically translated "feast" or "season," but its meaning runs deeper. The root ya'ad (יעד) means "to appoint" or "to designate" — the mem prefix turns it into a noun: an appointed time, a scheduled meeting with God. These were not merely celebrations; they were ritual appointments with the Creator.

The first time moedim appears in scripture is striking: "And God said, Let there be lights in the firmament of the heaven to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs, and for seasons [moedim], and for days, and years" (Genesis 1:14). The very purpose of the sun and moon — from the moment of creation — was to mark God's appointed times. The Psalmist echoes this: "He appointed the moon for moedim" (Psalm 104:19). The feast days are not an afterthought added at Sinai — they are woven into the fabric of creation itself.

"A Statute Forever"

Leviticus 23 describes each of these festivals as chuqqat olam — "a statute forever throughout your generations" (Leviticus 23:14, 21, 31, 41). The Torah is explicit that these appointed times were not temporary measures but permanent features of the covenant relationship between God and His people.

Older Than Sinai

Ancient Jewish tradition understood the moedim as predating the Mosaic law. The Book of Jubilees (2nd century BC, preserved among the Dead Sea Scrolls) claims that the Festival of Weeks was celebrated by Noah after the flood, and that Abraham observed the Feast of Tabernacles — both long before Moses received the Torah at Sinai. Jubilees sees these appointed times as built into the structure of time itself, not as innovations introduced at Sinai but as patterns that had existed since creation and were observed by the patriarchs.

What Happened at the Festivals

In ancient Israel, the feast days were not merely celebrations. They gathered covenant people at God's house to hear His word, renew their commitments, and share in sacred meals shaped by sacrifice. In a world where most people did not own written scripture, this was one of the main ways Torah was heard, remembered, and passed on. Song, chant, teaching, sacrifice, and communal meal all worked together.

One Pattern, Three Traditions

What we are looking at when we look at the moedim is not a Jewish-only phenomenon. Israel's appointed times establish a covenant pattern — gathering God's people at His house, at His invitation, to feast on His word and renew their covenants — and that same pattern surfaces in three distinct traditions, each in its own form.

Israel under Sinai. The three pilgrimage feasts gathered the covenant nation to the place where the Lord placed His name. Passover rehearsed the founding redemption. Shavuot remembered the giving of Torah. Sukkot re-enacted the wilderness sojourn. Sacrifice, communal meal, and the public reading of the law belonged to all three (Deuteronomy 31:10-13; Nehemiah 8:1-18). One of the major purposes of the gathering was that the people would hear and learn — "that they may hear, and that they may learn, and fear the Lord your God, and observe to do all the words of this law" (Deuteronomy 31:12).

The Nephites at the temple. When King Benjamin gathered his people in Mosiah 2, the Nephites were observing the law of Moses (Mosiah 2:3) and the entire scene reads as a covenant assembly in the same family of patterns:

"And they pitched their tents round about the temple, every man having his tent with the door thereof towards the temple, that thereby they might remain in their tents and hear the words which king Benjamin should speak unto them" (Mosiah 2:6).

The detail of tents pitched around the temple, doors facing the temple is striking. Latter-day Saint scholars have long noted the affinity with Israelite festival observance — particularly the tent/booth imagery of Sukkot and the covenant-renewal pattern of seasonal pilgrimage. The Nephites came with sacrifices "according to the law of Moses" (Mosiah 2:3) and stayed to hear the word. King Benjamin's invitation — "open your ears that ye may hear, and your hearts that ye may understand, and your minds that ye may comprehend the mysteries of God may be unfolded to your view" (Mosiah 2:9) — echoes the same covenant-learning purpose named in Deuteronomy.

The Nephites were not in Jerusalem, and some local traditions may have varied with time and circumstance. But the Book of Mormon is clear that they kept the law of Moses faithfully (Jarom 1:5; 2 Nephi 25:24; Alma 25:15). The pattern is unmistakable: a covenant people, gathered at the house of the Lord, with sacrifices offered according to the law of Moses, with the word taught, and with covenants renewed at the close (Mosiah 5).

Latter-day Saints today. It is not a coincidence that generations of Latter-day Saint teachers have used the Mosiah 2 image — tents turned toward the temple — to teach the purpose of General Conference, of stake conference, and of every Sabbath. Our gathering is not a Mosaic feast. We do not bring firstlings of the flock to the altar. The shedding of blood ended at the cross. But the underlying shape is unmistakably the same: a covenant people, gathered at the Lord's invitation, to hear and learn about His word through His prophets, to renew covenants at His table, to be taught and changed and sent home different.

Nephi names the heart of it in language the whole canon shares: "feast upon the words of Christ; for behold, the words of Christ will tell you all things what ye should do" (2 Nephi 32:3). Sinai gathered Israel to feast on Torah. The temple at Zarahemla gathered the Nephites to feast on the word. Conference and sacrament gather us to feast on the word of Christ. Three traditions, three contexts, three forms — one Lord, calling His covenant people together at His appointed times to feed them.

What this means for reading the moedim: when we encounter Passover, Shavuot, and Sukkot in the Old Testament, we are not looking at a foreign religion's calendar. We are looking at the original form of a pattern our own discipleship still lives inside — the same Lord, calling, gathering, feeding, and sending. The traditions and experiences differ. The purpose does not.

Why Blood Offerings?

To understand the feasts, we have to understand what was happening at their center: sacrifice. Leviticus answers the "why" directly: "For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul" (Leviticus 17:11). Blood, in the Torah's symbolic economy, is life. To offer blood is to acknowledge that life — including the life of the worshipper — belongs to God, and that the cost of sin is paid for in life, not in lesser currencies. Every offering pointed past itself to the one infinite offering still to come.

The major sacrifice types (Leviticus 1-7) are worth knowing, because each one teaches a different facet of atonement and covenant:

  • עֹלָה (ʿolah) — the burnt offering ("that which goes up"). The whole animal is consumed on the altar; nothing is kept back. It teaches total consecration.
  • מִנְחָה (minchah) — the grain offering. A bloodless gift of fine flour, oil, and frankincense. It teaches devotion of one's labor.
  • שְׁלָמִים (shelamim / zevach shelamim) — the peace or fellowship offering. Part is burned to God, part is given to the priests, and part is eaten by the worshipper and family in a covenant meal in the Lord's presence. This is the covenant meal sacrifice.
  • חַטָּאת (chattat) — the sin offering. For unintentional sin, ritual impurity, or covenant breach.
  • אָשָׁם (asham) — the guilt or trespass offering. For specific offenses, often with restitution.

The feasts gathered these offerings together at appointed times, weaving consecration, gratitude, fellowship, repentance, and restitution into the rhythm of the covenant year.

The Lord's Feast

Of these, the shelamim — the fellowship offering — is the most directly relevant to this lesson, because it is the sacrifice in which the worshipper eats with the Lord. The peace offering is not mainly about removing sin (the chattat does that); it is about communion. The covenant ratification meal in Exodus 24, the priestly portions eaten in the holy place, the family meals at the appointed times — all belong to this category. They anticipate the Last Supper, where Christ ratified the new covenant in His own blood and shared the covenant meal with His disciples. And He pointed beyond even that night: "I will not drink henceforth of this fruit of the vine, until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom" (Matthew 26:29). The full arc runs from the shelamim at Sinai, through the upper room, through the sacrament we partake of each Sabbath, to the great covenant meal yet to come — "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9).

About the Prophetic Critique of Feast Days

Modern readers sometimes assume the prophets condemned the feasts themselves. They did not. They condemned the way Israel was abusing the feasts — using ritual observance to paper over injustice and hypocrisy.

"Bring no more vain oblations; incense is an abomination unto me; the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I cannot away with; it is iniquity, even the solemn meeting. Your new moons and your appointed feasts my soul hateth … Wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil; learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" (Isaiah 1:13-17).

Isaiah's quarrel is with appointed times observed by hands stained with oppression. The remedy he prescribes is not to cancel the feasts but to repent and reform. The same logic runs through Amos 5:21-24, Hosea 6:6, and Micah 6:6-8. The prophets' point is that ritual divorced from righteousness is intolerable — not that ritual itself was a mistake.

About Paul

Several Pauline passages are sometimes read as a flat rejection of feast observance: Colossians 2:16-17, Galatians 4:9-11, and sometimes Romans 14:5-6. Read in context, Paul is not denouncing the appointed times. He is dealing with Gentile converts being pressured to take on the full ritual law as a condition of salvation, and with disputes among Jewish and Gentile believers over how to live together. His repeated emphasis is that the feasts were "a shadow of things to come" whose substance is Christ — not that they were never meaningful.

And Paul's own practice confirms this. Luke records that Paul sailed past Ephesus because "he hasted, if it were possible for him, to be at Jerusalem the day of Pentecost" (Acts 20:16). He timed his travel around the feast of Unleavened Bread (Acts 20:6). He told the Corinthians directly, "Christ our passover is sacrificed for us: Therefore let us keep the feast" (1 Corinthians 5:7-8). Paul did not reject the moedim; he understood them as filled with Christ.

What Actually Changed

Modern Latter-day Saints often summarize the New Covenant by saying, "the law of Moses was done away with." That is true in a broad sense, but it can flatten what specifically changed and what carried forward. The more accurate answer is this: the demand of justice was satisfied once and for all in Christ, and so the shedding of blood in animal sacrifice ended.

Alma is unmistakable on the central change:

"Therefore, it is expedient that there should be a great and last sacrifice, and then shall there be, or it is expedient there should be, a stop to the shedding of blood; then shall the law of Moses be fulfilled" (Alma 34:13).

And the resurrected Lord, in 3 Nephi, draws the line in His own words:

"And ye shall offer up unto me no more the shedding of blood; yea, your sacrifices and your burnt offerings shall be done away" (3 Nephi 9:19-20).

The shedding of blood ends because Christ's blood has answered for sin. What replaces it is the offering of the whole inner person — a broken heart and a contrite spirit. Other older covenant practices, like circumcision as the Abrahamic sign or the feast days as covenant patterns, have a different history of fulfillment and continuation and should not be collapsed into the same category as daily and seasonal blood offerings.

The Restoration Pattern

When the Church was restored, the Lord instructed Joseph Smith to gather the Saints regularly (D&C 20:61-62; D&C 44:1). This became what we know as General Conference. It is not a coincidence that our modern General Conference aligns broadly with the spring and fall seasons of Pesach and Sukkot — the great pilgrimage periods when Israel gathered to the temple. God still gathers His covenant people at His appointed times, to teach them and bless them, because He loves them.

Blood Ritual

"Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, Behold the blood of the covenant" (Ex 24:8).

Structure:

  1. Moses builds altar with twelve pillars (24:4)
  2. Young men offer sacrifices (24:5)
  3. Half the blood sprinkled on altar (24:6) — God's side
  4. Covenant read aloud; people commit (24:7)
  5. Half the blood sprinkled on people (24:8) — Israel's side

Significance:

Blood represented life (Lev 17:11). The covenant "costs" life—both parties bound in life-and-death commitment. Breaking covenant is capital offense.

  • "As it were the body of heaven in his clearness"

Who Rests:

  • Servants ("son of thy handmaid")
  • Foreigners ("stranger")
  • Animals ("ox and ass")

Sabbatical Year (Exodus 23:10-11)

Israel's covenant economy was ordered around a rhythm of release. Every seventh year the land was to lie fallow (Exodus 23:10-11; Leviticus 25:1-7); debts among Israelites were to be released (Deuteronomy 15:1-11); and Hebrew bondservants were to be set free with provisions for their new beginning (Deuteronomy 15:12-15). Every fiftieth year — the Year of Jubilee — extended the pattern: ancestral lands returned to their original families, and liberty was proclaimed "throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof" (Leviticus 25:10). Land could not be permanently sold because, in covenant logic, the land was not ultimately Israel's: "The land shall not be sold for ever: for the land is mine" (Leviticus 25:23).

The practical effect was that no Israelite could be permanently locked out of inheritance, freedom, or sustenance. Wealth could concentrate for a season, but the system was designed to reset.

The poor and the gleaner. The same covenant logic shaped the harvest. Israelites were commanded not to reap the corners of their fields, not to gather what fell, and not to glean their vineyards a second time: "Thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger: I am the Lord your God" (Leviticus 19:9-10; cf. Leviticus 23:22; Deuteronomy 24:19-21). The edge of the field belonged, by law, to those who had no field of their own.

The book of Ruth shows this provision in action. Ruth, a Moabite widow returning to Bethlehem with her mother-in-law Naomi, supports them by going "to glean ears of corn after him in whose sight I shall find grace" (Ruth 2:2). She arrives in the field of Boaz, a kinsman, who not only allows her to glean but instructs his workers to leave handfuls on purpose for her (Ruth 2:15-16). What Leviticus commanded as a baseline, Boaz extended as covenant kindness — and the result is the line that produces David and ultimately Christ (Ruth 4:17-22; Matthew 1:5-6).

The lesson is not just economic. The sabbatical, jubilee, and gleaning laws together teach that in God's covenant order, provision for the vulnerable is not charity tacked onto the system — it is the system.

3. Week 18: Key Passages Study

Text (KJV)

"And if any mischief follow, then thou shalt give life for life, Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot, Burning for burning, wound for wound, stripe for stripe."

Few phrases in Torah are more often misread. Modern readers tend to hear this as a license for personal revenge — if you hurt me, I get to hurt you back the same way. Read in its own world, it is almost the opposite: a deliberate limit on retaliation, not a permission for it.

Literary Analysis

The principle: proportionate justice. In the ancient Near East, blood feuds easily escalated. An insult could trigger a wound; a wound could trigger a death; a death could trigger the destruction of a household. The lex talionis ("law of retaliation") draws a hard line: the response to harm may not exceed the harm. One eye, not two. One tooth, not a life.

The literary context — the pregnant woman case. Exodus 21:22-25 is case law. It begins with a specific scenario in which men fighting accidentally injure a pregnant woman. If the harm is limited, a fine is set. If lasting injury follows, the response must be proportionate: life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, and so on. The list is not a manual for revenge; it is a measuring rod for a judge weighing damages in a difficult case.

Why the formula appears three times. The talionic formula recurs in Leviticus 24:17-22 and Deuteronomy 19:16-21, each time in a different context and each time tied to the same principle: the penalty must fit the crime.

Theological Significance

  1. Limiting vengeance: the law sets a ceiling on retaliation
  2. Equal justice: the same standard applies regardless of social class
  3. Human value: every injury matters and must be answered fairly

Rabbinic interpretation: By the time of the Mishnah and Talmud, the dominant Jewish reading of "eye for eye" was monetary compensation rather than literal mutilation (cf. Bava Kamma 83b-84a). The idea is equivalent restitution, not physical retaliation.

LDS Connection

When Jesus says, "Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matthew 5:38-39), He is not accusing Moses of cruelty. The law of proportionate justice still stands as the floor of public order. What He raises is the personal standard above that floor.

A detail worth noticing: He specifies the right cheek. A right-handed strike to a person's right cheek is most naturally a backhand — a cultural insult from a superior to an inferior. Read this way, "turn the other cheek" is not passive doormat ethics; it refuses both retaliation and humiliation. (This cultural reading is plausible and widely cited, though debated among scholars.)

The lex talionis tells the magistrate, don't take more than was taken. Jesus tells the disciple, and you, when wronged personally, can choose not to take what you are owed. The first protects the community from runaway vengeance. The second invites individual disciples to live by the higher law of mercy that fulfills it.

Text (KJV)

"Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child. If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry; And my wrath shall wax hot, and I will kill you with the sword; and your wives shall be widows, and your children fatherless."

Literary Analysis

The passage names three vulnerable groups: stranger, widow, orphan. God's response to their mistreatment is fierce—the penalty mirrors the crime (oppressors' families become widows and orphans).

Note the basis: "For ye were strangers in the land of Egypt." Israel's ethics flow from memory. Because they suffered oppression, they must not oppress.

Theological Significance

  1. God hears the vulnerable: "They cry at all unto me, I will surely hear"
  2. Covenant memory shapes ethics: Remembering Egypt creates compassion
  3. Measure-for-measure justice: Those who create widows become widowed

Rabbinic Interpretation

The Talmud counted precisely how many times the Torah issues this command. Rabbi Eliezer the Great taught: "For what reason did the Torah issue warnings in thirty-six places — and some say forty-six places — with regard to causing any distress to a convert?" (Bava Metzia 59b). No other commandment receives this level of repetition. The sheer count reveals how seriously the rabbis took God's concern for the vulnerable outsider.

Rabbi Natan derives a principle from the same verse: "A defect that is in you, do not mention it in another" (Bava Metzia 59b). Israel's own history as strangers is not leverage for superiority but a foundation for humility — those who were once outsiders must never weaponize that history against those who occupy the outsider position now.

The Talmud also preserves a teaching from Imma Shalom: "All the gates of Heaven are locked, except for the gates of prayer for victims of verbal mistreatment" (Bava Metzia 59b). When the vulnerable cry out, God always hears — no gate can block their prayer.

LDS Connection

Fast offerings exist specifically to care for the vulnerable. President Gordon B. Hinckley frequently emphasized visiting widows, caring for the fatherless. James 1:27 summarizes: "Pure religion... is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction."

  2. God identifies with the poor: Their cry reaches His ears

  • Foreigners ("stranger")

Sabbath democratizes rest. On the seventh day, master and slave are equals. Even animals are dignified.

  2. Creation care: Even animals deserve refreshment
  3. Human equality: On Sabbath, all receive rest

  • Ex 24:3: First response after hearing (verbal report)

  • Half on people (Israel's side) — Ex 24:8

  1. Informed consent: Israel heard the full covenant before committing
  2. Life-and-death commitment: Blood = life; breaking covenant is fatal
  3. God binds Himself too: Altar blood represents God's commitment

LDS Connection

Jesus explicitly invokes this passage: "This is my blood of the new covenant, which is shed for many" (Mark 14:24). The sacrament renews covenant sealed by Christ's blood. We don't sprinkle blood; we partake of symbols that represent it.

Text (KJV)

"Then went up Moses, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel: And they saw the God of Israel: and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone, and as it were the body of heaven in his clearness. And upon the nobles of the children of Israel he laid not his hand: also they saw God, and did eat and drink."

Literary Analysis

After the covenant is read and ratified by sacrifice (Exodus 24:3-8), Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel ascend the mountain. What follows is one of the most striking scenes in scripture: "they saw the God of Israel ... and did eat and drink" (Exodus 24:10-11).

Two things are happening at once: a theophany and a covenant meal.

In the ancient Near East, a shared meal between covenant partners ratified and embodied the covenant. We see the same pattern when Jacob and Laban cut their covenant and "did eat bread" together (Genesis 31:46, 54), and when Abimelech makes covenant with Isaac and "they did eat and drink" before parting in peace (Genesis 26:30-31). To eat together is to declare, we are now bound. Exodus 24 raises that pattern to its highest possible expression: the people's representatives eat in the very presence of the covenant Lord.

Seventy elders, plus Moses, Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, make seventy-four — a representative body standing in for the whole house of Israel.

Theological Significance

  1. Communion with God: covenant creates fellowship; they eat in His presence
  2. Representative covenant: the elders stand for the whole house of Israel
  3. The Lord's feast: the covenant Lord hosts the meal that seals the bond

LDS Connection

Latter-day Saints can carefully recognize in this scene a pattern that comes to its fullness in the New Covenant: a covenant ratified by blood (Exodus 24:8; cf. Matthew 26:28), followed by a covenant meal eaten in the presence of the Lord. When Christ institutes the Lord's Supper, He gathers His covenant representatives, ratifies the new covenant in His own blood, and shares a meal. The sacrament we partake of each Sabbath belongs to that pattern.

Exodus 24 does not "establish the sacrament" in any direct sense — that establishment belongs to the upper room. But it does establish the shape of covenant communion that the sacrament fulfills: the eaten meal is the seal of the covenant, and the covenant Lord is the host.

Seeing God and Living

Readers often stop short here. Exodus 24:11 says plainly that the elders "saw God, and did eat and drink." A few chapters later, the Lord tells Moses, "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live" (Exodus 33:20). How do we hold these together?

The Restoration gives a specific lane for this question.

Joseph Smith Translation. The JST recasts Exodus 33:20 in a way that resolves the apparent contradiction:

"And he said unto Moses, Thou canst not see my face at this time, lest mine anger be kindled against thee also and I destroy thee and thy people; for there shall no man among them see me at this time and live, for they are exceeding sinful. And no sinful man hath at any time, neither shall there be any sinful man at any time, that shall see my face and live" (JST Exodus 33:20).

Two things change. First, "at this time" — the restriction is linked to Israel's present spiritual condition after the golden calf, not to an unchangeable law of nature. Second, the universal principle is sharpened: it is no sinful man who cannot see God and live, not no man at all.

Moses 1. Moses 1 is the clearest Restoration framing of how mortals can see God at all. Moses "saw God face to face, and he talked with him, and the glory of God was upon Moses; therefore Moses could endure his presence" (Moses 1:2). Moses himself explains the mechanism: "my natural eyes could not have beheld; for I should have withered and died in his presence ... I was transfigured before him" (Moses 1:11). And the principle is stated outright: "no man can behold all my glory, and afterwards remain in the flesh on the earth" (Moses 1:5).

The Restoration framework, then, is not "the elders did not really see God" or "Exodus 24 contradicts Exodus 33." It is that mortals can see God when they are transfigured or otherwise quickened by His Spirit so as to endure His presence. So when covenant representatives eat and drink in the presence of the God of Israel, the right Latter-day Saint reading is not that the text is exaggerating, but that they were sustained in His presence long enough to share the covenant meal.

Text (KJV)

"Behold, I send an Angel before thee, to keep thee in the way, and to bring thee into the place which I have prepared. Beware of him, and obey his voice, provoke him not; for he will not pardon your transgressions: for my name is in him. But if thou shalt indeed obey his voice, and do all that I speak; then I will be an enemy unto thine enemies, and an adversary unto thine adversaries. For mine Angel shall go before thee." (Exodus 23:20-23)

Literary Analysis

This passage stands at the climax of the Covenant Code, immediately before the covenant ratification in Exodus 24. After dozens of case laws (mishpatim), God's final instruction shifts from legislation to revelation: He will send a divine figure to guide Israel.

The language is extraordinary. This is not an ordinary malʾakh (messenger). Three details set this Angel apart:

  • "My name is in him" — He bears the divine Name
  • "He will not pardon your transgressions" — He exercises divine judicial authority
  • "Obey his voice" — the same language used for obeying God Himself

Theological Significance

The Angel who bears God's name occupies a unique position in Israelite theology. He is sent by the Lord, yet carries the Lord's name and authority. The passage does not prove every Christological conclusion by itself, but it clearly presents a messenger whose delegated presence is far more than ordinary.

This passage also foregrounds a side of Christ that modern readers sometimes resist: the One who answers the demands of justice, not only the One who comforts. Mercy and justice are not two different beings. They are two faces of the one Mediator who can extend mercy precisely because He has answered justice.

LDS Connection

Latter-day Saint readers often, and reasonably, recognize in this Angel the premortal Christ — Jehovah Himself — leading covenant Israel through the wilderness. The Restoration anchor is direct: "Behold, I am he that gave the law, and I am he who covenanted with my people Israel" (3 Nephi 15:5).

At the same time, the passage should be handled carefully. The Hebrew word malʾakh can describe created angelic messengers elsewhere, so the Christ-identification rests on the canon as a whole, not on this line alone.

What Exodus 23 shows us, then, is something like a veiled theophany. The Lord's name is in the Angel; His authority moves with Israel; His justice is real. But the encounter is mediated — close enough to lead the people, measured enough that they are not consumed.

5. Week 18: Jewish Perspective

The mishpatim (ordinances) of Exodus 21-23 have been central to Jewish legal development for millennia. Rabbinic tradition developed these case laws into comprehensive systems of justice, ethics, and social responsibility. This section explores how Jewish tradition has understood and applied these ancient statutes.

The Two Torahs

Jewish Tradition Teaches:

  • Written Torah: The five books of Moses (Torah she-bichtav)
  • Oral Torah: Rabbinic interpretation passed orally, eventually written as Mishnah and Talmud (Torah she-be'al peh)

Exodus 21-23 as Paradigm

The Covenant Code doesn't cover every possible situation. Rabbinic tradition understands these laws as paradigms—principles to be applied to new cases through interpretation.

Example: "If a man steal an ox or a sheep..." (Ex 22:1) becomes the basis for all property law. What about stealing a camel? A coat? Money? Rabbis extrapolate principles.

Authoritative Interpretation

The Mishnah (codified ~200 CE) and Talmud (completed ~500 CE) systematically apply Torah law to countless scenarios, creating Jewish halakha (law).

LDS Parallel

We have canonized scripture AND living prophets who apply timeless principles to contemporary issues. The Handbook of Instructions applies gospel principles to Church administration, just as Talmud applies Torah to daily life.

Rabbinic Interpretation

The Talmud (Bava Kamma 83b-84a) extensively discusses lex talionis and concludes that "eye for eye" means monetary compensation, not literal mutilation.

Arguments Given:

  1. Practical impossibility: How do you exact an eye without killing? What if the injurer is already blind?
  2. Textual evidence: Exodus 21:30 allows ransom payment for capital offense; if ransom works for life, why not for eye?
  3. Principle of equality: A small eye isn't equal to a large eye; only monetary value equalizes

The Famous Saying

Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai (Mekhilta): The verse establishes that the injurer must pay the monetary value equivalent to the injury.

Why Use This Language?

Rabbis suggest the Torah uses vivid language to emphasize the seriousness of injury. "You deserve to lose your eye—but you'll pay its value instead."

LDS Resonance

This interpretive flexibility reminds us that scripture requires wisdom to apply. We don't literalize every command (we don't build arks or sacrifice lambs) but seek underlying principles. The Spirit guides application.

Foundational Ethics

Two verses invoke Egypt as ethical foundation:

"Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Ex 22:21).

"Thou shalt not oppress a stranger: for ye know the heart of a stranger, seeing ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Ex 23:9).

Memory as Moral Teacher

Jewish tradition emphasizes that remembering suffering should increase compassion, not harden hearts.

Passover Haggadah:

"In every generation, each person should see themselves as if they personally went out of Egypt."

The Exodus isn't just ancestral history—it's personal identity. We were slaves; therefore, we must not enslave.

"You Know the Heart"

Exodus 23:9 adds a psychological dimension: "ye know the heart (nephesh) of a stranger." Having been outsiders, Israelites understand what vulnerability feels like. Empathy should flow from experience.

The Thirty-Six Commandments

The rabbinic tradition counted precisely how many times the Torah commands protection of the stranger. Rabbi Eliezer the Great taught: "For what reason did the Torah issue warnings in thirty-six places — and some say forty-six places — with regard to causing any distress to a convert?" (Bava Metzia 59b). No other category of commandment receives this level of repetition in the Torah. The sheer count reveals how seriously the rabbis understood God's concern for the vulnerable outsider.

Rabbi Natan, interpreting Exodus 22:20 ("for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt"), derives a principle of moral humility: "A defect that is in you, do not mention it in another" (Bava Metzia 59b). Israel's past as slaves is not a source of shame to hide but a responsibility to remember — and the specific responsibility it creates is humility toward those who now occupy the outsider position Israel once held.

LDS Application

Our trials should enlarge our compassion, not embitter us. Converts remember what it felt like to be searching. Those who have suffered should "bear one another's burdens" (Mosiah 18:8). Memory of struggle becomes foundation for ministry.

"Na'aseh v'Nishma" — "We Will Do and We Will Hear"

When Israel responded to the covenant, they said (Ex 24:7):

"All that the LORD hath said will we do, and be obedient."

In Hebrew: "na'aseh v'nishma" — literally "we will do and we will hear."

Rabbinic Praise

The Talmud (Shabbat 88a) celebrates this response. Israel committed to DO before fully HEARING (understanding). This was considered supreme faith.

The story: When Israel said "na'aseh v'nishma," angels descended and crowned each Israelite twice—one crown for "we will do," one for "we will hear."

Meaning

Action precedes understanding. We don't wait until we comprehend everything before obeying. Obedience itself becomes teacher.

LDS Parallel

Baptism at eight; temple covenants before full comprehension; missionary service before expertise. We commit, then learn. Nephi's "I will go and do" (1 Ne 3:7) echoes this pattern.

Who Were They?

Exodus 24:9-11 records that Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders ascended Sinai, saw God, and ate in His presence.

Significance of Seventy

The number seventy appears repeatedly:

  • 70 descendants of Jacob entered Egypt (Gen 46:27)
  • 70 elders appointed to help Moses (Num 11:16-17)
  • 70 members of Sanhedrin (later tradition)

Symbolic meaning: Seventy represents completeness or totality—all Israel, through representatives, encountered God.

"They Saw God"

Jewish commentators grappled with this text. How could they see God and live?

Rashi (11th century): They gazed at God presumptuously and deserved punishment (Nadab and Abihu later die, Lev 10). But God delayed judgment so as not to disturb the covenant ceremony's joy.

Other views: They saw a manifestation—God's glory, the sapphire pavement—but not His essence.

LDS Perspective

We understand that God can reveal Himself in degrees. Moses later saw God "face to face" (Ex 33:11) yet was also told "thou canst not see my face" and live (Ex 33:20). Different encounters reveal different dimensions.

ANE Context

Blood rituals were common in ancient Near Eastern treaties. Blood represented life (the seat of life-force). Sealing covenant with blood meant: "May my blood be shed if I break this covenant."

Divided Blood

Moses divided the blood:

  • Half on altar (representing God)
  • Half on people (representing Israel)

Both parties are bound. Covenant is bilateral.

Rabbinic Commentary

Ibn Ezra (12th century): The blood on the altar ascended to God; the blood on people marked them as consecrated. Both were now bound together.

Covenant Language

The phrase "behold the blood of the covenant" (Ex 24:8) became paradigmatic. Jesus' words at the Last Supper directly echo this passage.

LDS Application

The sacrament is our regular covenant renewal. We don't use blood, but we partake of symbols representing Christ's blood—the blood that seals the new and everlasting covenant.

A famous Talmudic story (Bava Metzia 59b) draws on Deuteronomy 30:12 — "It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?" — to make a point about how Torah is interpreted within the covenant community. In the story, even a heavenly voice does not overrule the consensus of the rabbis on a question of law, and a later sage hears the Lord respond with something close to delight: "My children have prevailed over Me, My children have prevailed over Me."

This story may cause a Latter-day Saint audience to pause. A community that overrules God, and God is happy about it? But if we look carefully, there is something genuinely beautiful here: God rejoices when His covenant people grow into wisdom, exercise agency well, and learn to apply His word with care. That is the bittersweet goal of every righteous parent — to see their children grow wise enough to carry the work forward. It is a teaching worth preserving.

Where the Restoration Draws a Different Line

The tension is not with the story itself, but with how far some readings push it. This passage is sometimes taken — even within Jewish tradition, and certainly in popular retellings — as if revelation effectively ended at Sinai, leaving human interpretation as the only legitimate channel afterward. That is where Latter-day Saints must be clear: the heavens are not closed. "We believe all that God has revealed, all that He does now reveal, and we believe that He will yet reveal many great and important things pertaining to the Kingdom of God" (Articles of Faith 1:9). God still speaks — through living prophets (D&C 1:38), through the Holy Ghost to every covenant member, and through scripture both ancient and modern. Agency is the means by which we align with His will, not a license to substitute our preferences for His commands.

Where the Insight Holds

Read within those boundaries, the underlying instinct of the Talmudic passage maps cleanly onto Restoration teaching. God does delight in His children's growth. Not every righteous decision arrives by burning bush or angelic visit; very often the Lord's guidance comes as we study an issue out, decide as best we can, and act in faith (D&C 9:7-9). When members of the Church wrestle prayerfully with hard questions and reach wise, righteous conclusions in alignment with the prophets and the Spirit, that is not a substitute for revelation — it is one of the forms revelation takes.

What the Restoration rejects is any framing in which the community of interpreters has authority apart from God's continuing voice. What it affirms is that God still speaks, that He has authorized prophets and apostles to speak in His name, and that He does indeed delight when His covenant children — guided by His Spirit — become wise.

Focus: Covenant Commitment and Teaching

Missionary Application:

When teaching investigators about baptism, remember Exodus 24:

  1. Covenant terms are explained (we teach before baptism)
  2. Investigators commit ("I will follow Jesus")
  3. Baptism seals the covenant, not by replacing blood, but by binding the disciple to the blood Christ has already shed

Discussion:

  1. How do you help investigators understand covenant seriousness?
  2. What if someone doesn't fully understand everything before baptism?
  3. How does "na'aseh v'nishma" (we will do and hear) apply?

Key Point: Under the Mosaic order, covenants were sealed in blood — the blood of bulls and goats sprinkled on the people (Exodus 24:8) and offered repeatedly at the altar. Under the new and everlasting covenant, the demands of justice have been answered once for all in the blood of Christ (Hebrews 9:11-14; Alma 34:13-14). What now seals the disciple to the covenant is baptism by water and the gift of the Holy Ghost — water as the symbol of cleansing and renewal. Baptism through immersion symbolizes death, burial, and rising with Christ (Romans 6:3-5). The Spirit is the witness and sanctifier of the covenant (Moroni 8:25-26). Baptism does not "replace blood"; it points to the blood that has already been shed and seals the believer to the One who shed it.
