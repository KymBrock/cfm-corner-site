At a Glance

This week we dive into the creation accounts—not one, but three parallel narratives in Genesis, Moses, and Abraham. Rather than viewing these as competing scientific accounts, scholars and teachers this week invite us to see them as complementary windows into profound theological truths about God's nature, our divine identity, and the ongoing creative process in our own lives.

Key Themes Emerging:

  • Creation as an ongoing, messy, beautiful process
  • The divine council and Christ's eternal presence
  • Eve reinterpreted: from "helper" to powerful partner
  • Separating light from darkness as a life principle
  • Joy found in the creative process, not just the finished product
Why These Scriptures Matter

The creation narratives are not merely ancient cosmology—they establish foundational truths about who we are and why we're here. As John Hilton III emphasized: "Before you were ever born, Christ was there and he is not going anywhere." These chapters reveal that we existed before this life, that we participated in the pre-mortal council, and that creation itself was an act of profound joy.

The temple endowment centers on these creation accounts for good reason. Understanding them deepens our appreciation for our own divine potential and the covenants we make.

Major Themes from This Week's Commentary
1. Creation as Ongoing Process

Dr. Rebekah Call (Follow Him) offers a paradigm-shifting perspective: creation isn't a finished event—it's an ongoing process that includes us.

"Sometimes it's easy to forget that the creative process is messy."

Life's messiness—our mistakes, struggles, and disorder—isn't a deviation from God's plan; it IS the plan. We came here to learn good from evil through experience, and that requires the opportunity to make mistakes.

Application: When life feels chaotic, remember that God works through the mess to create something beautiful in us.

2. Christ's Eternal Presence

John Hilton III (Scripture Central) beautifully articulates Christ's role from the beginning:

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."

Christ wasn't an afterthought added to address the fall—He was central to creation itself. The joy that accompanied creation (Job 38:7) continues in Christ's work with us today.

Key Insight: Every step of our personal "creation" is valuable to God. He doesn't just care about the finished product—He celebrates our day-to-day progress.

3. Reinterpreting Eve: Ezer Kenegdo

Dr. Rebekah Call shared groundbreaking research on the Hebrew phrase traditionally translated "help meet" (עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ, ezer kenegdo):

"What our theology does for Eve. That's what I'm so excited about. Changes everything."

The word ezer (helper) is the same word used to describe God himself as Israel's helper. This isn't a subordinate role—it's a powerful, essential partnership. Latter-day revelation restores Eve's agency and wisdom in choosing to partake of the fruit.

4. Separating Light from Darkness

Scripture Central highlighted a practical principle from Day 1 of creation:

"When we separate out the light from the darkness, remember that the Savior said, 'I am the light of the world.'"

Just as God's first creative act was separating light from darkness, we're invited to do the same in our lives—prioritizing what truly matters over lesser distractions.

"Sometimes the very good is the biggest enemy of the best."

Application: What "good" things might be crowding out the "best" things in your life? Temple attendance? Meaningful scripture study? Quality family time?

5. The Divine Council

Talking Scripture explored the rich Hebrew of Genesis 1:1, revealing that creation involved a divine council:

"The concept of a grand council where Elohim (Heavenly Father) and other divine beings participate in creation aligns with LDS beliefs about Heavenly Mother, Jesus Christ, Holy Ghost, and the noble and great ones."

The plural "let us" in Genesis reflects this council—we were there, we participated, and we shouted for joy at the plan.

6. Time, Number, and Creation: Reading Genesis with Ancient Eyes

One of the most persistent tensions in modern religious discourse is the apparent conflict between the biblical creation account and contemporary scientific models. This tension, however, does not arise from the biblical text itself—it emerges from a mismatch of mathematical languages.

The Modern Assumption vs. Ancient Reality

Modern readers instinctively assume time consists of uniform, measurable units. But this way of thinking depends on mathematical concepts that were not available to the biblical world:

  • Positional decimal notation
  • Zero as a placeholder
  • Zero as a number

These concepts developed very late—zero as a true mathematical number emerges only in the 7th century AD with Brahmagupta. The biblical texts were written more than a millennium earlier.

What Ancient Numbers Meant

In the ancient Mediterranean world, Hebrew, Greek, and Roman cultures used letters to represent numbers. There was no symbol for zero, and numbers often carried qualitative meaning in addition to quantity.

The Hebrew term אֶלֶף (eleph, "thousand") could denote:

  • A literal thousand
  • A clan or military unit
  • A large organized group under a single leader

When Psalm 90:4 declares that "a thousand years" in God's sight are like a single day, it expresses a relational truth about divine time—not a conversion formula.

**The Meaning of Yom (Day)**

The Hebrew word יוֹם (yom), translated "day" in Genesis 1, does not intrinsically mean a twenty-four-hour period. Throughout the Hebrew Bible, yom may refer to:

  • Daylight hours
  • A full day-night cycle
  • An unspecified period
  • An extended era

Genesis itself uses yom flexibly. Genesis 2:4 speaks of "the day" in which God made heaven and earth—summarizing the entire creation sequence in a single yom.

A Harmonic Model of Creation

A musical analogy illuminates the structure: In the harmonic overtone series, a fundamental tone establishes identity, overtones emerge in ordered relationship, each with different wavelengths—yet all belong to one coherent system.

Applied to creation, this suggests the "days" of Genesis are not equal temporal units but harmonically related stages. Creation unfolds harmonically, not mechanically.

No Necessary Conflict

Modern science investigates mechanisms, processes, and durations. Scripture proclaims order, purpose, and meaning. These are different questions, addressed through different languages.

The biblical creation account was composed in a world without zero, without decimal place value, and without uniform units of time. Its numbers are symbolic and relational; its "days" are ordered phases of divine activity.

Read this way, Genesis does not compress creation into six short days—it expands our understanding of time itself.

The problem was never the text. The problem was the math we brought with us.

7. Comprehensive Textual Comparison: Three Creation Accounts

A detailed verse-by-verse analysis reveals systematic differences across Genesis, Moses, and Abraham that illuminate distinct theological emphases:

Divine Identity:

  • Genesis uses singular "God" with hints of plurality ("Let us")
  • Moses clarifies the Godhead: "I, God, said unto mine Only Begotten"
  • Abraham consistently employs plural "the Gods" with council language

Creation Vocabulary:

Genesis/Moses Abraham Theological Shift
Created Organized Implicit → Explicit ex materia
Made Prepared Command → Planning
Was good They obeyed Quality → Compliance
Day Time Earth time → Kolob time
Firmament Expanse Solid dome → Open space

Unique Content:

  • Moses alone teaches: spirit creation preceded physical; agency explicitly granted ("thou mayest choose for thyself")
  • Abraham alone teaches: explicit council planning; relative time (Kolob); spirit placed into body

Key Insight (Abraham 4:18): "The Gods watched those things which they had ordered until they obeyed"—creation as matter responding to divine will rather than instantaneous command.

Notes on Key Terms

**On bara (create):** The Hebrew bara is used only of God as the acting subject—never of human activity. But its significance lies not in "creation from nothing" (a later theological interpretation) but in marking discontinuity. Bara appears at three key moments in Genesis 1: the creation of the cosmos (v. 1), the creation of animal life (v. 21), and the creation of humanity (v. 27). These mark the three great discontinuities—where something genuinely new appears that cannot be derived from what came before. Humans make (asah); only God creates (bara). The Septuagint translates this with epoiēsen (from poieō, "to make"), while the Vulgate uses creavit (from creo, "to create").

**On ezer (helper):** Far from implying subordination, ezer describes powerful, rescuing aid. Of its 21 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible, 16 refer to God as Israel's helper. The Greek boēthos literally means "one who runs to the cry"—urgent, vital rescue. The Latin adiutorium (whence English "aid") appears in the liturgical prayer Deus, in adiutorium meum intende ("O God, come to my assistance").

**On yom (day):** The Hebrew yom appears over 2,300 times in the Old Testament with meanings ranging from daylight hours to extended eras. Genesis 2:4 uses yom to describe the entire creation sequence as a single "day," demonstrating its flexibility. The Greek hēmera and Latin dies carry similar semantic range.

Closing Thought

President Emily Bell Freeman's insight captures the spirit of this week's study:

"Within the beautiful imagery of the creation, in the midst of every step, there is an oft-repeated phrase: God saw that it was good."

God doesn't wait for perfection to pronounce His work good. Neither should we. The very act of striving, learning, and growing—messy as it is—is pleasing to Him. We are, each of us, works in progress, and that is exactly how it should be.
