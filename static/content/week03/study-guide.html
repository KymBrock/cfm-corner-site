This week we encounter the most foundational narrative in scripture: the Creation. We read this account not once, but three times—in Genesis, Moses, and Abraham—each offering unique perspectives that illuminate different aspects of God's creative work.

Genesis 1–2 provides the familiar biblical account, structured around seven days of creative work and rest. Chapter 1 presents a cosmic, ordered creation, while Chapter 2 zooms in on the garden, the creation of Adam, and the forming of Eve as his perfect companion.

Moses 2–3 offers the same narrative but through the lens of Moses's vision (introduced in Moses 1). Here we learn that Christ ("mine Only Begotten") was the Creator under the Father's direction. Moses adds clarifying details, such as the spiritual creation preceding the physical.

Abraham 4–5 provides the most distinctive account. The language shifts from "God" to "the Gods" (reflecting the divine council introduced in Abraham 3), and the vocabulary changes from "created" to "organized." Here creation is explicitly a collaborative work of the Godhead preparing a place for Abraham's posterity—and by extension, all of God's children.

Together, these three accounts reveal creation as temple-building, identity-establishing, and covenant-inaugurating. The pattern of seven, the divine rest, the image of God in humanity, and the institution of marriage all carry profound theological weight.

Theme 1: Creation as Temple-Building

The creation narrative is fundamentally a temple text. Taylor Halverson (Scripture Central) notes that the number seven appears throughout as a "covenant signature"—the same pattern seen in Israelite temple construction. God doesn't merely make a world; He constructs a cosmic temple where He will dwell with His children.

The garden of Eden, with its eastward orientation, its guarded entrance, its precious materials, and its divine presence, prefigures every temple that will follow. When God "rested" on the seventh day, the Hebrew shabbat (שָׁבַת) implies not exhaustion but enthronement—the divine King taking His seat in His completed palace.

Theme 2: Humanity in God's Image

"Let us make man in our image, after our likeness" (Genesis 1:26). This declaration is the theological climax of the creation week. The Hebrew tselem (צֶלֶם, "image") refers to a representative statue or likeness—in the ancient Near East, kings placed their images throughout their kingdoms to represent their authority and presence. Humanity is God's living image, placed in creation to represent Him and exercise stewardship on His behalf.

The paired term demut (דְּמוּת, "likeness") adds the dimension of resemblance. Together, the terms convey both function (we represent God) and nature (we are like Him). This is not mere metaphor—Latter-day revelation confirms that God the Father and Jesus Christ possess glorified, tangible bodies (D&C 130:22), and we are literally created in that image.

This continues Week 02's theme of divine identity. Having learned "thou art my son" (Moses 1:4), we now learn what that means: we bear God's image and share His likeness. We are not merely creatures; we are His children with the potential to become like Him (Moses 1:39; D&C 76:58).

This doctrine carries profound implications for how we view ourselves and others. Because every person bears God's image, every person possesses inherent, inviolable dignity. Genesis 9:6 grounds the prohibition against murder in this reality: "for in the image of God made he man." James likewise warns against cursing "men, which are made after the similitude of God" (James 3:9). To demean or mistreat another human being is to dishonor the divine image they carry.

The Savior's prayer in John 17 illuminates where this image-bearing leads. Christ prays "that they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us" (John 17:21). The "oneness" He describes is not a merging of identity but a unity of purpose, will, and love—the same unity that defines the Godhead. We who bear God's image are invited to grow into His likeness through covenant, becoming one with Him even as the Father and Son are one.

Theme 3: Marriage as Creation Ordinance

The formation of Eve and the institution of marriage in Genesis 2/Moses 3/Abraham 5 establishes the eternal pattern. The Hebrew phrase ezer kenegdo (עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ), often translated "help meet," actually means something like "a power/strength corresponding to him." Eve is not subordinate help but essential counterpart—without whom Adam cannot fulfill the divine mandate.

The "one flesh" union (Genesis 2:24) is the first covenant established in Eden, instituted before the Fall, making marriage the oldest ordinance in human history.

Theme 4: Organized, Not Created

Abraham's account introduces crucial vocabulary. The Gods "organized" rather than creating ex nihilo (from nothing). This aligns with D&C 93:29: "Intelligence, or the light of truth, was not created or made." God works with eternal materials, organizing chaos into cosmos.

Even the opening word of Genesis may hint at this. The Hebrew bereshit (בְּרֵאשִׁית) is built from rosh (רֹאשׁ, "head") with the suffix -it (ית). This suffix frequently forms words denoting a portion derived from something larger: kaf (כַּף, "spoon") becomes kapit (כַּפִּית, "teaspoon"); sak (שַׂק, "sack") becomes sakit (שַׂקִּית, "small bag"); shear (שְׁאָר, "remainder") becomes sheerit (שְׁאֵרִית, "remnant"). Similarly, reshit means not just "beginning" but "firstfruits" — the initial portion set apart from a harvest (Leviticus 23:10; Proverbs 3:9).

A grammatical detail reinforces this reading. The traditional vocalization uses a sheva under the bet (בְּרֵאשִׁית, bereshit), which functions as an indefinite article — "in a beginning." If the text intended "in the beginning" as an absolute cosmic origin, we would expect a qamatz (בָּרֵאשִׁית, bareshit) indicating the definite article. The ancient Greek Septuagint confirms this, rendering the phrase without a definite article. This suggests Genesis opens not with the absolute beginning of all existence, but with a beginning — the beginning of this earth's organization, and more particularly, the beginning of God's covenant relationship with humanity.

This "firstfruits" meaning connects remarkably to Abraham's vision of the premortal council. God showed Abraham "the intelligences that were organized before the world was; and among all these there were many of the noble and great ones" (Abraham 3:22). These were the reshit — the chief portion, the firstfruits among eternal intelligences — whom God chose to be rulers. And in Abraham's account, Jehovah was chosen to be the rosh, the head of that council, serving and giving glory to the Father (Abraham 3:24–27). Creation, then, is not an absolute beginning from nothing but God working with the reshit of what already existed, organized under the rosh who executes the Father's will.

This has profound implications: we ourselves were not created from nothing but organized from eternal intelligence. Our core identity is as old as God Himself.

Why ANE Context Matters for These Chapters

The creation accounts were not written in a vacuum. They entered a world full of competing creation stories—Egyptian, Babylonian, Canaanite, and Sumerian myths that Israel would have known. Understanding these alternatives reveals what Genesis/Moses/Abraham affirm and deny.

What Moses/Genesis Corrects:

  • One God, not many: Clarifies one divine God, not the plethora of competing gods typical of the ancient world
  • Matter unorganized, not deified chaos: Unlike Egyptian cosmology where primordial chaos was itself a deity (Nun), Genesis presents the formless material as simply that — material, not a god. Restoration scripture calls this "matter unorganized" (Abraham 4:1; D&C 93:33)
  • Creation by divine command: Like Memphite theology but attributed to one sovereign God
  • Purpose for humanity: Humans are not afterthoughts or servants to feed and fight for the gods and their whims; they are the pinnacle, made in God's image
  • No divine combat: Unlike myths where creation results from cosmic battles between gods
  • Nature serves God, not the reverse: Ancient traditions created stories to describe and deify natural events — the sun, storms, rivers, and fertility each had their own god requiring appeasement. The Moses and Abraham accounts correct this: natural events serve God's purposes. Nature is not God, but God controls nature. The sun and moon are simply "lights" (Genesis 1:16) — not the deities Shamash and Sin worshipped in Mesopotamia, nor Ra and Thoth in Egypt

Mesopotamian Creation Myths (Abraham's Context)

**The Enuma Elish (Babylonian Creation Epic):**

  • Divine combat: Marduk kills Tiamat (chaos dragon) and creates the world from her corpse
  • Humanity's purpose: Humans created to serve gods and relieve them of labor
  • Polytheism: Multiple generations of gods with conflict and intrigue

Other Mesopotamian Elements:

  • Ziggurats as artificial mountains to reach heaven
  • Astral worship—sun, moon, and stars as deities
  • Divine genealogies — gods producing other gods through successive generations

What Abraham 4–5 Corrects:

  • "The Gods" ≠ polytheism: The divine council works in unity under the Father
  • Creation ≠ divine combat: Unlike myths where the earth is formed from the corpse of a slain god (Tiamat), creation results from deliberate organization. The war in heaven (Abraham 3:27–28; Revelation 12:7–9) was a moral conflict over agency and the plan of salvation — not competing gods battling for cosmic supremacy
  • Stars governed, not divine: Celestial bodies obey, not command (from Abraham 3)
  • Humanity's dignity: Not servants to relieve gods, but children to inherit

The Sabbath vs. ANE Rest

In Mesopotamian mythology, when a god "rested," it typically meant the god had finished his temple and taken up residence there. The Enuma Elish concludes with Marduk enthroned in his temple after defeating Tiamat.

Genesis Parallel:

  • God completes His work (the cosmic temple)
  • God "rests" (shabbat)—not from exhaustion but in enthronement
  • The seventh day is blessed and sanctified

The Difference: In Israel, all people share in this rest through the Sabbath. It's not just divine privilege; it's democratized holiness.

ANE Background: Royal Images

In the ancient Near East, a king would place images (selem, צֶלֶם in Hebrew) of himself throughout his kingdom. These statues represented his authority and presence in places he couldn't physically be.

Egypt: Pharaohs erected statues; the statue was the pharaoh for purposes of worship and presence

Mesopotamia: Kings placed images at borders and in temples as extensions of their rule

Genesis Application

When God says humanity is made in His image (tselem) and likeness (demut), the ancient hearer would understand: humans are God's living statues, placed in creation to represent His authority and presence.

Radical Implications:

  • Not just kings but all humans are in God's image
  • Humanity is God's representatives, not His servants or slaves
  • This grounds human dignity, worth, and purpose

"One Flesh" — Covenant Union

The statement "they shall be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24) establishes the first human covenant. In ANE cultures, covenant-making often involved:

  • Verbal declaration
  • Witnesses
  • Physical act or symbol
  • Binding commitment

Marriage in Eden contains all these elements. It is the first ordinance, instituted before the Fall, and therefore part of God's original, unfallen design for humanity.

  1. Listen to the Spirit.

Personal revelation may illuminate how to make the Sabbath truly holy in your circumstances.

  1. Balance with other revelation.

The Sabbath points forward to the ultimate rest in God's presence (Hebrews 4:1–11).

  1. Opposition in all things.

Rest is contrasted with labor; holy time with ordinary time. The distinction sanctifies both.

  1. Why are there three creation accounts?

Each offers a complementary perspective: Genesis provides the historical foundation, Moses reveals Christ as Creator, Abraham shows the divine council's collaboration. Together they give fullness.

  1. Is this a science text?

No. The creation accounts are theological, not scientific. They answer who and why, not how and when. Attempting to extract modern scientific information misreads the genre.

  1. What does "image of God" actually mean?

In ANE context, it means humanity is God's living representative, placed in creation to exercise His delegated authority and reflect His character.

  1. Why is marriage so prominent in the creation account?

Because human partnership and procreation are central to God's purpose. Marriage is not a social construct but a creation ordinance, preceding all others.

  1. What's the significance of "The Gods" in Abraham?

It reveals creation as the work of the united Godhead—Father, Son, and divine council—not a lone deity. This anticipates temple understanding.

3. Key Passages Study: Genesis 1–2; Moses 2–3; Abraham 4–5

What This Week Covers

This week presents the creation account in three parallel versions—Genesis, Moses, and Abraham—offering the most complete picture of how God brought our world into being. Together, these six chapters establish foundational truths about God's nature, humanity's identity, and the purpose of existence.

Genesis 1–2 provides the familiar biblical account structured around seven days. Chapter 1 is cosmic and orderly, presenting creation as God's word bringing form to chaos. Chapter 2 narrows focus to the garden, the creation of Adam from dust, and Eve from Adam's side.

Moses 2–3 offers the same events but frames them as part of Moses's vision (from Moses 1). Key additions include: Christ explicitly identified as Creator ("mine Only Begotten"), and clear indications of spiritual creation preceding physical creation.

Abraham 4–5 provides the most distinctive perspective. The language shifts from "God" to "the Gods," reflecting the divine council's collaborative work. The vocabulary changes from "created" to "organized," emphasizing that God works with eternal materials rather than creating from nothing.

Genre & Form

The creation accounts blend several genres:

  • Cosmogony — Account of the world's origin
  • Temple Text — Establishing sacred space where God dwells
  • Poetic Structure — Especially Genesis 1's refrains and parallel days
  • Narrative — Especially Genesis 2's story of Adam and Eve

Structural Overview

Genesis 1 follows a 3+3+1 pattern:

  • Days 1–3: Creating REALMS (light/dark, sky/water, land/plants)
  • Days 4–6: Creating RULERS for those realms (luminaries, fish/birds, animals/humans)
  • Day 7: SABBATH—God's enthronement in His cosmic temple

Genesis 2 follows a problem-solution pattern:

  • Problem: No one to till the ground (v. 5)
  • Solution: Adam formed (v. 7)
  • Problem: Not good for man to be alone (v. 18)
  • Solution: Eve formed (vv. 21–22)
  • Resolution: One flesh (v. 24)

Abraham 4–5 follows a council-execution pattern:

  • "The Gods" deliberate ("Let us...")
  • They execute the plan
  • They observe and approve

4. Word Studies: Genesis 1–2; Moses 2–3; Abraham 4–5

This week's creation accounts contain some of the most theologically significant Hebrew vocabulary in all of scripture. Understanding these terms unlocks layers of meaning that translation alone cannot convey.

Why Study Hebrew for Creation?

  1. Translation flattens meaning — "Create" and "made" in English obscure distinctions Hebrew carefully maintains
  2. Theological precision — Terms like bara (create) and asah (make) carry different implications
  3. Pattern recognition — Hebrew wordplays and structural features become visible
  4. Deeper appreciation — Seeing the original language enriches worship and study

1. bara — Create

בָּרָא

Layer 1: Hebrew Foundation

ElementDetails
Strong's NumberH1254
Transliterationbara
Pronunciationbaw-RAW
Rootb-r-ʾ (ב-ר-א)
Root MeaningTo create, shape, form; used exclusively of divine activity
Part of SpeechVerb (Qal perfect)

Key Insight:

Bara is used only of God as the acting subject in the Hebrew Bible—never of human activity. But its significance lies not in "creation from nothing" (a later theological interpretation) but in marking discontinuity—moments where something genuinely new appears that cannot be derived from what came before. Humans make (asah); only God creates (bara).

Occurrences This Week:

  • Genesis 1:1 — "God created (בָּרָא) the heaven and the earth"
  • Genesis 1:21 — "God created (בָּרָא) great whales"
  • Genesis 1:27 — "God created (בָּרָא) man in his own image... male and female created (בָּרָא) he them"
  • Genesis 2:3 — "God created (בָּרָא) and made (עָשָׂה)"

Theological Significance:

Bara appears at three key moments in Genesis 1: the creation of the cosmos (v. 1), the creation of animal life (v. 21), and the creation of humanity (v. 27). These mark the three great discontinuities in creation — where something genuinely new appears.

Abraham's Vocabulary:

Notably, Abraham 4 uses "organized" rather than "created" — this is not contradiction but complementary revelation. Both are true: God creates (brings genuinely new things into being) and organizes (works with eternal materials).

ElementDetails

Why This Matters:

The LXX translators chose poieō (ἐποίησεν), a general term for "making," rather than a more specialized word. This broadens the semantic range but loses the Hebrew distinction between bara (divine-only creation) and asah (general making). New Testament writers inherit this translation choice.

Key Insight:

Tov appears seven times in Genesis 1 (vv. 4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25, 31) — a covenant signature number. The final occurrence adds me'od (מְאֹד, "very/exceedingly"), creating the superlative "very good."

**The Seven-Fold Tov:**

  1. v. 4 — Light is tov
  2. v. 10 — Land and seas are tov
  3. v. 12 — Vegetation is tov
  4. v. 18 — Luminaries are tov
  5. v. 21 — Sea creatures and birds are tov
  6. v. 25 — Land animals are tov
  7. v. 31 — Everything together is tov me'od (very good)

What "Good" Means Here:

Tov doesn't primarily mean morally good (though that's included). It means fitting, suitable, fulfilling its purpose. Each creation is tov because it does what God designed it to do. The whole is tov me'od because everything together fulfills God's cosmic purpose.

Part 3: Image of God — ANE Background (10 min)

  • In ANE, tselem = royal statue representing the king's authority
  • Genesis democratizes this: ALL humans, not just kings, bear God's image
  • This grounds human dignity, worth, and vocation

Discussion: How should the "image of God" doctrine shape our ethics?

Part 4: Sabbath Theology (10 min)

  • In ANE, divine "rest" = enthronement in completed temple
  • God's rest on day 7 = taking His seat as King in His cosmic temple
  • The Sabbath has no "evening and morning" — it remains open (Hebrews 4:9–11)

Discussion: What does it mean that we're invited into God's rest?

Part 5: Marriage as Creation Ordinance (5 min)

  • Instituted BEFORE the Fall — not a remedy for fallen nature
  • Ezer kenegdo = strength/power corresponding — not subordination
  • "One flesh" = the first human covenant

Application: How does knowing marriage is pre-Fall change how we view it?

Closing Challenge:

Assign students to write a one-page reflection: "What does it mean to be made in God's image, and how should this shape my life?"
