The Grief That Comes Afterward ▶︎

There is a kind of trouble that only shows up once the trouble is over.

While the thing is happening, you are busy. There are decisions to make, people to call, a roof to patch. It is afterward — when the house is quiet and nobody is asking you for anything — that you have to decide what to think about a God who let it happen.

That is the exact position of the people these chapters address. Last week's reading was spoken into Isaiah's own world, with Assyria at the gate and Jerusalem holding on. This week nobody is bargaining. The armies have come and gone.

Here is how the week opens to them.

"Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God… cry unto her, that her warfare is accomplished."

Her warfare is accomplished. Past tense — before anything has visibly changed for the exiles. The account against them is settled — it is no longer what stands between them and God. The exile itself is not over. These chapters are not addressed to people bracing for a blow. They are addressed to people living on the far side of one, in Babylon, a century and a half after Isaiah's own lifetime, many of whom had never seen Jerusalem.

These chapters do not tell anyone the loss was smaller than it felt, and they do not explain it away. They argue — which is why so much of this week reads forceful rather than gentle. If you have ever wanted something to read in that particular season — not during, but after — this is the week.

(If you are already asking how Isaiah can be speaking to people born long after him, that question is taken up at the end of this page, under If a Question Stays With You. What follows here is not a walkthrough — the Overview does that, chapter by chapter. These are the handful of things worth stopping over.)

Babylon and the God Who Carries His People ▶︎

Babylon had conquered Jerusalem and taken many of its people away. To someone living under that empire, it did not look like a phase. It looked like how the world is now.

Isaiah's answer to that is not an argument. It is a picture of moving day.

"Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth, their idols were upon the beasts, and upon the cattle: your carriages were heavy loaden; they are a burden to the weary beast… they could not deliver the burden, but themselves are gone into captivity." (Isaiah 46:1–2)

Bel and Nebo were two of the empire's great gods. Here they are freight — and the verse says plainly what that comes to: they could not deliver the burden. The gods went into exile too, on a cart.

Then the Lord answers the idols' failure by doing what they cannot: He carries His people and delivers them.

"which are borne by me from the belly, which are carried from the womb… and even to hoar hairs will I carry you: I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you." (Isaiah 46:3–4)

From the womb to grey hair — the whole span of a life, with no stretch of it where they were carrying themselves. The contrast is not that our God is stronger than theirs. It is that one kind of god rides, and the other kind carries.

The next chapter turns on the empire herself. Isaiah 47 writes Babylon as a woman who expects to sit comfortably for ever — and it is blunt about what she did to the captives in her hands: "thou didst shew them no mercy; upon the ancient hast thou very heavily laid thy yoke." (The Overview sets out what she says and what the Lord says back.)

Later scripture also uses Babylon as a symbol. Revelation 18:4 calls God's people to come out of her so they will not take part in her sins. D&C 133:14 calls people out of wickedness, which it names "spiritual Babylon." In those later passages, leaving Babylon means turning away from a way of life that draws people from God, not merely moving away from a city.

Which leaves a reader with one question, and it is worth sitting with rather than answering quickly: what am I carrying, and what is carrying me?

Work, standing, comfort, an ambition — none of these is wrong to value, and Isaiah's test is not about how good the thing is. It is about which direction the weight runs. Anything you have to keep propping up is not holding you up.

The prodigal is that same discovery told from the inside. A son decides his father's house is smaller than what is out there, finds out otherwise, and starts home with a speech ready — including the line "make me as one of thy hired servants." He never gets to that line. His father sees him "a great way off", and runs, and reaches him before the speech is finished (Luke 15:18–22).

And two things belong beside that, because an analogy like it can be made cruel. Scripture does not say every exile came home wiser, or that a hard season reliably teaches anybody anything. And a great deal of suffering follows from somebody else's choices, or from nothing but the ordinary hardness of being mortal. To a reader in that position, Isaiah 46 is not an accusation. It is the same promise as everyone else gets: "even I will carry, and will deliver you."

What "Wait Upon the Lord" Actually Asks ▶︎

Isaiah 40:31 closes a chapter that has just finished saying how small the nations are and how tired the young men get:

"But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint."

Read who the verse before it is about. "Even the youths shall faint and be weary, and the young men shall utterly fall" (40:30). Isaiah picks the fittest people he can think of and uses them as his example of running out. So the promise is not aimed at people who are managing. It is aimed at the ones already empty — which is close to the reverse of how the verse usually gets quoted at somebody.

And look at where the list ends. Mount up, run, walk. The plainest of the three comes last.

Waiting here means continuing to look to the Lord for strength when your own has run out; it is not passive resignation, and it is not a demand to pretend you are okay. Your weariness is not a measure of your faith or worthiness. If today you can only manage the next step—or even the next breath—the promise meets you exactly where you are.

The Promise That Assumes the Fire ▶︎

Isaiah 43:2 never promises that the waters will be cancelled or the fire left unlit. The waters, the rivers, the fire and the flame are all assumed; what is promised is company inside them. The Overview places that promise in Isaiah 43's chapter story; here we stay with what it can and cannot promise a person in distress.

The chapter has been speaking to the whole nation as though to one person from its opening words — "thus saith the LORD that created thee, O Jacob" — and then it presses that singular all the way down: "I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine."

Not a name. Thy name. A whole people is being addressed the way you would address one person standing in front of you. The names in the verse are still Jacob and Israel — a nation, not you.

And elsewhere in scripture that promise gets a scene, with three men in it who have names.

Shadrach, Meshach and Abed-nego were told to bow to a golden image or be thrown into a furnace. What they said before the fire was lit is the part most worth having:

"Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods."

— Daniel 3:17–18

But if not. They do not claim to know they will be rescued. They say He is able, and that their answer is the same either way — and they said it before they knew which it would be.

Then they were thrown in — and the king who ordered it looked and said: "Lo, I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God" (Daniel 3:25). He had thrown in three men. Now he saw four.

Daniel 3 is a parallel, not a prediction — and it is a rescue, which not everyone gets. Scripture also uses fire for more than one thing: Key Passages, passage 5 sets this one beside the furnace of Isaiah 48, and the difference matters to anyone standing in one.

The three men's answer is worth carrying into an uncertain week: He is able — but if not, we will still serve Him. That is the difference between trusting God and trusting a particular outcome.

Where the Whole Week Has Been Going ▶︎

Ten chapters of argument. Courtroom summons, satire, evidence, empire politics, a foreign king named — on the book's own account — long before he was born. And then, in chapter 49, the audience speaks in its own voice. And what comes out is not a rebuttal.

"But Zion said, The LORD hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me."

After everything, that is what they actually think. And the reply is not a tenth chapter of proof.

"Can a woman forget her sucking child…? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands."

Something cut in, permanently, where the hands are always in view.

The case is won with an argument. The person is answered with a scar.

For Latter-day Saints, Isaiah's image may bring to mind Bountiful, where the people came to the risen Savior one by one and "did see with their eyes and did feel with their hands" the prints of the nails in His hands and feet (3 Nephi 11:15) — though Isaiah is speaking of God's promise to remember Zion, not describing the Crucifixion. Both can lead us back to the promise: "yet will I not forget thee."

One Thing to Take With You ▶︎

Nephi copied Isaiah 48 and 49 for a family that had left Jerusalem. His version of Isaiah 49 speaks to people who have been "broken off and ... driven out" (1 Nephi 21:1). That opening would have sounded close to home. Historical & Cultural Context sets the two texts side by side.

"Comfort ye, comfort ye my people." It was said to people who had lost, and it was said twice.

A word on the dating question, since it is the one readers raise first. How can Isaiah be speaking to people born a century and a half after him? The book does not avoid the question — it names the Persian king Cyrus outright, at 44:28 and 45:1, generations before there was a Persian empire for him to rule, and it offers that as evidence rather than apologising for it.

Whom a prophecy is addressed to, and when it was written down, are two different questions. Historical & Cultural Context sets out the main readings side by side and leaves the question open, because it is genuinely open. Nothing in this week's reading depends on how it comes out.
