First, a Word About Where We Are ▶︎

Last week we reached the end of Psalms. After so many prayers about trouble, the book closes with an invitation: "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD." It does not pretend the laments away; it goes through them.

This week we open two books that ask how to live wisely. Proverbs teaches habits worth cultivating; Ecclesiastes asks what happens when hard work and good judgement cannot secure the outcome we hoped for.

The Overview follows both books chapter by chapter. This letter offers a few starting points for your own reading.

A Letter to Fellow Students ▶︎

You may know "He shall direct thy paths" and "To every thing there is a season" by heart. This week, try reading the sentences around them. Both become more useful when we let the whole passage speak, especially when we need guidance and cannot yet see what comes next.

A note on the two books ▶︎

The tension between these books need not make either one less valuable. We need wise counsel, and we also need room to acknowledge what we cannot explain. Read them together without asking either to do all the work.

"Lean Not Unto Thine Own Understanding" — the Half We Skip ▶︎

Start with Proverbs 3:5–6 and notice what comes between trusting the Lord and receiving His direction: "lean not unto thine own understanding." The verse does not ask us to stop thinking. It asks us not to make our own judgement the final authority. Key Passages, passage 2 explores the Hebrew imagery of leaning and making a path straight.

Here is the question I keep returning to: can I act on the direction I have without demanding to know the whole route? God can guide us in many ways. This passage gives us reason to trust Him even when the next step is clearer than the destination.

A Mother's Counsel and a Woman's Strength ▶︎

Proverbs 31 begins with counsel a mother taught her son, King Lemuel. The poem that follows, verses 10–31, praises a woman's work, generosity and wisdom. The text does not identify the poem's author, so we should distinguish the mother's opening counsel from the portrait that follows it.

One helpful companion reading is Ruth 3:11, where Boaz calls Ruth a "virtuous woman." Key Passages, passage 5 explains the shared expression and the poem's alphabetical structure.

Ruth is a foreign widow working to support herself and Naomi. Read her story beside the woman who buys a field and opens her hand to the poor in Proverbs 31. Their circumstances differ, but both show strength through faithful action. The comparison helps us read the poem as praise, not as a schedule one woman must somehow complete every day.

The Man Who Ran the Experiment ▶︎

In Ecclesiastes 2, the speaker pursues pleasure, building projects and wealth, yet cannot find lasting satisfaction in his achievements. That is a recognisable disappointment: reaching a goal does not always bring the peace we expected. The book also makes room for enjoying daily gifts (2:24–25), so its questions are not a command to stop enjoying life.

Its unsettling passages troubled ancient readers too. Jewish Perspective, section 1 describes the recorded dispute over its sacred status. You need not settle every question before this book can help you name an experience honestly.

Eternity in the Heart, and the View Withheld ▶︎

After the familiar list of seasons in Ecclesiastes 3:1–8, read verse 11. It holds together a longing to understand and the limits of what we can see. Key Passages, passage 6 explains why many translations use eternity where the King James has world, and sets the passage beside Moses 1.

I find that longing familiar. We want to know how the pieces of a life fit together; often we can see only a few. Not understanding the whole does not make the part we have been given meaningless.

"The Whole of Man" ▶︎

Ecclesiastes ends by calling us to reverence, obedience and accountability before God (Ecclesiastes 12:13–14). Key Passages, passage 7 explores the translation "the whole duty of man" and why it can invite reflection on a whole life, not merely a list of completed obligations.

What would it mean to let those commitments shape the decision in front of you today?

The Invitation ▶︎

Both books end up in the same place, by opposite roads. Proverbs says trust Him because the world has a grain and He knows it. Ecclesiastes says trust Him because you have seen how little of it you can hold. Neither is offering you a technique for a well-run life. Both are pointing at a Person.

And if you are carrying a decision this week, try the small thing rather than the large one. Take the decision you actually have, read those two verses against it honestly, and ask what you are currently leaning your weight on. Most of us find the answer is my own understanding. Finding that is the beginning of the week's work, and it is enough.
