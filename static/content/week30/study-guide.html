1. Week 30: Overview

This week's reading gathers four kings of Judah — Asa, Jehoshaphat, Uzziah, and Hezekiah — and reads their reigns through the distinctive lens of the Chronicler. Second Chronicles is not simply a retelling of Kings; it is a Second-Temple, worship-centered history written to a community rebuilding after exile, and it presses one question relentlessly: when the crisis comes, on whom will you lean? The word that runs like a thread through these chapters is darash — "to seek." Kings who seek the Lord are strengthened and given rest; kings who lean on their own strength, on foreign alliances, or on their own pride are broken. The pattern is stated outright by the prophet Azariah: "The Lord is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you; but if ye forsake him, he will forsake you" (2 Chronicles 15:2).

The theme verse comes from the reading's dramatic center. When a vast coalition of Moab, Ammon, and their allies marches on Judah, King Jehoshaphat does not muster first — he seeks first. He proclaims a fast, gathers the nation, and prays a prayer that ends in disarming honesty: "we have no might against this great company that cometh against us; neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee" (2 Chronicles 20:12). The answer comes through a Levite singer: "Be not afraid nor dismayed by reason of this great multitude; for the battle is not yours, but God's" (20:15). "Ye shall not need to fight in this battle: set yourselves, stand ye still, and see the salvation of the Lord" (20:17). Judah wins the battle by sending its choir out ahead of its army, singing "for his mercy endureth for ever" — and the enemy destroys itself (20:21–24). It is the purest picture in scripture of what it means to stand still and let God fight.

But the reading is honest about how fragile trust can be. Asa begins by casting himself entirely on God against a million-man army — "help us, O Lord our God; for we rest on thee" (14:11) — and ends his reign leaning on the king of Syria instead of God, imprisoning the prophet who rebukes him, and, when disease strikes his feet, seeking "not to the Lord, but to the physicians" (16:12). Uzziah is "marvellously helped, till he was strong" — and then "when he was strong, his heart was lifted up to his destruction," and the king who prospered by seeking God is struck with leprosy for seizing the priests' incense (26:15–16). The same soul can start out trusting God and end up trusting itself; strength given by God can curdle into pride against God.

The reading closes on a gentler note. Hezekiah reopens the temple and keeps a great Passover, inviting even the scattered remnant of the northern kingdom to "turn again unto the Lord" (30:6). When many arrive ritually unprepared and eat the Passover "otherwise than it was written," Hezekiah prays not for judgment but for mercy: "The good Lord pardon every one that prepareth his heart to seek God... though he be not cleansed according to the purification of the sanctuary" (30:18–19). "And the Lord hearkened to Hezekiah, and healed the people" (30:20). The chapter that began in ruin ends in the greatest joy Jerusalem had known since Solomon (30:26) — because God honors the prepared heart over the perfect procedure.

For Latter-day Saint readers, these chapters are a sustained meditation on the counsel to trust in the Lord rather than "the arm of flesh" (2 Nephi 4:34; cf. Jeremiah 17:5), on the pride cycle that runs through the Book of Mormon, and on a God who "looketh on the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7) and judges "according to the desire of their hearts" (D&C 137:9). Four kings, one question: seek the Lord, and stand still.

Last week (2 Kings 16–25) carried the story all the way to its end: Jerusalem burned, the temple leveled, Judah in Babylon. This week does not move forward. It doubles back and retells much of that same monarchy from a completely different vantage — a Second-Temple community that has returned from exile and rebuilt, asking what in their history still holds. **Kings asks why did we lose the temple? Chronicles asks *what still stands, now that we have it back?*** That is why Asa and Jehoshaphat, whom Kings treats briefly, are given whole chapters here, and why Uzziah's leprosy finally gets a reason attached to it.

The outline above shows the whole book so nothing arrives from nowhere. Two threads especially are worth carrying into the reading. First, the chapter just before our last one — Hezekiah's reopening of the temple (29:3) — is precisely what makes the Passover of chapter 30 possible; our text begins the moment the sanctuary is back in order. And second, the book's ending: where Kings closes in ashes, Chronicles closes with Cyrus's decree freeing the exiles to rebuild (36:22–23). A door left open — which is the whole reason this book sounds the way it does.

Asa: From "We Rest on Thee" to "He Sought Not to the Lord" (2 Chronicles 14–16) — assigned

Asa's reign is the reading's clearest arc from trust to self-reliance. He begins well: he "did that which was good and right in the eyes of the Lord" (14:2), tears out the foreign altars, high places, images, and groves, and commands Judah "to seek the Lord God of their fathers, and to do the law" (14:4). Because the land has rest, he builds and fortifies — a Chronicler's signature: seeking God produces rest ("the Lord had given him rest," 14:6; "the Lord gave them rest round about," 15:15; cf. 20:30).

The test arrives in the form of Zerah the Ethiopian and an army of overwhelming size (14:9). Asa's response is a model prayer of dependence: "Lord, it is nothing with thee to help, whether with many, or with them that have no power: help us, O Lord our God; for we rest on thee, and in thy name we go against this multitude" (14:11). The Lord routs the invaders. Coming home, Asa is met by the prophet Azariah, whose words become the reading's governing principle: "The Lord is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you; but if ye forsake him, he will forsake you" (15:2). Asa "took courage," renews the altar and the covenant, gathers even defectors from the north who see "that the Lord his God was with him" (15:9), and the people "sware unto the Lord with a loud voice" (15:14). He even removes his own grandmother from her position as queen mother for making an idol (15:16). For a generation, there is peace.

Then the arc bends. When Baasha of Israel fortifies Ramah to blockade Judah, Asa does not pray — he empties the temple and palace treasuries to hire Ben-hadad of Syria to break the siege (16:2–3). It works, militarily. But the seer Hanani delivers the verdict that reframes the whole story: "Because thou hast relied on the king of Syria, and not relied on the Lord thy God, therefore is the host of the king of Syria escaped out of thine hand" (16:7). Then the line that names the week's theme in the negative: "For the eyes of the Lord run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him. Herein thou hast done foolishly" (16:9). The same king who once cast himself entirely on God now leans on a pagan army — and when Hanani says so, Asa is enraged and imprisons him (16:10). The final note is tragic in its symmetry: diseased in his feet, "yet in his disease he sought not to the Lord, but to the physicians" (16:12). The verb of his greatness — seek — is exactly what he stops doing.

Jehoshaphat: Teaching, Alliance, and the Battle That Is God's (2 Chronicles 17–20) — assigned

Jehoshaphat is the reading's most developed portrait, and the Chronicler frames him as a king who institutionalizes the seeking of God. He "sought to the Lord God of his father" (17:4), and the Lord "stablished the kingdom in his hand" (17:5). His most distinctive act is educational: in the third year of his reign he sends princes, Levites, and priests throughout the cities of Judah, and "they taught in Judah, and had the book of the law of the Lord with them" (17:9). A kingdom is strengthened not only by walls but by a people taught the word.

Jehoshaphat's great flaw is entanglement. He "joined affinity with Ahab" (18:1) and nearly dies fighting Ahab's war at Ramoth-gilead, saved only when "Jehoshaphat cried out, and the Lord helped him" (18:31). Returning home, he is met by another prophet, Jehu son of Hanani, with a rebuke that echoes his father's alliance sin: "Shouldest thou help the ungodly, and love them that hate the Lord? therefore is wrath upon thee from before the Lord" (19:2). Yet Jehoshaphat responds not with Asa's rage but with reform. He appoints judges across the land with a charge that grounds all justice in God: "Take heed what ye do: for ye judge not for man, but for the Lord... let the fear of the Lord be upon you" (19:6–7).

Then comes chapter 20, the theological heart of the week. A vast coalition of Moab, Ammon, and Mount Seir advances on Judah. Jehoshaphat "feared, and set himself to seek the Lord, and proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah" (20:3). His public prayer models covenant dependence — recalling God's promises, laying the crisis before Him, and ending in the honest admission that becomes the posture of faith: "we have no might against this great company that cometh against us; neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee" (20:12). The Spirit falls on Jahaziel, a Levite of the sons of Asaph, who delivers the oracle that names the week: "Be not afraid nor dismayed by reason of this great multitude; for the battle is not yours, but God's" (20:15); "Ye shall not need to fight in this battle: set yourselves, stand ye still, and see the salvation of the Lord with you" (20:17). Judah worships. And then, in one of scripture's most striking military scenes, Jehoshaphat sends the temple singers out ahead of the soldiers, praising "the Lord... for his mercy endureth for ever" — and "when they began to sing and to praise, the Lord set ambushments" against the enemy, who annihilate one another (20:21–23). Judah gathers spoil for three days and assembles in a valley they name Berachah, "blessing," to bless the Lord (20:26). The realm is quiet, "for his God gave him rest round about" (20:30). The battle was won by worship, not weapons.

Uzziah: "Marvellously Helped, Till He Was Strong" (2 Chronicles 26) — assigned

Uzziah's reign compresses the pride cycle into a single life. He begins at sixteen and reigns fifty-two years, and "he sought God in the days of Zechariah... and as long as he sought the Lord, God made him to prosper" (26:5). The prosperity is spectacular: victories over the Philistines and Arabians, fortified towers, cisterns, a great army, and even engineers who devise "engines... to shoot arrows and great stones" from the towers (26:15). The Chronicler's summary is precise about the source and the danger: "his name spread far abroad; for he was marvellously helped, till he was strong."

Then the hinge: "But when he was strong, his heart was lifted up to his destruction: for he transgressed against the Lord his God, and went into the temple of the Lord to burn incense upon the altar of incense" (26:16). Eighty priests confront him — "It appertaineth not unto thee, Uzziah, to burn incense unto the Lord, but to the priests... go out of the sanctuary; for thou hast trespassed" (26:18). Uzziah rages, censer in hand — and leprosy rises in his forehead. He is thrust out, "a leper unto the day of his death, and dwelt in a several house" (26:21), cut off from the very temple he had presumed to enter. The help that made him strong was God's; the strength he claimed as his own destroyed him. It is the exact inversion of "stand ye still": a king who would not stay in his place.

Hezekiah's Passover: Mercy for the Prepared Heart (2 Chronicles 30) — assigned

The reading ends where Chronicles' hope lives — in the restored worship of the temple. Hezekiah reopens and cleanses the house of God and summons all Israel to keep the Passover. Because the priests are not yet sanctified and the people not yet gathered in the first month, he keeps it in the second — an accommodation for the sake of the whole (30:2–3). His invitation reaches into the shattered northern kingdom: couriers carry letters to Ephraim and Manasseh urging, "turn again unto the Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, and he will return to the remnant of you" (30:6). Some laugh the messengers to scorn; but others "humbled themselves, and came to Jerusalem" (30:11), and in Judah "the hand of God was to give them one heart" (30:12).

The Passover reveals the Chronicler's — and Hezekiah's — theology of mercy. A great many worshippers, especially from the north, "had not cleansed themselves, yet did they eat the passover otherwise than it was written" (30:18). Rather than turn them away, Hezekiah prays: "The good Lord pardon every one that prepareth his heart to seek God, the Lord God of his fathers, though he be not cleansed according to the purification of the sanctuary" (30:18–19). The response is one of the tenderest lines in Chronicles: "And the Lord hearkened to Hezekiah, and healed the people" (30:20). The joy is so great that the assembly keeps another seven days, and "there was great joy in Jerusalem: for since the time of Solomon... there was not the like" (30:26). The book of Kings measures Hezekiah by his trust under siege; the Chronicler measures him by his mercy at the altar — and by a God who honors the prepared heart above the perfect procedure.

The Chronicler tells four reigns with one measuring line: seeking God brings strength and rest; leaning on anything else — foreign armies, personal power, ritual technicality, or one's own pride — brings collapse. Jehoshaphat wins by standing still and singing; Asa loses by hiring Syria; Uzziah falls the moment his God-given strength becomes self-exaltation; Hezekiah's mercy heals a people who prepared their hearts to seek God. The battles of these chapters are never really about numbers. They are about where the heart leans when the multitude appears on the horizon.

2. Week 30: Historical & Cultural Context

The most important context for this week is not a place but a perspective. First and Second Chronicles retell much of the same history as Samuel–Kings, but with a different purpose. The Old Testament Seminary Teacher Manual observes that the books were "compiled sometime after 537 B.C." — that is, for the community that had returned from Babylon — and that their writers "dwell with most satisfaction upon the ecclesiastical and religious aspects of the history, and the progress of temple worship in Jerusalem." That emphasis is unmistakable in the text itself: Chronicles foregrounds the temple, the Levites, the priesthood, music, and covenant worship, and reads the monarchy as a long lesson in one question — did this king seek the Lord, or not? Fittingly, the book closes with Cyrus's decree that the exiles return and rebuild the temple (2 Chronicles 36:22–23) — the very hope its temple-centered retelling serves. (The same manual notes we "do not know exactly who wrote or compiled" Chronicles; the traditional attribution to Ezra is just that — traditional.)

This is why the same kings look different here than in Kings. Chronicles reads history through a single principle — one the prophets state within the reading itself: "The Lord is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you; but if ye forsake him, he will forsake you" (15:2; cf. 16:9). Seeking God brings help, strength, and rest; forsaking Him brings defeat and disease, often swiftly and visibly. Biblical scholars call this the Chronicler's theology of "immediate retribution" (the term is Raymond B. Dillard's) — reward and punishment that "follow immediately on the heels of the precipitating events." It is the lens behind the Chronicler's editorial choices — and it has its limits, to be read alongside Job and the New Testament, where the righteous also suffer.

The Chronicler names his own sources. Unlike a modern narrator, he repeatedly cites written records: "the book of Jehu the son of Hanani" (20:34), and — strikingly — "the rest of the acts of Uzziah... did Isaiah the prophet, the son of Amoz, write" (26:22). Chronicles presents itself as a documented history drawn from prophetic records, not free composition.

Reading these chapters alongside Kings shows what the Chronicler chose to include that Kings omits — and the additions are the heart of the week:

  • Asa's later failure and disease (16) — Kings praises Asa broadly; Chronicles adds Hanani's rebuke, the imprisonment of the prophet, and Asa's fatal turn to physicians.
  • Jehoshaphat's teaching program and the Moab–Ammon deliverance (17; 20) — the Levites carrying the law through Judah, and the great "the battle is not yours, but God's" victory, are largely unique to Chronicles.
  • Uzziah's pride and leprosy (26) — Kings notes only that Uzziah (Azariah) was a leper; Chronicles supplies the reason: his unlawful incense-offering.
  • The mechanics of Hezekiah's Passover (30) — the second-month timing, the invitation to the north, and the prayer for the unclean are Chronicles' own.

For a teaching setting, the payoff is clear: the Chronicler is not repeating Kings but interpreting it — showing the inner spiritual logic (seek/forsake, humble/proud) beneath the political events.

These reigns unfold across the ninth and eighth centuries BC — with Asa's reign beginning in the late tenth century and Hezekiah's extending into the early seventh (by the standard Albright and Thiele chronologies) — in the southern kingdom of Judah, after the division of Solomon's kingdom and alongside the northern kingdom of Israel (whose Baal-tainted dynasty of Ahab appears in chapter 18). The geopolitical pressures visible in the reading are the ones that defined the period:

  • Egypt/Cush to the south (Zerah's invasion, 14).
  • Aram/Syria to the north (Asa's alliance with Ben-hadad, 16).
  • The northern kingdom of Israel, whose alliance with Judah under Ahab nearly kills Jehoshaphat (18), and whose collapse to Assyria (the fall of Samaria, 722/721 BC) is the backdrop to Hezekiah's invitation to "the remnant" (30).
  • Trans-Jordan neighbors — Moab, Ammon, and the peoples of Mount Seir/Edom — whose coalition attacks Jehoshaphat (20).

The Chronicler assumes the reader knows this larger political map; his interest is in how each king responded spiritually to the pressures it created.

Asa faces "Zerah the Ethiopian" (Hebrew Kushi, a Cushite) with an enormous force (14:9). The text does not identify Zerah further, and its round number ("a thousand thousand," a million) functions in the Hebrew idiom to convey an overwhelming, humanly hopeless multitude rather than a census figure. What the text does insist on is theological: the size of the army is exactly the point of Asa's prayer — "it is nothing with thee to help, whether with many, or with them that have no power" (14:11).

A striking feature of this week is how densely the prophetic voice is woven through it. Chronicles presents a steady stream of messengers sent to correct kings at their turning points:

  • Azariah son of Oded meets Asa after victory with the covenant principle (15:1–7).
  • Hanani the seer rebukes Asa for the Syrian alliance — and is imprisoned for it (16:7–10).
  • Jehu son of Hanani rebukes Jehoshaphat for the Ahab alliance (19:2–3).
  • Jahaziel, a Levite of the sons of Asaph, delivers the battle oracle (20:14–17).
  • Eighty priests led by Azariah confront Uzziah in the sanctuary (26:17–18).

The pattern is a teaching point in itself: God consistently sends correction at the moment of a king's success or danger, and the king's response to the prophet — Asa's rage versus Jehoshaphat's reform — often matters more than the original misstep.

Uzziah's affliction (26:19–21) left a long shadow in Israel's memory. Scripture itself links his reign to a famous earthquake: Amos dates his prophecy "two years before the earthquake" in Uzziah's era (Amos 1:1), and Zechariah, centuries later, still measures terror against it — "ye shall flee, like as ye fled from before the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" (Zechariah 14:5). These are scripture-internal anchors for how significant Uzziah's reign felt to later Israel.

Because he died a leper, Uzziah was buried apart from the other kings, "in the field of the burial which belonged to the kings; for they said, He is a leper" (26:23).

Two details of Hezekiah's Passover reward context. First, the second-month timing: Hezekiah keeps the feast in the second month because the priests were not yet sanctified and the people not yet gathered (30:2–3). This is not an arbitrary improvisation — the Law itself provided for a delayed, second-month Passover for those unable to keep it at the appointed time (Numbers 9:10–11). Hezekiah applies a provision meant for individuals to the whole nation, an act of pastoral flexibility grounded in the Torah, not against it.

Second, the invitation to the north: Hezekiah proclaims the feast "throughout all Israel, from Beer-sheba even to Dan" (30:5), and his couriers carry the letters into the territory of Ephraim and Manasseh (30:1, 10). This is poignant in its timing — the northern kingdom had fallen (or was falling) to Assyria, and Hezekiah reaches out to its scattered "remnant" (30:6) to gather them back to the temple. The Passover becomes an act of reunification, an attempt to reconstitute a covenant people around Jerusalem's altar after the north's collapse — which is exactly the kind of gathering-of-the-remnant the Chronicler's own post-exilic audience longed for.

3. Week 30: Key Passages Study

The Text

"And Asa cried unto the Lord his God, and said, Lord, it is nothing with thee to help, whether with many, or with them that have no power: help us, O Lord our God; for we rest on thee, and in thy name we go against this multitude. O Lord, thou art our God; let not man prevail against thee." (14:11)

The Insight

Faced with Zerah's overwhelming army, Asa's prayer is a theology of dependence in three moves: God's power is indifferent to numbers ("whether with many, or with them that have no power"); the plea is bare need ("help us"); and the weight is placed entirely on God ("we rest on thee" — the verb sha'an, to lean; see Word Studies). The final line reframes the whole battle: it is not Judah versus Zerah but "man" versus God — "let not man prevail against thee." Asa is not asking God to join his fight; he is joining God's.

4. Week 30: Word Studies

The vocabulary of these chapters is the Chronicler's theology in miniature. Two verbs govern the reading — darash, "to seek," and sha'an, "to lean/rely." A third pair frames its warning — chazaq, "to be strong," and gavah, "to be lifted up." A fourth names its rescue and its reward — yeshuah, "salvation," and simchah, "joy." Trace these six words and you have traced the spiritual logic of Asa, Jehoshaphat, Uzziah, and Hezekiah: seek the Lord and lean on Him; be made strong without being lifted up; and see His salvation turn into joy.

Root and Form

The root is d-r-sh (דרש): to resort to, seek with care, consult, inquire of. It is the Chronicler's signature verb — his single most characteristic measure of a king. To darash the Lord is not a passing wish but a deliberate turning of the whole self toward God to consult His will and do it.

Appears

2 Chronicles 14:4 — Asa "commanded Judah to seek (darash) the Lord God of their fathers." 15:2 — "if ye seek (darash) him, he will be found of you." 15:12 — the covenant "to seek the Lord God of their fathers with all their heart." 20:3 — Jehoshaphat "set himself to seek (darash) the Lord." 26:5 — Uzziah "sought (darash) God in the days of Zechariah... and as long as he sought the Lord, God made him to prosper." The verb marks every high point of the reading — and its absence marks every fall.

Meaning

Darash is covenant seeking: consulting God through His word and prophets, and aligning one's life with the answer. The Chronicler treats it as the hinge of national destiny. Azariah states the principle as a two-edged promise: "The Lord is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you; but if ye forsake him, he will forsake you" (15:2). Uzziah's tragedy is defined by it — he prospered "as long as he sought," and fell when he stopped.

Latter-day Saint Connection

The promise attached to darash runs straight through Restoration scripture: "Seek, and ye shall find" (Matthew 7:7); "Seek me diligently and ye shall find me" (D&C 88:63); "he that diligently seeketh shall find" (1 Nephi 10:19, the pattern of revelation). Moroni's promise (Moroni 10:4–5) is darash made personal — to ask, seek, and receive an answer through the Spirit. Chronicles insists that seeking is not a one-time event but a sustained posture: "as long as he sought the Lord."

5. Week 30: Jewish Perspective

The rabbis read the kings of Judah closely, and four of this week's figures — Hezekiah, Uzziah, Asa, and Jehoshaphat — each drew rabbinic reflection. The richest tradition concerns the very thing that makes Hezekiah's Passover unusual: his manipulation of the calendar so that a scattered, unprepared nation could keep the feast at all.

The Chronicler notes almost in passing that Hezekiah kept the Passover in the second month rather than the first, "for they could not keep it at that time, because the priests had not sanctified themselves sufficiently, neither had the people gathered themselves together to Jerusalem" (2 Chronicles 30:2–3). The rabbis noticed — and debated whether he was right to do it.

The Talmud preserves a baraita listing six actions of Hezekiah, three of which "the Sages of his generation conceded to him" and three of which "the Sages did not concede":

"The Sages taught: King Hezekiah performed six actions. With regard to three of them, the Sages of his generation conceded to him; and with regard to three of them, the Sages did not concede to him... [Among the three they did not concede:] He intercalated the year, delaying the advent of the month of Nisan during Nisan... He declared the first of Nisan to be the thirtieth of Adar and only then intercalated the year (see II Chronicles 30:2)."

— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 56a

The issue is technical but profound. Hezekiah adjusted the calendar — retroactively declaring the new month — so that the Passover could be pushed into the "second month," giving the priests time to sanctify themselves and the people time to gather. The Torah did provide a second-month Passover (Numbers 9:10–11), but for individuals who were unclean or far away, not as a national reset declared by the king. Some Sages held that Hezekiah overstepped; the calendar is not a king's to bend, even for a good cause.

And yet the biblical text renders its own verdict: when a multitude ate the Passover ritually unprepared, "Hezekiah prayed for them... The good Lord pardon every one that prepareth his heart to seek God... And the Lord hearkened to Hezekiah, and healed the people" (30:18–20). The rabbis may have hesitated over the mechanism; the Chronicler insists that heaven ratified the mercy. It is a classic case of the tension between the exact letter of the law and the pastoral need to bring a whole people to worship — and here God comes down on the side of the prepared heart.

LDS Connection

The principle Hezekiah embodies — that sacred requirements exist for the people's coming to God, not as ends that can shut them out — is the same one Jesus names: "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath" (Mark 2:27). The Restoration applies it constantly (accommodations for the sick, the distant, and the new in keeping ordinances and gathering). Hezekiah's willingness to adjust the timing so no one who prepared his heart would be excluded, and God's healing response, anticipates a gospel in which "the Lord looketh on the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7) and judges "according to the desire of their hearts" (D&C 137:9). (Note: also striking — the same baraita records that the Sages approved Hezekiah's grinding of the copper serpent, Nechushtan; see Week 29 for that thread.)

The rabbis read Uzziah's leprosy not as random misfortune but as a punishment measured to his sin. A baraita in Arakhin 16a, cataloging the offenses for which tzara'at (leprosy) comes, names arrogance and points straight at Uzziah:

"And for arrogance, as it is written with regard to King Uzziah: 'But when he was strong his heart became arrogant so that he did corruptly, and he trespassed against the Lord his God; for he went into the temple of the Lord to burn incense upon the altar of incense'... And it states: 'Then Uzziah was angry; and he had a censer in his hand to burn incense; and while he was angry with the priests, the leprosy broke forth in his forehead.'"

— Babylonian Talmud, Arakhin 16a

A second strand of tradition reaches the same leprosy by a different route: Sanhedrin 48b reads Uzziah's leprosy as a fulfillment of the curse David pronounced on the house of Joab — "let there not fail from the house of Joab... a leper" (2 Samuel 3:29) — worked out among Solomon's royal descendants. And Leviticus Rabbah 17:3, as a general principle, has leprosy fall on "one who steals what is not his" — with Uzziah as the man who "sought to usurp the high priesthood." For the rabbis, the disease is the visible form of an invisible trespass: he reached for an office that was not his, and the reaching surfaced on his forehead.

Scripture itself remembers a great earthquake in Uzziah's reign — Amos dates his prophecy "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1), and Zechariah still measures terror against it generations later (Zechariah 14:5). The Aramaic Targum Jonathan preserves that same dating on both verses ("the earthquake that was in the days of Uzziah"). The later tradition that the quake struck at the very moment of his trespass is an external development and is not found in the classical rabbinic sources of this vault — so this guide notes only what those sources actually say: the earthquake belonged to his reign.

LDS Connection

Uzziah is a case study in D&C 121:37 — authority or office grasped "to gratify our pride" forfeits the very thing it reaches for: "Amen to the priesthood or the authority of that man." The correction the priests give Uzziah, to stay in the place God assigned and honor the boundary of the office, is the same principle by which priesthood is held "only... by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness" — never by self-promotion (D&C 121:41).

Chronicles closes Asa's reign on a somber medical note: "Asa in the thirty and ninth year of his reign was diseased in his feet, until his disease was exceeding great: yet in his disease he sought not to the Lord, but to the physicians" (2 Chronicles 16:12). The rabbis identified the ailment and, characteristically, read a moral cause into it. Sotah 10a names both:

"Asa was stricken in his feet... this indicates that gout [padagra] grabbed hold of him."

"Rava taught: For what reason was Asa punished in his feet? Because he made Torah scholars perform forced labor [angarya], as it is stated: 'Then King Asa made a proclamation unto all Judah; none was exempted.'"

— Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 10a

Two distinct critiques of Asa sit side by side, and it is worth keeping them separate. The Chronicler's own critique is that Asa "sought not to the Lord, but to the physicians" (16:12) — a failure of reliance, the exact negative image of the "we rest on thee" that had won his earlier battle (14:11). The rabbinic critique is narrower and specific: Rava faults him for conscripting Torah scholars into his royal building corvée, and reads the gout as a measure-for-measure answer. Together they frame a king who began by leaning wholly on God and ended leaning on everything but Him.

LDS Connection

Asa's decline warns that early faith does not immunize a person against late self-reliance. "Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding" (Proverbs 3:5) is precisely the posture the older Asa abandoned. The Restoration renews the charge to seek God first and in all things — "Counsel with the Lord in all thy doings, and he will direct thee for good" (Alma 37:37) — a habit Asa kept in war but lost on his sickbed.

Jehoshaphat's deliverance turns on worship: he sends singers ahead of the army, and "when they began to sing and to praise, the Lord set ambushments" against the enemy (2 Chronicles 20:21–22). The rabbis noticed something precise about the singers' refrain. In Sanhedrin 39b, Rabbi Yonatan asks why this victory-song is shortened (the parallel discussion in Megillah 10b preserves the same teaching in the name of Rabbi Yoḥanan):

"'He appointed them that should sing unto the Lord, and praise in the beauty of holiness, as they went out before the army, and say: Give thanks to the Lord, for His mercy endures forever' (II Chronicles 20:21). And Rabbi Yonatan says: For what reason is it not stated in this praise: 'Give thanks to the Lord for He is good, for His mercy endures forever'... This is because the Holy One, Blessed be He, is not gladdened by the downfall of the wicked."

— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 39b

The observation is striking: at the very moment of a God-given victory, the liturgy deliberately drops the words "for He is good," because even a righteous triumph involves the death of human beings God would rather have seen repent. Judah may rejoice in deliverance; heaven does not celebrate the destruction of the enemy.

LDS Connection

This is the God of Ezekiel 33:11 — "As I live, saith the Lord God, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live." It is the God who, in Enoch's vision, weeps over His own creations facing judgment (Moses 7:28–37), and who reminds the Restoration that "the worth of souls is great in the sight of God" (D&C 18:10) — even the souls on the losing side of Jehoshaphat's battle. Praise for deliverance and grief over the lost are not contradictions in the divine heart.

6. Week 30: Teaching Applications
  • "On whom are we leaning?" object lesson. Have a family member lean their full weight against a wall, then against a chair with a broken leg. Connect to sha'an ("rely") — Asa leaned on God (14:11) and later on Syria (16:7). Ask each person to name one thing they lean on when they're afraid. Where is that leaning wise, and where does it need to shift to God?
  • Family "Berachah" moment. Jehoshaphat's people gathered in the Valley of Blessing to thank God after the deliverance (20:26). At dinner, go around and name a recent "battle" God fought for your family. Name a real valley of blessing in your week.
  • Younger children: act out Jehoshaphat's army — the singers march in front (20:21). Let the kids be the choir. Talk about how Judah won by trusting and praising, not by fighting.
  • Compare Kings and Chronicles. Show how 2 Chronicles adds what Kings leaves out — Asa's later failure and disease, Jehoshaphat's prayer and deliverance, Uzziah's leprosy-by-pride, Hezekiah's Passover mercy. Ask: why might a post-exilic community rebuilding the temple emphasize these details? (The Chronicler's message: seek God and be restored.)
  • Trace one word through the class. Put darash ("seek") on the board and have the class find it at every high point (14:4; 15:2; 20:3; 26:5) — and its absence at every fall (16:12). Let the pattern make the point.
  • "Stand ye still" discussion. 2 Chronicles 20:17 echoes Exodus 14:13 (the Red Sea). What did "standing still" require Judah to actually do? Where do disciples today confuse faith with passivity — or confuse action with faithlessness?
  • "God's eyes are looking for me." Teach 2 Chronicles 16:9 simply: God is looking all over the world for people whose hearts are all His. Have children make paper "eyes" and talk about what it means to have a whole heart for Heavenly Father.
  • The singing army. Tell the story of Jehoshaphat's choir marching first (20:21). Sing a Primary song "as the army" and talk about how singing can help us feel brave when we're scared.
  • Coloring/telling: Asa's prayer — "help us, O Lord" (14:11). A little army trusting a big God.
  • Pride in strength — the Uzziah trap. Uzziah was great at everything (26:6–15) until his success made him proud (26:16). Discuss where teens face this: talent, popularity, achievement, a callout online. Pair with Alma 26:11–12 ("I will boast of my God"). How do you succeed and stay humble?
  • "What do you lean on?" Have youth privately list what they reach for first under stress (friends' approval, phone, control, God). No sharing required — just an honest audit against sha'an. What would it look like to shift the top of the list?
  • Active stillness challenge. Identify one situation this week where they can do their part and then genuinely leave the outcome to God ("stand ye still") instead of over-controlling it.
  • The Chronicler's retribution theology — and its limits. Chronicles tightens the link between faithfulness and blessing (seek → prosper; forsake → fall). Discuss its strengths and where it must be held alongside Job and the New Testament (the righteous who suffer). How do we teach cause-and-consequence without prosperity-gospel distortion?
  • Prophetic confrontation of kings. Azariah (15), Hanani (16), Jehu son of Hanani (19), Jahaziel (20), the priests (26). Chart how each king responds to the prophetic word (Asa jails Hanani; Jehoshaphat reforms). What determines whether correction produces repentance or rage?
  • Mercy vs. ritual boundary. Uzziah is barred from the sanctuary for crossing a boundary (26); the unclean at Hezekiah's Passover are welcomed across one (30). Discuss when holiness guards a boundary and when mercy opens it — and how to know the difference.
  • Teaching from weakness. Jehoshaphat's power was in admitting "neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee" (20:12). Missionaries often feel inadequate; this is the posture that invites God's help, not a disqualification. Practice praying/teaching from dependence rather than performance.
  • The invitation to the remnant. Hezekiah's couriers went to the least likely — the scattered, the mocking, the disqualified (30:6–11); some laughed, "nevertheless divers... humbled themselves, and came" (30:11). Model inviting everyone, expecting rejection from some and humility from others, and honoring the prepared heart over the perfect background.
  • Companionship discipleship parallels Jehoshaphat sending teachers two-and-more together with "the book of the law... with them" (17:9): the word carried into the field is what strengthens a people.
  • Heart audit (16:9). Sit with the image of God's eyes "running to and fro" seeking a heart "perfect [whole] toward him." Name honestly where your heart is currently divided, and one concrete step toward making it whole.
  • Your Red Sea (20:12–17). Identify a "great company" you have no might against. Write Jehoshaphat's prayer in your own words, ending with "my eyes are upon thee." Then define what your "stand ye still" is — your part vs. God's part.
  • The prepared-heart standard (30:18–20). Where do you disqualify yourself from worship or from returning because you're "not cleansed" enough? Let Hezekiah's prayer reframe it: God honors the prepared heart. Prepare yours, and come.
7. Week 30: Study Questions
  1. Asa uses the same verb — sha'an, "to lean" — first of God ("we rest on thee," 14:11) and later of Syria ("thou hast relied on the king of Syria," 16:7). What does it reveal that a person can shift the object of their trust without ever announcing they've stopped trusting God?
  1. Hanani tells Asa his winning alliance was actually a loss (16:7–9). Have you ever "solved" a problem in a way that worked but cost you a greater blessing God had prepared? How would you recognize that in the moment rather than in hindsight?
  1. Asa's decline shows up in three escalating steps: hiring Syria, jailing the prophet who rebuked him, and seeking "not to the Lord, but to the physicians" (16:12). What do these three have in common, and which of them is easiest to slip into unnoticed?
  1. Jehoshaphat's prayer ends, "neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee" (20:12). Why might admitting you don't know what to do be a stronger act of faith than having a plan?
  1. "Stand ye still" (20:17) did not mean Judah did nothing — they still marched out and sang. Where in your life is God asking for active stillness: doing exactly what He's asked while refusing to seize an outcome only He can give?
  1. The victory came while the choir was praising, before any fighting (20:21–22). What would change in how you face a trial if you began with worship instead of ending with it?
  1. Jehoshaphat institutionalized seeking God — sending Levites to teach the law throughout Judah (17:9). How does regularly teaching and being taught God's word change a community's default response to crisis?
  1. Uzziah was "marvellously helped, till he was strong" — and "when he was strong, his heart was lifted up" (26:15–16). Why is prosperity more spiritually dangerous than adversity? Which one are you in right now, and what does that danger look like for you?
  1. Uzziah's sin was reaching for something that "appertaineth not unto thee" (26:18) — a role that wasn't his. Where might humility look like staying in your place rather than reaching for more?
  1. Both Asa and Uzziah began by genuinely seeking God and still fell. What does that say about the need to keep seeking "as long as" we live (26:5), rather than treating faithfulness as a finished achievement?
  1. Hezekiah prayed that God would pardon those who "prepareth his heart to seek God... though he be not cleansed" (30:18–19) — and God "healed the people." When should mercy bend a rule, and when shouldn't it? How do you tell the difference?
  1. The invitation to the Passover went to the northern remnant — people with every reason to feel disqualified (30:6–11). Who in your life assumes they're too far gone to come back? What would Hezekiah's letter say to them?
  1. God honored the prepared heart over the perfect procedure. Is your worship built more on getting things exactly right, or on preparing your heart? What would shift if you trusted that a prepared heart is enough to begin?
  1. Four kings, one measuring line: seeking God brings strength and rest; leaning on anything else brings collapse. Trace the pattern in your own life over the past year — where were you seeking, and where were you leaning elsewhere?
  1. "The eyes of the Lord run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him" (16:9). If God is actively searching for undivided hearts to strengthen, what in your heart is currently divided — and what would it take to make it whole?
