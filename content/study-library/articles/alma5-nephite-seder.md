What if Alma 5 is not simply a sermon, but a structured liturgical sequence?

The Passover Seder is an ordered pattern of fifteen steps that moves its participants from bondage to deliverance, from impurity to sanctification, from estrangement to acceptance before God. The Hebrew word <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h5468/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="order" data-strongs="H5468" data-translit="seder" data-original="סֵדֶר" data-lang="hebrew">*seder*</a> means "order" — and Alma uses this very concept multiple times in his address, referring to what he calls the <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h6944/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="holy" data-strongs="H6944" data-translit="qodesh" data-original="קֹדֶשׁ" data-lang="hebrew">*Seder Kadosh*</a> — the "Holy Order." Specific references to this order appear in Alma 5:44, 49, and 54, and the concept is developed further in Alma 13, where the "holy order of God" becomes a central theological framework.

Upon careful examination, Alma's sermon follows the traditional structured pattern of the Seder's fifteen steps as outlined in the Jewish Haggadah. The Nephites, as a community of Israelites who faithfully observed the Law of Moses, would have known these patterns intimately. Alma, as high priest, appears to lead his people through each step in sequence — a Nephite Seder of repentance, sanctification, and covenant renewal.

This observance is traditionally associated with the recitation of songs, which Alma may allude to when he refers to the "song of redeeming love" (Alma 5:26). The traditional music patterns of the Seder are reminiscent of the Songs of Moses (Exodus 15:1; Deuteronomy 31) and the fifteen *Shir HaMa'alot* — the Songs of Ascent found in Psalms 120–134.

This article presents an interpretive reading, not a claim of direct textual dependence. But the structural parallels are striking, and they invite us to read Alma 5 with fresh eyes — as a prophet leading his people through an ancient pattern of covenant renewal that points, at every step, toward the Savior.

For a detailed exploration of each Seder step with its traditional Jewish context, see the [Passover Seder guide](/culture/jewish/moedim/passover/) in the Culture section.

## The Songs of Ascent and the Priestly Blessing

The number fifteen is not arbitrary. The fifteen steps of the Passover Seder correspond to the fifteen Songs of Ascent (Psalms 120–134) — a collection of psalms that were sung on the fifteen steps leading from the Court of Women to the Court of the Israelites in the Temple at Jerusalem.

In a landmark 1955 study published in the *Journal of Biblical Literature*, Leon J. Liebreich demonstrated that twelve of these fifteen psalms contain direct verbal allusions to the four key words of the Priestly Blessing in Numbers 6:24–26: <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1288/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="bless" data-strongs="H1288" data-translit="barak" data-original="בָּרַךְ" data-lang="hebrew">*barak*</a> (bless), <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h8104/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="keep, guard" data-strongs="H8104" data-translit="shamar" data-original="שָׁמַר" data-lang="hebrew">*shamar*</a> (guard), <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h2603/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="be gracious" data-strongs="H2603" data-translit="chanan" data-original="חָנַן" data-lang="hebrew">*chanan*</a> (be gracious), and <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h7965/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="peace" data-strongs="H7965" data-translit="shalom" data-original="שָׁלוֹם" data-lang="hebrew">*shalom*</a> (peace).

Critically, the *Tosefta Sotah* VII.7 records that the priests pronounced the Aaronic Blessing on the steps of the hall that led to the interior of the Temple. Liebreich concluded that the phrase *shir hama'alot* — "a Song of Ascents" — literally means "a song rendered in conjunction with the Priestly Blessing which was pronounced on the *ma'alot*" (the steps). Each Song of Ascent was in the nature of a congregational response following the Priestly Blessing.

This means the fifteen-step pattern was not merely a Seder convention or a literary device. It was embedded in the architecture and liturgy of the Temple itself — a physical, musical, and priestly ascent from the outer courts to the presence of God. When Alma speaks of the "holy order" and leads his people through a fifteen-step pattern of covenant renewal, he is drawing on a tradition that connects Passover, Temple worship, and the Priestly Blessing into a single ascending movement toward acceptance before the Lord.

Alfred Edersheim, in *The Temple: Its Ministry and Services*, describes how the Hallel (Psalms 113–118) was sung responsively during the Paschal offering — the Levites leading in song, the people repeating the first line of each psalm and responding to each subsequent line with "Hallelujah." The priests drew a threefold blast from their silver trumpets when the Passover lamb was slain. Music, sacrifice, and priestly blessing were a unified act.

## The Passover as Cosmic Pattern

Jewish tradition does not view Passover as a single historical event confined to the Exodus from Egypt. The rabbinic sources teach that Passover night — *leil shimurim*, the "night of vigil" — is a recurring cosmic moment of divine intervention.

The Midrash (Exodus Rabbah 18) teaches that on Passover night, God rescued Hezekiah from Sennacherib, delivered Daniel from the lion's den, saved Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah from the furnace, and that on this same night, the Messiah and Elijah will be exalted. Passover is not simply remembered — it recurs.

*Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer* (Ch. 21) pushes the pattern even further back, teaching that Adam himself instructed Cain and Abel to bring offerings on the eve of Passover. The first recorded offerings in human history are framed as paschal offerings, establishing Passover as a cosmic, pre-Sinaitic institution. For Latter-day Saints, this resonates powerfully with the teaching that Adam offered sacrifices "in similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten of the Father" (Moses 5:7) — the paschal connection making that similitude even more explicit.

The Zohar (Bo) teaches that the retelling of the Exodus at the Seder is itself a mystical act: the one who relates the story "fervently and joyously, telling the tale with a high heart, shall be found worthy to rejoice in the Shekinah in the world to come, for rejoicing brings forth rejoicing; and the joy of Israel causes the Holy One Himself to be glad." This transforms the Seder from a historical exercise into an encounter with the Divine Presence — and it illuminates why Alma's retelling of the deliverance of his fathers is not mere history but sacred liturgical act.

If the Passover pattern is cosmic — woven into the fabric of creation, observed by Adam, encoded in the Temple architecture, and destined to culminate in the Messiah — then it is not surprising that a Nephite high priest, faithful to the Law of Moses, would draw on this same pattern to call his people to repentance and covenant renewal.

## Part I: Preparation for the Coming of the Messiah

### Step 1: Kadesh — קֹדֶשׁ — "Sanctify"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 120 | **Theme:** Sanctification and readiness to enter into a covenant with God

The word <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h6942/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to be holy, sanctify" data-strongs="H6942" data-translit="qadash" data-original="קָדַשׁ" data-lang="hebrew">*kadesh*</a> means "holy," "sanctified," or "consecrated." Alma uses this word at the very beginning of his introduction: "I, Alma, having been *consecrated* by my father, Alma, to be a high priest over the church of God, he having power and authority from God to do these things" (Alma 5:3). The Seder begins with consecration — setting the evening apart as sacred — and so does Alma's sermon, establishing his priestly authority before proceeding.

> "Have ye sufficiently retained in remembrance the captivity of your fathers?" — Alma 5:6

<br>

### Step 2: Urchatz — וּרְחַץ — "Wash"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 121 | **Theme:** Spiritual cleanliness and purity

The ritual washing of <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h7364/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to wash" data-strongs="H7364" data-translit="rachats" data-original="רָחַץ" data-lang="hebrew">*urchatz*</a> symbolizes the purification necessary before approaching the sacred. Alma connects this directly to baptism at the end of verse 3: "yea, and he did baptize his brethren in the waters of Mormon." Before entering the covenant pattern, one must be washed — a principle that holds from the Levitical priesthood through the baptismal font.

> "Have you sufficiently retained in remembrance his mercy and long-suffering towards them?" — Alma 5:6

<br>

### Step 3: Karpas — כַּרְפַּס — "Greens Dipped in Salt Water"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 122 | **Theme:** Tears and suffering as precursors to redemption

At the Seder table, green herbs are dipped in salt water — the green symbolizing new life, the salt water symbolizing tears. Alma recalls the suffering and bondage of the fathers: "they were in the midst of darkness; nevertheless, their souls were illuminated by the light of the everlasting word" (Alma 5:7). New life emerges from tears.

The Midrash (Exodus Rabbah 17:3) teaches that even the hyssop — the lowliest of plants — accomplished the greatest redemption, for it was used to apply the Paschal blood to the doorposts. As Alma would later teach: "by small and simple things are great things brought to pass" (Alma 37:6).

> "Were they not in the bonds of iniquity? Yea, were they not in the hands of the devil?" — Alma 5:7 (translation note)

<br>

### Step 4: Yachatz — יַחַץ — "Break"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 123 | **Theme:** Brokenness and humility before God

The middle matzah is <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h2673/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to divide" data-strongs="H2673" data-translit="chatsah" data-original="חָצָה" data-lang="hebrew">broken in two</a> — the larger piece hidden (the *afikomen*), the smaller piece remaining. This act of breaking symbolizes the brokenness that precedes redemption. Alma asks his people to confront their own brokenness: what is the condition of their hearts? Have they experienced the mighty change?

> "And according to his faith there was a mighty change wrought in his heart." — Alma 5:12

<br>

### Step 5: Maggid — נָגַד — "Tell, Preach, Declare"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 124 | **Theme:** Recounting spiritual heritage and deliverance

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h5046/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to tell, declare" data-strongs="H5046" data-translit="nagad" data-original="נָגַד" data-lang="hebrew">*Maggid*</a> is the heart of the Seder — the retelling of the story of deliverance. It traditionally begins with questions (not limited to four — one could ask ten or twenty), and Alma asks nearly fifty. The questions are listed in full at the end of this article.

Alma describes the deliverance of the Nephites from bondage through the account of his father Alma and Abinadi, mirroring the Exodus narrative. He declares: "For I am called to speak after this manner, according to the holy order of God" (Alma 5:44) — the *Seder Kadosh* itself.

The traditional Maggid also includes the telling of the Four Sons (the Wise, the Wicked, the Simple, and the One Who Does Not Know How to Ask). It is likely that Alma drew on this model in a later Passover when he gave individual counsel to his three sons in Alma 36–42 — Helaman the faithful, Shiblon the steady, and Corianton the wayward.

> "I say unto you, can you imagine to yourselves that ye hear the voice of the Lord, saying unto you, in that day: Come unto me ye blessed?" — Alma 5:16

## Part II: The Life and Ministry of Jesus Christ

### Step 6: Rachtzah — רָחַץ — "Wash Again"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 125 | **Theme:** Renewed purification and commitment

The second washing in the Seder signals a deeper level of purification — not the initial washing of preparation, but the washing that comes after hearing the story of deliverance. Having heard the Maggid, participants wash again before partaking. Alma calls his people to this deeper purification: are they truly clean? Can they look up to God with a pure heart?

> "Can ye look up to God at that day with a pure heart and clean hands?" — Alma 5:19

<br>

### Step 7: Motzi — מוֹצִיא — "Bring Forth"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 126 | **Theme:** God's provision and sustenance

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h3318/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to go or come out" data-strongs="H3318" data-translit="yatsa" data-original="יָצָא" data-lang="hebrew">*Motzi*</a> is the blessing over bread — acknowledging God as the one who "brings forth bread from the earth." This step recognizes divine provision. Alma asks whether his people are prepared to receive what God brings forth — the fruits of the Spirit, the blessings of covenant faithfulness.

> "I say unto you, if ye have experienced a change of heart, and if ye have felt to sing the song of redeeming love, I would ask, can ye feel so now?" — Alma 5:26

<br>

### Step 8: Matzah — מַצָּה — "Unleavened Bread"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 127 | **Theme:** Sincerity, humility, and haste

While Motzi is the general blessing over bread, <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h4682/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="unleavened bread" data-strongs="H4682" data-translit="matstsah" data-original="מַצָּה" data-lang="hebrew">*matzah*</a> focuses specifically on unleavened bread — bread without the puffing of leaven, symbolizing humility and sincerity before God. The Midrash (Exodus Rabbah 15) maps each element of the Paschal meal to a patriarch: the matzah corresponds to Sarah, whose quick preparation of bread for the visiting angels (Genesis 18:6) exemplifies responsive faith.

Alma warns against the leaven of pride: "Behold, are ye stripped of pride? I say unto you, if ye are not ye are not prepared to meet God" (Alma 5:28).

> "Behold, are ye stripped of pride?" — Alma 5:28

<br>

### Step 9: Maror — מָרוֹר — "Bitter Herbs"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 128 | **Theme:** The bitterness of suffering and the purpose of adversity

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h4844/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="bitter herb" data-strongs="H4844" data-translit="maror" data-original="מְרֹר" data-lang="hebrew">*Maror*</a> — the bitter herbs — recalls the bitterness of bondage in Egypt. Alma confronts his people with the bitter reality of sin: "ye are in the bonds of iniquity" (Alma 5:7). But the bitter herbs also serve a redemptive purpose. As the Savior taught: "I give unto men weakness that they may be humble; and my grace is sufficient for all men that humble themselves before me" (Ether 12:27).

Christ Himself drank the bitter cup: "which suffering caused myself, even God, the greatest of all, to tremble because of pain, and to bleed at every pore" (D&C 19:18). His willingness to endure the bitter gives Him the unique capacity to succor those who taste bitterness in mortality.

> "For I say unto you that whatsoever is good cometh from God, and whatsoever is evil cometh from the devil." — Alma 5:40

<br>

### Step 10: Korech — כֹּרֶךְ — "The Sandwich"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 129 | **Theme:** Binding together the bitter and the sweet

In the Seder, <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h3738/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to bind, wrap" data-strongs="H3738" data-translit="karak" data-original="כָּרַךְ" data-lang="hebrew">*korech*</a> combines the matzah and bitter herbs together in a single bite — a tradition attributed to Hillel. The bitter and the unleavened are bound together, teaching that suffering and humility are inseparable companions on the path to redemption. Alma weaves together warnings and promises, judgment and mercy, calling his people to hold both realities simultaneously.

> "For every shepherd that doth not raise his voice to warn shall be held accountable." — Alma 5:59 (paraphrase)

<br>

### Step 11: Shulchan Orech — שֻׁלְחָן עוֹרֵךְ — "The Set Table"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 130 | **Theme:** Partaking of the communal and festive meal

*Shulchan Orech* is the step where participants partake of the festive meal — symbolizing community, unity, and the blessings of God's provision. In Christian tradition, this parallels the sacrament, where believers partake in remembrance of Christ's atoning sacrifice.

Alma extends a direct invitation to partake: "Come and fear not, and lay aside every sin, which easily doth beset you... and come and go forth, and show unto your God that ye are willing to repent" (Alma 5:57, paraphrased from Alma 7:15). The set table is prepared. The invitation is given. Will they come?

> "Behold, he sendeth an invitation unto all men, for the arms of mercy are extended towards them." — Alma 5:33

## Part III: The Resurrection, Gathering of Israel, Second Coming, and Millennium

### Step 12: Tzafun — צָפוּן — "Hidden"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 131 | **Theme:** The hidden things revealed — the Afikomen found

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h6845/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to hide, treasure up" data-strongs="H6845" data-translit="tsaphan" data-original="צָפַן" data-lang="hebrew">*Tzafun*</a> is the moment when the hidden *afikomen* — the broken piece of matzah concealed during Yachatz — is found, brought forth, and eaten. What was broken and hidden is now revealed and shared. This step carries powerful resurrection symbolism: the body that was broken and buried is brought forth in glory.

Alma teaches of the resurrection and the revealing of hidden things: "the secrets of the heart shall be revealed" (Alma 5:18, referencing the broader prophetic tradition). What is hidden will be brought to light.

> "For our words will condemn us, yea, all our works will condemn us... and our thoughts will also condemn us." — Alma 12:14

<br>

### Step 13: Barech — בָּרֵךְ — "Bless"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 132 | **Theme:** Blessings, priesthood, and the Third Cup of Redemption

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1288/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to bless, kneel" data-strongs="H1288" data-translit="barak" data-original="בָּרַךְ" data-lang="hebrew">*Barech*</a> — the blessing after the meal — includes the pouring of the Third Cup, the Cup of Redemption. This is the cup that Jesus likely identified at the Last Supper when He said: "This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you" (Luke 22:20). The Talmud (Pesachim 64a) records that the Hallel was sung continuously while the Paschal offerings were being brought — the sacrificial act and the musical act inseparable.

Alma's teachings illustrate the dual aspects of divine interaction: warning and blessing. His initial call to repentance in Alma 5 highlights the barriers that sin creates. His later blessing upon the people of Gideon (Alma 7) illustrates the joy and peace that flow from living in accordance with divine principles — repentance preceding redemption, obedience bringing forth blessing.

> "Come unto me and ye shall partake of the fruit of the tree of life." — Alma 5:34

<br>

### Step 14: Hallel — הַלֵּל — "Praise"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 133 | **Theme:** Praise, thanksgiving, and the Fourth Cup

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1984/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to praise, shine" data-strongs="H1984" data-translit="halal" data-original="הָלַל" data-lang="hebrew">*Hallel*</a> is the singing of psalms of praise — the great crescendo of the Seder. The Fourth Cup, the Cup of Praise, is poured and drunk with the declaration: "I will take you as my people" (Exodus 6:7). Edersheim describes how during the Temple Passover, the Levites led the congregation in responsive singing of Psalms 113–118, with the people responding "Hallelujah" — the most participatory musical event in the Temple calendar.

Alma's reference to the "song of redeeming love" (Alma 5:26) may echo this very tradition. Have you felt to sing it? Can you feel so now? The Hallel is not a performance — it is a lived experience of gratitude that must be renewed.

> "If ye have felt to sing the song of redeeming love, I would ask, can ye feel so now?" — Alma 5:26

<br>

### Step 15: Nirtzah — נִרְצָה — "Accepted"

**Psalm of Ascent:** Psalm 134 | **Theme:** Being accepted of God — the culmination

<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h7521/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" data-lexicon="to be pleased with, accept" data-strongs="H7521" data-translit="ratsah" data-original="רָצָה" data-lang="hebrew">*Nirtzah*</a> means "accepted" — the Seder is complete, and the participants are accepted before God. This is the summit of the ascending pattern. Liebreich demonstrated that the fifteenth and final Song of Ascent (Psalm 134) concludes with the Priestly Blessing itself: "The Lord that made heaven and earth bless thee out of Zion" — the very benediction that was pronounced on the Temple steps where these songs were sung.

Alma's sermon builds toward this culmination: will his people be found worthy to stand before God? Will they be accepted? The entire fifteen-step pattern has been leading to this moment — from sanctification through suffering, from retelling through purification, from the broken bread to the praise song — all converging on the question: will God accept you?

> "Come unto me and bring forth works of righteousness." — Alma 5:35 (paraphrase)

The Seder concludes with the declaration: "Next year in Jerusalem!" — the hope of return, of gathering, of the Messiah's coming. Alma, speaking to Nephites in a distant land, carries the same hope forward: the holy order endures, the pattern holds, and the God who delivered their fathers will deliver them — if they will walk the ascending path.

## Alma's Fifty Questions

One of the most striking features of Alma 5 is its relentless questioning. The Maggid section of the Seder traditionally centers on questions — the famous Four Questions asked by the youngest participant — but the tradition is not limited to four. One could ask ten or twenty. Alma asks nearly fifty.

This question-driven approach mirrors the Seder's pedagogical method. The Zohar teaches that the retelling of the Exodus must be done "fervently and joyously" — and Alma's relentless questioning is itself a form of sacred retelling, pressing his audience to internalize the pattern of deliverance not as distant history but as present spiritual reality.

Among Alma's questions:

- "Have ye sufficiently retained in remembrance the captivity of your fathers?" (5:6)
- "Have ye received his image in your countenances?" (5:14)
- "Can you imagine to yourselves that ye hear the voice of the Lord?" (5:16)
- "Can ye look up to God at that day with a pure heart and clean hands?" (5:19)
- "Could ye say, if ye were called to die at this time... that ye have been sufficiently humble?" (5:27)
- "Is there one among you who is not stripped of envy?" (5:29)
- "Is there one among you that doth make a mock of his brother?" (5:30)
- "If ye have experienced a change of heart, and if ye have felt to sing the song of redeeming love, I would ask, can ye feel so now?" (5:26)

Each question is a step in the ascending pattern — pressing deeper, demanding more honest self-examination, moving the listener from comfortable remembrance toward uncomfortable accountability. The questions are not rhetorical. They are liturgical. They are the Maggid of the Nephite Seder.
