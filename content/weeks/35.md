description: "Week 35 crosses a seam in the Psalter. Fifteen of its seventeen psalms sit inside Psalms 42–83, the block scholars call the Elohistic Psalter, where the editors preferred the ordinary word for God over the covenant name — and the reading hands you the proof in a form anyone can check: Psalm 70 is the same poem as Psalm 40:13–17, which you read last week, with the divine name changed. At the centre is the Psalter's great prayer of repentance. Psalm 51 is the most precisely dated poem in the book — its heading places it after Nathan confronted David — and its central petition asks not to be repaired but re-made: 'Create in me a clean heart, O God,' using the verb of Genesis 1:1, whose subject in the Old Testament is always God. Around it stand a psalm that says money cannot ransom a brother (49), the prayer of old age that asks not for relief but for time to finish testifying (71), and Asaph's long history lesson told to children with the failures left in (78)."
quick_summary: "This week is about what a person brings when the ordinary remedies have run out. David's sin had no offering appointed for it under the law — so Psalm 51 offers 'a broken and a contrite heart' instead, and asks God for the Genesis verb. The study guide traces seven Hebrew words, including 'ak (only), the small particle that opens six verses of Psalm 62 and comes into English six different ways so no reader can see it; and padah (to redeem), whose Greek translation gave the New Testament the word behind 'a ransom for many.' It reads the rabbis on why Psalm 51 does not end where you expect, and it is careful about the New Testament: of twelve places these psalms are carried into the Gospels and epistles, only two are the Savior's own words — the rest are Paul, Peter, the disciples remembering, a crowd He corrects, or the narrative itself."
