## The Missing Word

English cannot form a sentence without a verb. "God holy" is a fragment. You need "God **is** holy" to make it complete. The verb "is" — called a **copula** — links the subject to the predicate.

Hebrew doesn't need it.

In Hebrew, placing a subject next to a predicate creates a complete sentence. No verb required. No "is," no "am," no "are." The grammar simply places two elements side by side and trusts the reader to hear the connection.

These are called **verbless sentences** (or **nominal sentences**, because they are built from nouns, pronouns, and adjectives rather than verbs). And the first words God speaks at Sinai are one:

<div style="background: rgba(74,107,82,0.08); border-left: 4px solid #4a6b52; border-radius: 8px; padding: 20px; margin: 20px 0;">

<p dir="rtl" lang="he" style="font-size: 1.4em; line-height: 1.8; margin-bottom: 12px; text-align: right;"><strong>אָנֹכִי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ</strong></p>

*"**I** [am] the LORD your God"* — [Exodus 20:2](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/20/2/)

</div>

There is no verb in this sentence. No form of "to be." Just a pronoun (<span dir="rtl" lang="he">אָנֹכִי</span>, *anokhi*, "I"), a name (<span dir="rtl" lang="he">יְהוָה</span>, YHWH), and a noun with a pronoun suffix (<span dir="rtl" lang="he">אֱלֹהֶיךָ</span>, [*elohekha*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h430/kjv/wlc/0-1/), "your God"). Three elements. No verb. And it is the most important sentence in the Torah.

In Lesson 12, we introduced this idea briefly when we looked at <span dir="rtl" lang="he">זֶה אֵלִי</span> — "This [is] my God" ([Exodus 15:2](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/15/2/)). Now let's explore how verbless sentences work, when Hebrew *does* use a "to be" verb, and why the difference matters.

## How Verbless Sentences Work

A verbless sentence has two parts:

1. **Subject** — who or what the sentence is about
2. **Predicate** — what is being said about the subject

In English, a verb links them: "The LORD **is** great." In Hebrew, they simply stand side by side:

<div style="background: rgba(74,107,82,0.08); border-left: 4px solid #4a6b52; border-radius: 8px; padding: 20px; margin: 20px 0;">

<p dir="rtl" lang="he" style="font-size: 1.3em; line-height: 1.8; margin-bottom: 8px; text-align: right;">כִּי גָדוֹל יְהוָה מִכׇּל הָאֱלֹהִים</p>

*"For **great** [is] the LORD above all gods"* — [Exodus 18:11](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/18/11/)

</div>

<span dir="rtl" lang="he">גָדוֹל</span> ([*gadol*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1419/kjv/wlc/0-1/)) is an adjective meaning "great." <span dir="rtl" lang="he">יְהוָה</span> is the subject. No verb connects them. Hebrew simply says: "Great — the LORD." The reader supplies the "is."

**1. Pronoun + Noun (Identity)**

The subject is a pronoun, the predicate is a noun — this declares *who someone is*:

The absence of a verb makes the statement absolute. There is no process. There is no becoming. There is only identity.

Notice something? In Hebrew, the adjective often comes **first** — before the noun. <span dir="rtl" lang="he">גָדוֹל יְהוָה</span> literally says "Great — the LORD," not "The LORD — great." This is one way Hebrew signals that the adjective is the **predicate** (telling you something about the subject) rather than a simple **modifier** (describing the noun). Compare:

- <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הַמֶּלֶךְ הַגָּדוֹל</span> (*hammelekh haggadol*) — "the great king" ← adjective *follows* noun, both have the article = **modifier**
- <span dir="rtl" lang="he">גָדוֹל הַמֶּלֶךְ</span> (*gadol hammelekh*) — "great [is] the king" ← adjective *precedes* noun, no article on adjective = **predicate** (verbless sentence)

This distinction is important. The word order and the presence or absence of the article tell you whether you're reading a noun phrase ("the great king") or a complete sentence ("the king is great").

If you find a pronoun next to a noun, or a noun next to an adjective (especially with mismatched articles or reversed word order), you're probably looking at a verbless sentence. Supply "is" or "am" or "are" in your English translation and keep going.

## When Hebrew *Does* Use "To Be"

Hebrew is not *always* verbless. It has a perfectly good verb for "to be" — <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span> ([*hayah*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1961/kjv/wlc/0-1/)) — but it uses it in specific circumstances:

### Past tense: something *was*

<div style="background: rgba(74,107,82,0.08); border-left: 4px solid #4a6b52; border-radius: 8px; padding: 20px; margin: 20px 0;">

<p dir="rtl" lang="he" style="font-size: 1.3em; line-height: 1.8; margin-bottom: 8px; text-align: right;">וְהָאָ֗רֶץ הָיְתָ֥ה תֹ֙הוּ֙ וָבֹ֔הוּ</p>

*"And the earth **was** formless and void"* — [Genesis 1:2](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/1/2/)

</div>

Here <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיְתָה</span> (*hayetah*) is the 3fs Qatal (past) form of <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span> — "she was" (referring to <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָאָרֶץ</span>, "the earth," which is grammatically feminine). Hebrew needs the verb here because the state is **past** — the earth *was* this way, before God acted.

### Future tense: something *will be*

<div style="background: rgba(74,107,82,0.08); border-left: 4px solid #4a6b52; border-radius: 8px; padding: 20px; margin: 20px 0;">

<p dir="rtl" lang="he" style="font-size: 1.3em; line-height: 1.8; margin-bottom: 8px; text-align: right;">וְאַתֶּם תִּהְיוּ לִי מַמְלֶכֶת כֹּהֲנִים וְגוֹי קָדוֹשׁ</p>

*"And you **shall be** to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation"* — [Exodus 19:6](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/19/6/)

</div>

Here <span dir="rtl" lang="he">תִּהְיוּ</span> (*tihyu*) is the 2mp Yiqtol (future/imperfect) form — "you shall be." God is speaking about what Israel *will become*, not what they already are. The verb marks the statement as future.

### The pattern

| Tense | Hebrew uses... | Example |
|-------|---------------|---------|
| **Present** | No verb (verbless sentence) | <span dir="rtl" lang="he">גָדוֹל יְהוָה</span> — "Great [is] the LORD" |
| **Past** | <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span> (Qatal form) | <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיְתָה תֹהוּ</span> — "[it] **was** formless" |
| **Future** | <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span> (Yiqtol form) | <span dir="rtl" lang="he">תִּהְיוּ לִי</span> — "you **shall be** to me" |

When something simply **is** — right now, timelessly, absolutely — Hebrew needs no verb. When something **was** or **will be**, Hebrew reaches for <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span> to anchor it in time.

<div style="background: rgba(213,169,60,0.12); border-left: 4px solid #d5a93c; border-radius: 8px; padding: 16px 20px; margin-bottom: 20px;">

**The theological weight:**

As we noted in Lesson 12, the root ה-י-ה (*h-y-h*, "to be/exist") is the root from which the Divine Name <span dir="rtl" lang="he">יְהוָה</span> (YHWH) derives. God's name at the burning bush — <span dir="rtl" lang="he">אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה</span> (*ehyeh asher ehyeh*, "I Will Be What I Will Be," [Exodus 3:14](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/3/14/)) — is built from this verb.

Consider what this means for Exodus 20:2. God could have said <span dir="rtl" lang="he">אָנֹכִי **הָיִיתִי** יְהוָה</span> ("I **was** the LORD") or <span dir="rtl" lang="he">אָנֹכִי **אֶהְיֶה** יְהוָה</span> ("I **will be** the LORD"). He uses neither. The verbless sentence places His identity outside of time. He does not say "I was" or "I will be" your God. He says "I — the LORD your God." No tense. No process. Just Being.

The One whose Name *is* the verb "to be" speaks His covenant identity in a sentence that has no verb at all. The silence where "is" should be *is* the Name.

</div>

- **Translation:** "For I [am] the LORD your God, a jealous God"
- **Type:** Pronoun + Noun (expanded identity declaration)
- **Note:** <span dir="rtl" lang="he">קַנָּא</span> ([*qanna*](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h7067/kjv/wlc/0-1/)) means "jealous" or "zealous" — not petty envy but the fierce exclusivity of covenant love.

</div>
</details>

</div>

<div style="background: rgba(87,137,156,0.08); border-left: 4px solid #57899c; border-radius: 8px; padding: 16px 20px; margin-bottom: 20px;">

**5. Bonus — Contrast:** [Exodus 19:6](https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/19/6/) — <span dir="rtl" lang="he">וְאַתֶּם תִּהְיוּ לִי מַמְלֶכֶת כֹּהֲנִים</span>

<details>
<summary style="cursor: pointer; color: #57899c; font-weight: 600;">Show answer</summary>
<div style="margin-top: 8px;">

- This is **not** a verbless sentence. The verb <span dir="rtl" lang="he">תִּהְיוּ</span> (*tihyu*, "you shall be") is present — a future form of <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span>.
- **Why the verb?** Because this is a **promise**, not a present reality. Israel is not yet a kingdom of priests. They *will be*, if they keep the covenant. The verb anchors the promise in future time.
- Compare with Exodus 20:2: God's identity is verbless (timeless), but Israel's identity requires a verb (they are becoming something they are not yet).

</details>

## What Comes Next

You now understand how Hebrew builds sentences from nouns alone — and when it reaches for the verb <span dir="rtl" lang="he">הָיָה</span> to anchor something in time. This means you can recognize three kinds of structures when reading:

- ✅ **Verbal sentences** — built around a verb (most of Hebrew narrative)
- ✅ **Verbless sentences** — two nouns or a noun and adjective, no verb (declarations, descriptions, equations)
- ✅ **הָיָה sentences** — past or future states that need a time marker

Next, we'll explore what happens when **two nouns link together** to form a single concept — the **construct state** (סמיכות). You've already seen one in this lesson: <span dir="rtl" lang="he">אֱלֹהֵי אָבִי</span> (*elohei avi*, "God of my father"). That phrase has no word for "of" — the nouns simply lean on each other. How that works, and how to recognize it, is the subject of Lesson 14.
