<h2 style="text-align: center;">Major Sites & Geography</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The places where three thousand years of civilization unfolded &mdash; and where the biblical narrative meets the archaeological record</p>

<p>Egypt is the Nile. Without the river's annual flood depositing rich silt across the floodplain, the civilization that built the pyramids, produced the Book of the Dead, and shaped the biblical narrative could never have existed. A narrow ribbon of green cuts through the eastern Sahara &mdash; rarely more than a few miles wide &mdash; and along that ribbon, one of humanity's greatest civilizations flourished for three millennia.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Understanding Egypt's Geography</h3>

<h4>Upper and Lower Egypt</h4>

<p>Egyptian geography seems backward to modern readers: <strong>Upper Egypt</strong> is in the south, <strong>Lower Egypt</strong> is in the north. This is because the Nile flows <em>northward</em> &mdash; from the highlands of East Africa to the Mediterranean Sea. "Upper" and "Lower" refer to elevation, not compass direction.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Upper Egypt</strong> &mdash; The narrow Nile valley stretching from Aswan (the first cataract) to just south of Cairo. Major cities: Thebes/Luxor, Edfu, Elephantine. This is the land of temples, tombs, and the Valley of the Kings.</li>
<li><strong>Lower Egypt</strong> &mdash; The broad Nile Delta, where the river fans out into multiple branches before reaching the Mediterranean. Major cities: Memphis, Alexandria, Avaris/Pi-Ramesses, Goshen. This is the land of agriculture, trade, and &mdash; in biblical terms &mdash; the land of Israel's sojourn.</li>
</ul>

<p>The <strong>Double Crown</strong> of the pharaohs combined the White Crown of Upper Egypt and the Red Crown of Lower Egypt, symbolizing the unity of the two lands &mdash; a unity that was always somewhat tenuous and that broke apart during each intermediate period.</p>

<h4>The Nile's Cataracts</h4>

<p>Six cataracts (stretches of rapids and shallow water) interrupt the Nile between Aswan and Khartoum. The <strong>first cataract</strong> at Aswan marked Egypt's traditional southern border. <strong>Elephantine Island</strong>, site of the Jewish military colony, sits at this first cataract &mdash; literally at the edge of Egypt.</p>

  <p>Founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BC, Alexandria became the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean world. Its <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Library_of_Alexandria/" target="_blank">Library</a></strong> (the Mouseion), established under <strong>Ptolemy II Philadelphus</strong> around 283 BC, aimed to collect all the world's knowledge and may have held hundreds of thousands of scrolls at its peak. The library suffered major damage during Julius Caesar's siege in 48 BC, and its remaining collections were likely destroyed during the decree of Emperor Theodosius I in AD 391. The <strong>Pharos Lighthouse</strong>, one of the Seven Wonders, guided ships into the harbor.</p>

  <p>For Jewish history, Alexandria was home to the largest Jewish community in the ancient world, the birthplace of the <strong>Septuagint</strong>, and the home of <strong>Philo</strong>. For Christian history, it was the seat of one of the five great patriarchates, the birthplace of monasticism, and the intellectual home of Clement, Origen, and Athanasius.</p>

  <p>The modern <strong><a href="https://www.bibalex.org/" target="_blank">Bibliotheca Alexandrina</a></strong>, opened in 2002 near the site of the ancient library, is a deliberate echo of Alexandria's scholarly legacy. Today, most of ancient Alexandria lies beneath the modern city or underwater in the harbor. Visitors can also explore the <strong>Catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa</strong> &mdash; Roman-era underground tombs blending Egyptian, Greek, and Roman funerary art &mdash; and <strong>Pompey's Pillar</strong>, remnants of Roman Alexandria.</p>

  <p>Three names for overlapping areas of the eastern Delta that are central to the biblical narrative:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>Goshen</strong> &mdash; The biblical name for the region where Joseph settled his family (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/47/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 47:6">Genesis 47:6</a>). The exact boundaries are uncertain, but it corresponds to the eastern Delta's pastureland.</li>
    <li><strong>Avaris</strong> (modern Tell el-Dab'a) &mdash; The <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Hyksos/" target="_blank">Hyksos</a> capital (c. 1650&ndash;1550 BC), located within the Goshen region. Austrian archaeologist Manfred Bietak's excavations have revealed a Semitic settlement with Canaanite-style architecture, Syro-Palestinian pottery, and donkey burials &mdash; cultural markers consistent with a Semitic population living in Egypt.</li>
    <li><strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Pi-Ramesses/" target="_blank" data-ref="Pi-Ramesses">Pi-Ramesses</a></strong> &mdash; The great city built by Ramesses II in the eastern Delta, near the site of Avaris. <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/1/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 1:11">Exodus 1:11</a> mentions that the Israelites built the store cities "Pithom and Raamses" &mdash; Pi-Ramesses is the most likely candidate for the latter.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Also in the Delta: <strong>Tanis</strong> (San el-Hagar), the 21st&ndash;22nd Dynasty capital whose royal tombs rival the Valley of the Kings (discovered 1939).</p>

  <div class="smw-callout">
    <p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The geography of the Exodus:</strong> The eastern Delta is where the biblical narrative concentrates. Joseph's family settled here. The Israelites labored here. Moses confronted Pharaoh here. And the Exodus journey began here, heading east toward the Red Sea (or Sea of Reeds). Understanding that Goshen, Avaris, and Pi-Ramesses all occupy the same general region &mdash; the eastern Delta, closest to Canaan &mdash; helps make geographic sense of the entire patriarchal-through-Exodus narrative.</p>
  </div>

  <p>The three great pyramids &mdash; <strong>Khufu</strong> (the Great Pyramid), <strong>Khafre</strong>, and <strong>Menkaure</strong> &mdash; are the most iconic monuments of Egyptian civilization. The Great Pyramid stood as the tallest structure on earth for nearly 4,000 years (until the construction of Lincoln Cathedral's spire around AD 1311).</p>

  <p>The pyramids were already over a thousand years old when Abraham entered Egypt. By the New Kingdom (Moses's era), they were ancient monuments that Egyptians themselves visited as tourists. The <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Great_Sphinx_of_Giza/" target="_blank">Great Sphinx</a></strong>, carved from the bedrock of the plateau, was periodically buried in sand and excavated even in antiquity.</p>

  <p>Modern archaeology has established that the pyramids were built by organized labor forces (not slaves) &mdash; workers' villages, bakeries, and administrative records have been excavated at Giza. The adjacent <strong><a href="https://grandegyptianmuseum.org/" target="_blank">Grand Egyptian Museum</a></strong> (opened 2025) &mdash; the largest archaeological museum in the world &mdash; houses over 100,000 artifacts, including Tutankhamun's complete treasures.</p>

  <p><strong>Saqqara</strong> is the vast necropolis of nearby <a href="/culture/ancient/egypt/08-major-sites/">Memphis</a>, stretching across the desert plateau for nearly four miles. It contains burials spanning almost every period of Egyptian history. Its most famous monument is the <strong>Step Pyramid of Djoser</strong> (c. 2670 BC), designed by the legendary architect <strong>Imhotep</strong> &mdash; the world's oldest monumental stone structure and the prototype from which all later pyramids evolved.</p>

  <p>Beyond the Step Pyramid, Saqqara holds the <strong>Serapeum</strong> &mdash; the underground catacombs where the sacred Apis bulls were mummified and entombed in enormous granite sarcophagi &mdash; as well as decorated mastaba tombs whose painted reliefs provide some of the most detailed images of daily life in the Old Kingdom. Recent excavations continue to uncover new tombs and workshops, making Saqqara one of Egypt's most active archaeological sites.</p>

  <p><strong>Memphis</strong> was Egypt's first great capital, founded at the strategic junction where Upper and Lower Egypt meet. It served as the administrative heart of the Old Kingdom and remained a major religious and commercial center for nearly three thousand years. Memphis was the cult center of <strong>Ptah</strong>, the creator god whom Egyptian theology credited with speaking the world into existence. The word "Coptic" &mdash; and ultimately "Egypt" itself &mdash; derives from the Greek name for Memphis's great temple: <em>Hwt-ka-Ptah</em> ("mansion of the spirit of Ptah") became <em>Aigyptos</em>, which became <em>qibt</em>.</p>

  <p>Memphis figures prominently in biblical prophecy. Jeremiah prophesied against the city (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/46/14/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 46:14, 19">Jeremiah 46:14, 19</a>), and Jewish refugees settled there after Jerusalem's destruction in 586 BC (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/44/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 44:1">Jeremiah 44:1</a>). Isaiah and Hosea also reference the city under its Hebrew name <strong>Noph</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/19/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 19:13">Isaiah 19:13</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/hos/9/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Hosea 9:6">Hosea 9:6</a>). Today the site is best known for the fallen <strong>Colossus of Ramesses II</strong> preserved in an open-air museum at Mit Rahina.</p>

  <p><strong>Heliopolis</strong> (Egyptian <em>Iunu</em>; biblical <strong>"On"</strong>) was one of ancient Egypt's most important sacred cities and the principal center of the solar cult of <strong>Ra</strong>. Its priesthood developed the <strong>Ennead</strong> &mdash; the group of nine creator gods headed by Atum-Ra &mdash; whose theology shaped Egyptian religion for millennia. The great obelisks that became synonymous with Egypt originated here; Heliopolis was filled with them, and pharaohs later carried the tradition to temple complexes across the country.</p>

  <p>Heliopolis has a direct biblical connection. Joseph married <strong>Asenath</strong>, the daughter of <strong>Potiphera, priest of On</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/41/45/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 41:45">Genesis 41:45</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/46/20/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 46:20">Genesis 46:20</a>). That Joseph's wife came from a priestly family at Egypt's foremost religious center underscores the extraordinary position he held in Egyptian society.</p>

  <p>Very little of the ancient city survives above ground today &mdash; modern Cairo has grown over most of the site. The <strong>Obelisk of Senusret I</strong> (c. 1950 BC), the oldest standing obelisk in Egypt, still marks the location and stands as a solitary witness to the city that once rivaled Memphis and Thebes in importance.</p>

  <p>The <strong>Ben Ezra Synagogue</strong> is famous for the discovery of the <strong>Cairo Genizah</strong> &mdash; a storeroom containing approximately <strong>400,000 manuscript fragments</strong> spanning a thousand years (c. AD 870&ndash;1880). Jewish law prohibits destroying documents containing the name of God, so worn-out texts were deposited in the genizah rather than discarded.</p>

  <p>The discovery, principally by Solomon Schechter in 1896, revolutionized the study of medieval Jewish history, literature, liturgy, and daily life. Genizah fragments are now housed in libraries worldwide, with the largest collection at Cambridge University.</p>

  <p>Tradition holds that the synagogue was built on the site where the infant Moses was found in the Nile, or where the prophet Jeremiah gathered the Jewish community. The nearby <strong>Hanging Church</strong> and <strong>Church of Saints Sergius and Bacchus</strong> (Abu Serga), built over a crypt traditionally associated with the Holy Family's stay in Egypt, testify to the deep roots of both Jewish and Christian life in Egypt.</p>

  <p>The mountain where Moses received the Law is the pivotal site of the covenant between God and Israel. After the Exodus from Egypt, the Israelites camped at the foot of the mountain while Moses ascended into the cloud of God's presence to receive the Ten Commandments and the instructions for the Tabernacle (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/19/16/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 19:16-20">Exodus 19:16&ndash;20</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/20/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 20:1-17">Exodus 20:1&ndash;17</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/24/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 24:12-18">Exodus 24:12&ndash;18</a>). The exact identification of the mountain remains debated among scholars, but <strong>Jebel Musa</strong> in the southern Sinai has been the traditional site since at least the 4th century AD.</p>

  <p>At the foot of Jebel Musa stands <strong>St. Catherine's Monastery</strong>, built by order of Emperor Justinian in the 6th century AD. It is one of the oldest continuously operating Christian monasteries in the world. Its library preserves the <strong>Codex Sinaiticus</strong>, discovered by Constantine von Tischendorf in 1844 &mdash; one of the earliest near-complete manuscripts of the Greek Bible and a cornerstone of modern biblical textual scholarship.</p>

<div class="smw-site" data-id="horeb" data-name="Mount Horeb" data-top="31.6" data-left="89.8" data-marker-top="31.6" data-marker-left="89.8" data-icon="horeb">
  <p class="smw-meta"><strong>Period:</strong> Biblical era &nbsp;|&nbsp; <strong>Location:</strong> Debated &mdash; see below</p>

  <div class="smw-img-wrap">
    <img class="lightbox-img" src="/images/culture/egypt/08-split-rock-horeb.png" alt="The Split Rock near Jebel al-Maqla">
    <p class="smw-img-caption">The "Split Rock of Moses" near Jebel al-Maqla in northwestern Saudi Arabia &mdash; identified as such by local tradition, though not archaeologically verified.</p>
  </div>

  <p>The relationship between <strong>Horeb</strong> and <strong>Sinai</strong> is one of the most debated questions in biblical geography. "Sinai" appears primarily in Exodus and Leviticus, while "Horeb" appears in Deuteronomy and the historical books. Some scholars treat them as two names for the same mountain; others see Horeb as a broader region with Sinai as a specific peak within it; still others propose that the burning bush narrative took place at a different mountain entirely from the one where the Law was given.</p>

  <p>The traditional identification places both Sinai and Horeb at <strong>Jebel Musa</strong> in the southern Sinai Peninsula (the site of St. Catherine's Monastery). However, alternative proposals include <strong>Jebel al-Maqla</strong> near Jabal al-Lawz in northwestern Saudi Arabia, based partly on Paul's reference to "mount Sinai in Arabia" (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gal/4/25/" target="_blank" data-ref="Galatians 4:25">Galatians 4:25</a>) and the location of biblical Midian. Other candidates have been proposed in the central and northern Sinai. None of these identifications can be determined definitively.</p>

  <p>Horeb is especially associated with <strong>Elijah's</strong> journey. Fleeing Queen Jezebel, the prophet traveled forty days and forty nights to "Horeb the mount of God," where he sheltered in a cave. There God passed by &mdash; not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire, but in the <strong>"still small voice"</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/19/8/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 19:8-12">1 Kings 19:8&ndash;12</a>). Moses encountered God at Horeb as well: the burning bush appeared "in Horeb" (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/3/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 3:1-6">Exodus 3:1&ndash;6</a>), and the rock that yielded water was struck "in Horeb" (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/17/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 17:6">Exodus 17:6</a>).</p>

  <p>In 1999, Yale Egyptologists John and Deborah Darnell discovered two short inscriptions carved into the limestone walls of a remote desert wadi along an ancient caravan route. These <strong>Wadi el-Hol inscriptions</strong>, dating to roughly 1800 BC, are among the earliest known examples of alphabetic writing. The letter forms were adapted from Egyptian hieroglyphs to write a Semitic language &mdash; the same innovation seen at <a href="/culture/ancient/egypt/08-major-sites/">Serabit el-Khadim</a> in the Sinai.</p>

  <p>The inscriptions appear to have been carved by Semitic-speaking workers or soldiers serving in the Egyptian military. Together with the <strong>Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions</strong> from Serabit el-Khadim, they provide the strongest evidence that the alphabet was invented by Semitic peoples living in close contact with Egyptian culture during the early second millennium BC &mdash; a revolution in communication explored further in our <a href="/culture/ancient/egypt/04-writing-systems/">Writing Systems</a> section.</p>

  <p>The <strong>Temple of Hathor</strong> at Dendera is one of the most complete and visually stunning temples in Egypt. Though the current structure dates to the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, worship at Dendera stretches back to at least the Old Kingdom. The temple's ceilings preserve some of the finest astronomical art in the ancient world, including the famous <strong>Dendera Zodiac</strong> &mdash; a circular star map now displayed in the Louvre &mdash; which represents one of the earliest known depictions of the constellations in their recognizable forms.</p>

  <p>The complex includes a <strong>mammisi</strong> (birthing house) celebrating the divine birth of Hathor's son Ihy, underground <strong>crypts</strong> decorated with ritual scenes, and exterior carvings depicting <strong>Cleopatra VII</strong> and her son <strong>Caesarion</strong> making offerings to the gods &mdash; among the last royal images of pharaonic Egypt. The temple roof, accessible by stairway, offers panoramic views and preserves chapels dedicated to the resurrection of Osiris.</p>

  <p>The largest religious building ever constructed &mdash; built, expanded, and modified over nearly two thousand years by successive pharaohs. Karnak was the home of Amun-Ra and the center of Egyptian state religion. Its great <strong>Hypostyle Hall</strong> (134 columns, the largest reaching 69 feet) remains one of the most awe-inspiring architectural achievements of the ancient world.</p>

  <p><strong>Sheshonq I's</strong> (biblical Shishak) campaign against Judah is recorded on the <strong>Bubastite Portal</strong> at Karnak (c. 925 BC) &mdash; one of the rare cases where an Egyptian inscription directly corroborates a biblical account (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/14/25/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 14:25-26">1 Kings 14:25&ndash;26</a>).</p>

  <p><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Thebes_(Egypt)/" target="_blank">Thebes</a> (modern Luxor) was the great religious capital of the New Kingdom. The prophet Nahum referenced the Assyrian sack of Thebes (663 BC) as a warning to Nineveh (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/nah/3/8/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nahum 3:8">Nahum 3:8</a>). The Hebrew name for Thebes was <strong>No</strong> or <strong>No-Amon</strong> ("city of Amun"). In the Ptolemaic period, Thebes had the <strong>greatest concentration of Jews outside Alexandria</strong> (Muhlestein). The priest <strong>Hor</strong>, whose papyrus would become part of the Joseph Smith collection, served at the Karnak, Montu, and Khonsu temples in Thebes around 200 BC.</p>

  <p><strong>Luxor Temple</strong> was the southern partner to Karnak, connected by the great <strong>Avenue of the Sphinxes</strong> &mdash; a 1.7-mile processional road lined with sphinx statues, recently restored and reopened in 2021.   <p>Unlike the more sprawling temple city at Karnak, Luxor Temple preserves a compact, processional grandeur that makes it one of the clearest examples of how Egyptian state ritual shaped sacred architecture.</p>

  <p>The royal necropolis of the New Kingdom pharaohs, containing 65 known tombs cut into the limestone cliffs.   <p>The tombs are decorated with elaborate scenes from funerary texts including the <strong>Book of the Dead</strong>, the <strong>Book of Gates</strong>, and the <strong>Amduat</strong> ("Book of What Is in the Underworld") &mdash; the same religious traditions that produced the papyri Joseph Smith would later acquire.</p>

  <p>The <strong>Mortuary Temple of Hatshepsut</strong> at Deir el-Bahri is one of Egypt's most distinctive monuments, built in dramatic terraces against the western cliffs of Thebes. Its clean horizontal lines make it instantly recognizable among Egyptian temples.</p>

  <p><strong>Hatshepsut</strong> was one of the rare women to rule Egypt as pharaoh in her own right, reigning for approximately twenty years during which Egypt prospered. The temple preserves reliefs of her famous trading expedition to the land of <strong>Punt</strong> and assertions of her divine birth. After her death, her stepson <strong>Thutmose III</strong> systematically erased her name and images from monuments across Egypt &mdash; a campaign of <em>damnatio memoriae</em> that makes the survival of this temple all the more remarkable.</p>

  <p>The <strong>Temple of Horus</strong> at Edfu is the second-largest Egyptian temple (after Karnak) and one of the most remarkably well-preserved, having been buried under sand and village buildings for centuries. Built during the Ptolemaic period, it preserves the full plan of an Egyptian temple &mdash; from the massive pylon entrance through the hypostyle halls to the inner sanctuary &mdash; giving modern visitors the clearest picture of what a complete Egyptian temple looked like in its functioning state.</p>

  <p>Edfu's walls contain an unusually complete record of temple <strong>liturgy and ritual</strong>, including the dramatic annual reenactment of the myth of <strong>Horus defeating Seth</strong> &mdash; the triumph of order over chaos. The temple's inscriptions also preserve the "Building Texts," which describe the mythological origins of Egyptian temples themselves, tracing the tradition back to a primeval island of creation.</p>

  <p>A unique <strong>double temple</strong> dedicated to two gods: <strong>Sobek</strong> (the crocodile god) on the right and <strong>Horus the Elder</strong> on the left. The temple is perfectly symmetrical, with twin entrances, twin halls, and twin sanctuaries. Its riverside location overlooking the Nile &mdash; at a point where crocodiles once gathered in large numbers &mdash; makes it one of the most dramatically situated temples in Egypt.</p>

  <p>The temple walls include an unusual carved relief depicting what appear to be <strong>surgical instruments</strong>, suggesting medical knowledge associated with the site. Adjacent to the temple, a small <strong>Crocodile Museum</strong> displays mummified crocodiles recovered from a nearby necropolis &mdash; a vivid reminder of the reverence Egyptians held for Sobek's sacred animal. In antiquity, Kom Ombo also served as a <strong>military garrison</strong> controlling the trade routes from Nubia into Egypt.</p>

  <p>A small island at Egypt's southern frontier that became home to the <strong>Jewish military colony</strong> discussed in our <a href="/culture/ancient/egypt/03-jewish-communities/">Jewish Communities</a> section. The Aramaic papyri discovered here &mdash; letters, contracts, and religious documents from a Jewish garrison serving the Persian Empire &mdash; are among the most important extrabiblical witnesses to Jewish life in the 5th century BC.</p>

  <p>The island also housed a temple to the ram-headed god <strong>Khnum</strong>, whose priests destroyed the Jewish temple around 410 BC. A <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Nile/" target="_blank">Nilometer</a></strong> (ancient gauge for measuring the Nile flood) survives on the island and was critical for predicting agricultural productivity. Nearby in an Aswan granite quarry lies the <strong>Unfinished Obelisk</strong> &mdash; an enormous abandoned monument that reveals exactly how obelisks were carved from the bedrock.</p>

  <p>One of the last functioning Egyptian temples, Philae was dedicated to the goddess <strong>Isis</strong> and remained active well into the Christian era. The last known <strong>hieroglyphic inscription</strong> (AD 394) and the last known <strong>Demotic inscription</strong> (AD 452) were both carved here &mdash; making Philae the site where the ancient Egyptian writing tradition finally fell silent.</p>

  <p>Like Abu Simbel, the temple was relocated in the 1960s&ndash;70s to save it from the Aswan High Dam's floodwaters.</p>

  <p><strong>Ramesses II's</strong> rock-cut temple complex, featuring four colossal seated figures of the pharaoh (each 66 feet tall) guarding the entrance. The temple was designed so that twice a year, on February 22 and October 22, the rising sun penetrated the full length of the inner sanctuary to illuminate the statues of the gods within.</p>

  <p>Adjacent to the Great Temple stands the smaller <strong>Temple of Nefertari</strong>, dedicated to Ramesses' principal queen and the goddess Hathor &mdash; one of the very few Egyptian temples dedicated to a royal wife. In the 1960s, both temples were dismantled and relocated to higher ground to save them from the rising waters of Lake Nasser behind the Aswan High Dam &mdash; one of the greatest feats of archaeological engineering ever undertaken.</p>
