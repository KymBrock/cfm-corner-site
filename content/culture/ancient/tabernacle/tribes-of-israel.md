## The Levite Center — Guardians of the Sanctuary

Before describing the four outer camps, the inner ring belongs to Levi. Unlike the other tribes, Levi received no territorial inheritance — the Lord Himself was their inheritance (Deuteronomy 10:9). They camped directly around the Tabernacle and were responsible for its transport, assembly, care, and the priesthood that served within it.

---

### Levi — לֵוִי — "Joined" / "Attached"

#### The Brother

Levi was the third son of Jacob and Leah. At his birth, Leah said, "Now this time will my husband be joined unto me, because I have born him three sons" (Genesis 29:34). The name *Levi* (לֵוִי) comes from the root *lavah* (לָוָה), meaning "to join" or "to be attached." What began as a mother's longing for her husband's affection became a prophetic name — Levi's descendants would be *joined* to the service of God and would *attach* all Israel to the covenant through priesthood ordinance.

#### The Story of Simeon and Levi

Levi and his brother Simeon are bound together in one of the most troubling episodes in Genesis. After the assault on their sister Dinah at Shechem, Levi and Simeon took violent revenge, slaughtering the men of the city (Genesis 34:25–29). Jacob rebuked them sharply, and this act cast a long shadow over both brothers' futures.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:5–7)

> "Simeon and Levi are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. O my soul, come not thou into their secret; unto their assembly, mine honour, be not thou united: for in their anger they slew a man, and in their selfwill they digged down a wall. Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel: I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel."

Jacob does not bless Levi — he pronounces a scattering. Both Simeon and Levi would be dispersed among the tribes rather than receiving a consolidated territory. Yet what appears as a curse becomes, for Levi, a consecration. The scattering meant Levi would have cities distributed throughout *all* the tribal territories (Joshua 21), placing priests and Levites among every part of Israel as teachers, ministers, and keepers of the covenant.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:8–11)

> "Let thy Thummim and thy Urim be with thy holy one, whom thou didst prove at Massah, and with whom thou didst strive at the waters of Meribah; Who said unto his father and to his mother, I have not seen him; neither did he acknowledge his brethren, nor knew his own children: for they have observed thy word, and kept thy covenant. They shall teach Jacob thy judgments, and Israel thy law: they shall put incense before thee, and whole burnt sacrifice upon thine altar. Bless, LORD, his substance, and accept the work of his hands."

By the time of Moses, Levi's story had been transformed. The tribe that once acted in violent self-will at Shechem now stood with Moses at Sinai when he cried, "Who is on the Lord's side?" (Exodus 32:26). The Levites rallied. Their fierce loyalty — once misdirected — was now consecrated to God. Moses' blessing centers Levi's identity on four functions: bearing the Urim and Thummim (prophetic revelation), teaching Torah to Israel, offering incense (intercession), and performing sacrifice (atonement).

#### Tribal Role and Duties

Levi was divided into three clans, each assigned specific duties around the Tabernacle:

- **Kohathites** (south of the Tabernacle, 8,600) — Carried the most sacred furnishings: the Ark, the Table of Shewbread, the Menorah, the Altars, and the veil. Aaron and Moses were Kohathites. The priestly line descended exclusively through Aaron's sons within this clan.
- **Gershonites** (west of the Tabernacle, 7,500) — Responsible for the fabric of the sanctuary: the curtains, coverings, hangings of the court, and the screen for the gate.
- **Merarites** (north of the Tabernacle, 6,200) — Carried the structural framework: the boards, bars, pillars, sockets, and all the hardware of the Tabernacle and courtyard.

Moses, Aaron, and Aaron's sons camped on the **east** side — before the entrance — guarding the approach to the sanctuary (Numbers 3:38).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Levi's stone on the breastplate was *bareket* (בָּרֶקֶת). His flag bore three colors — one-third white, one-third black, one-third red — and depicted the Urim and Thummim, the instruments of divine revelation worn over the High Priest's heart.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Levi's role did not end with the ancient Tabernacle. In the latter-day restoration, the gathering of Israel requires the same functions Moses assigned to Levi: revelation (the Urim and Thummim restored through Joseph Smith), teaching the covenant (missionary work and gospel instruction), intercession (temple worship, where incense symbolizes prayers ascending to God), and sacrifice (the offering of a broken heart and contrite spirit). The Lord declared that "the sons of Levi" would again "offer unto the Lord an offering in righteousness" (D&C 13:1; Malachi 3:3). Every person who serves in the temple today performs a Levitical function — joining Israel to the covenant through sacred ordinance.

## Camp of Judah — East (186,400)

The eastern camp held the place of honor, facing the entrance of the Tabernacle. Judah led this camp, flanked by Issachar and Zebulun — all three sons of Leah. When Israel marched, the Camp of Judah moved first (Numbers 10:14). The east is the direction of sunrise, of the coming of light, and of the Messiah — the Lion of the Tribe of Judah.

---

### Judah — יְהוּדָה — "Praise"

#### The Brother

Judah was the fourth son of Jacob and Leah. At his birth, Leah declared, "Now will I praise the LORD" (Genesis 29:35). The name *Yehudah* (יְהוּדָה) contains the divine name — the root *yadah* (יָדָה), "to praise" or "to give thanks," wrapped around the letters of YHWH. Leah had moved beyond longing for her husband's love; with Judah, she turned wholly to praising God. That shift of heart became embedded in Judah's very name and in his tribe's mission.

Judah's story is marked by both failure and transformation. He proposed selling Joseph rather than killing him (Genesis 37:26–27) — an act that preserved Joseph's life but was still treacherous. He failed his daughter-in-law Tamar by withholding his son Shelah, and when confronted with evidence of his own hypocrisy, he declared, "She hath been more righteous than I" (Genesis 38:26). That moment of honest self-reckoning changed Judah. By the time of the famine in Egypt, it was Judah who offered himself as surety for Benjamin (Genesis 43:9) and who made the impassioned plea before Joseph, offering to become a slave in Benjamin's place (Genesis 44:33). Judah's arc is one of repentance and covenant leadership forged through accountability.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:8–12)

> "Judah, thou art he whom thy brethren shall praise: thy hand shall be in the neck of thine enemies; thy father's children shall bow down before thee. Judah is a lion's whelp: from the prey, my son, thou art gone up: he stooped down, he couched as a lion, and as an old lion; who shall rouse him up? The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be."

This is the most expansive and messianic of Jacob's blessings. Judah receives the lion as his emblem — courage, sovereignty, and protective strength. The "sceptre" establishes Judah as the royal tribe: Israel's kings would come from Judah. "Until Shiloh come" points to the Messiah — the ultimate ruler to whom the gathering of all people belongs. David, Solomon, and ultimately Jesus Christ all descended from this tribe.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:7)

> "Hear, LORD, the voice of Judah, and bring him unto his people: let his hands be sufficient for him; and be thou an help to him from his enemies."

Moses' blessing is notably brief for such a prominent tribe. It reads as a prayer that Judah be reunited with his brethren — a prophetic note that anticipates the division of the kingdom and the eventual scattering and regathering of Judah.

#### Camp Position and Census

Judah led the eastern camp with a census of **74,600** fighting men (Numbers 2:3–4). The eastern position placed Judah directly before the gate of the Tabernacle courtyard, the first camp to face the entrance where the presence of God dwelt.

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Judah's stone was *nofech* (נֹפֶךְ). His flag was the color of the heavens — sky blue — bearing the image of a lion (Genesis 49:9). The lion became the enduring symbol of the Davidic monarchy and, in Revelation, of Christ Himself: "the Lion of the tribe of Juda" (Revelation 5:5).

#### Historical Role

From Judah came the royal line of Israel. David was anointed king from Judah's descendants, and the promise of an eternal throne (2 Samuel 7:12–16) pointed through David's lineage to Jesus Christ. After the division of the kingdom (c. 930 BC), the southern kingdom retained the name "Judah" and included the tribes of Judah and Benjamin, along with many Levites. When the northern tribes were carried away by Assyria, Judah endured — and after the Babylonian exile, it was Judah that returned to rebuild Jerusalem and the temple. The word "Jew" (*Yehudi*) derives from Judah; the Jewish people who preserved Torah, temple worship, and covenant identity through millennia of exile carry the name and mission of this tribe.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

The gathering of Judah is one of the most visible prophetic fulfillments in modern history. The return of the Jewish people to the land of Israel, the preservation of Hebrew as a living language, and the survival of covenant identity through nearly two thousand years of dispersion all witness to Jacob's blessing that "the sceptre shall not depart from Judah." In Latter-day Saint understanding, the full gathering of Judah includes not only a physical return to the land but a future recognition of the Messiah — "and then shall the Jews look upon me and say: What are these wounds in thine hands and in thy feet?" (D&C 45:51). Judah's mission in the Gathering is to witness that God keeps His covenants across all generations.

### Issachar — יִשָּׂשכָר — "There Is a Reward" / "Man of Hire"

#### The Brother

Issachar was the ninth son of Jacob and the fifth son of Leah. His birth came after Leah gave her handmaid Zilpah to Jacob, and then Leah herself conceived again. At his birth she said, "God hath given me my hire, because I have given my maiden to my husband" (Genesis 30:18). The name *Yissakhar* (יִשָּׂשכָר) is typically understood from *sakhar* (שָׂכָר), meaning "wages," "reward," or "hire."

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:14–15)

> "Issachar is a strong ass couching down between two burdens: And he saw that rest was good, and the land that it was pleasant; and bowed his shoulder to bear, and became a servant unto tribute."

The image of a strong donkey resting between its loads speaks to Issachar's character: strength devoted to labor rather than to war. Issachar's territory in the fertile Jezreel Valley was among the richest agricultural land in Israel — pleasant and productive — and the tribe became known for steady, diligent service.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:18–19)

> "Rejoice, Zebulun, in thy going out; and, Issachar, in thy tents. They shall call the people unto the mountain; there they shall offer sacrifices of righteousness."

Moses pairs Issachar with Zebulun: Zebulun goes out (commerce and travel) while Issachar stays in the tents (study and teaching). Together they "call the people unto the mountain" — Issachar's contribution is spiritual leadership from a place of deep learning.

#### Camp Position and Census

Issachar camped in the eastern camp under Judah's standard with **54,400** men (Numbers 2:5–6).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Issachar's stone was sapphire (*sappir*, סַפִּיר). His flag was blackish blue (dark like the deep sky) bearing the image of the sun and moon, drawn from 1 Chronicles 12:32: "the children of Issachar, which were men that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do." Issachar understood the seasons — both calendrical and prophetic.

#### Historical Role

Issachar became known in Israel as the tribe of scholars and judges — "men that had understanding of the times." In Jewish tradition, Issachar represents Torah learning. Their partnership with Zebulun is one of the most celebrated in rabbinic thought: Zebulun engaged in commerce and financially supported Issachar's study, and both shared in the merit. This "Issachar-Zebulun partnership" became the model for the relationship between those who study and those who sustain the work of study through material support.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Issachar's mission — understanding the times and knowing what Israel ought to do — speaks directly to the work of the Gathering. In every dispensation, the Lord needs people who can read the signs, discern the seasons, and teach the covenant people what to do next. Scripture study, prophetic literacy, and gospel scholarship are Issachar's legacy. Those who devote themselves to understanding the word of God and helping others apply it carry forward the spirit of this tribe.

### Zebulun — זְבוּלֻן — "Dwelling" / "Honor"

#### The Brother

Zebulun was the tenth son of Jacob and the sixth and last son of Leah. At his birth, Leah said, "God hath endued me with a good dowry; now will my husband dwell with me, because I have born him six sons" (Genesis 30:20). The name *Zevulun* (זְבוּלֻן) is connected to *zaval* (זָבַל), meaning "to dwell" or "to honor." Leah hoped that bearing six sons would finally establish her honored place in Jacob's household.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:13)

> "Zebulun shall dwell at the haven of the sea; and he shall be for an haven of ships; and his border shall be unto Zidon."

Jacob places Zebulun at the coast — a tribe oriented outward, toward the sea and toward the nations. Zebulun's territory in the land of Israel stretched toward the Mediterranean trade routes, positioning the tribe at the crossroads of commerce and cultural exchange.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:18–19)

> "Rejoice, Zebulun, in thy going out; and, Issachar, in thy tents."

Zebulun "goes out" — into commerce, travel, and engagement with the wider world. Paired with Issachar's inward focus on study, the two represent a complete model: one sustains while the other studies, and together they "call the people unto the mountain."

#### Camp Position and Census

Zebulun camped in the eastern camp under Judah's standard with **57,400** men (Numbers 2:7–8).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Zebulun's stone was *yahalom* (יָהֲלֹם). His flag was white, bearing the image of a ship — the outward-facing merchant tribe whose livelihood came from the sea and trade.

#### Historical Role

Zebulun's territory, while not directly on the Mediterranean coast, sat along major trade routes connecting the interior of Israel to the coastal ports. This positioning made Zebulun a tribe of commerce and cultural interface. In the famous partnership with Issachar, Zebulun provided material wealth so that Issachar could devote itself to study. The tribe also produced courageous warriors — Judges 5:18 records that "Zebulun and Naphtali were a people that jeoparded their lives unto the death" in the battle against Sisera.

Zebulun's territory included the region that would later be called Galilee. Isaiah prophesied that "the land of Zabulon, and the land of Nephthalim" would see a great light (Isaiah 9:1–2) — a prophecy Matthew applies directly to Jesus beginning His ministry in Galilee (Matthew 4:13–16).

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Zebulun represents those who go out into the world to sustain the work of the covenant. Missionaries, providers, and those who use their resources and reach to bring the gospel to the nations carry the spirit of Zebulun. The tribe's connection to Galilee — where the Savior called His first disciples and launched His ministry — adds a layer of significance: the Gathering often begins in unexpected, peripheral places, carried by people willing to venture outward.

## Camp of Reuben — South (151,450)

The southern camp was led by Reuben, Jacob's firstborn, flanked by Simeon and Gad. All three mothers are represented here: Leah (Reuben, Simeon) and Zilpah (Gad). The Camp of Reuben marched second when Israel traveled (Numbers 10:18).

---

### Reuben — רְאוּבֵן — "Behold, a Son"

#### The Brother

Reuben was the firstborn of Jacob and Leah. At his birth, Leah said, "Surely the LORD hath looked upon my affliction; now therefore my husband will love me" (Genesis 29:32). The name *Re'uven* (רְאוּבֵן) is understood as "Behold, a son" — from *ra'ah* (רָאָה), "to see," and *ben* (בֵּן), "son." Leah's naming reflects both her pain and her faith: God *saw* her suffering and gave her a son.

Reuben showed flashes of compassion — he tried to rescue Joseph from the pit (Genesis 37:21–22) and later offered his own two sons as surety for Benjamin (Genesis 42:37). But his story is also marked by a critical failure: he "went and lay with Bilhah his father's concubine" (Genesis 35:22), an act that violated his father's household and ultimately cost him the birthright.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:3–4)

> "Reuben, thou art my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength, the excellency of dignity, and the excellency of power: Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel; because thou wentest up to thy father's bed; then defiledst thou it: he went up to my couch."

Jacob acknowledges what Reuben *should* have been — the firstborn, the one marked by dignity and power — but declares that his instability ("unstable as water") would prevent him from excelling. The birthright was divided: the double portion went to Joseph, the kingship to Judah, and the priesthood to Levi. Reuben retained the title of firstborn but lost the substance of it.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:6)

> "Let Reuben live, and not die; and let not his men be few."

Moses' blessing is the briefest of all — a simple prayer for survival. There is no special role, no unique gift. It reads as a plea that Reuben endure, even diminished.

#### Camp Position and Census

Reuben led the southern camp with **46,500** men (Numbers 2:10–11). The south position is significant — it is the direction of the Negev, the wilderness side, and in the camp layout Reuben was farthest from the Tabernacle entrance.

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Reuben's stone was *odem* (אֹדֶם) — a red stone. His flag was red, bearing the image of mandrakes (*duda'im*, דּוּדָאִים), recalling the episode in Genesis 30:14 when Reuben found mandrakes in the field and brought them to his mother Leah.

#### Historical Role

Reuben's tribe settled east of the Jordan (Numbers 32), along with Gad and half of Manasseh, in the rich pasturelands of the Transjordan. While they participated in the conquest of the land under Joshua, their separation from the main body of Israel by the Jordan River foreshadowed a pattern of distance. The tribe of Reuben never produced a significant judge, prophet, or king. In the Song of Deborah, Reuben is rebuked for staying among the sheepfolds instead of joining the battle: "For the divisions of Reuben there were great searchings of heart" (Judges 5:15–16). Reuben became an emblem of unfulfilled potential — greatness of position without greatness of action.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Reuben's story is a warning and a comfort. The warning: privilege of birth or position does not guarantee faithfulness. The comfort: even a diminished tribe is not abandoned. Moses prayed, "Let Reuben live, and not die." God preserves even those who have forfeited their highest blessings. In the Gathering, Reuben reminds us that covenant membership is sustained by grace, not by rank — and that it is never too late for a tribe (or a person) to answer the call they once neglected.

### Simeon — שִׁמְעוֹן — "Hearing" / "He Has Heard"

#### The Brother

Simeon was the second son of Jacob and Leah. At his birth, Leah said, "Because the LORD hath heard that I was hated, he hath therefore given me this son also" (Genesis 29:33). The name *Shimon* (שִׁמְעוֹן) comes from *shama* (שָׁמַע), "to hear." Like Reuben's name, Simeon's speaks to Leah's pain and to God's attentiveness to the unloved.

Simeon is paired with Levi in the slaughter at Shechem (Genesis 34) and shares in Jacob's rebuke. Unlike Levi, however, Simeon's tribe did not find a redemptive transformation for that fierce energy.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:5–7)

Simeon shares Levi's blessing — or rather, Levi's censure (see Levi above). Both are told they will be "divided in Jacob and scattered in Israel." For Levi, this became a priestly distribution. For Simeon, it became absorption. Simeon's tribal territory was an enclave *within* Judah's territory (Joshua 19:1–9), and over time the tribe was largely absorbed into Judah.

#### Moses' Blessing

Simeon is the only tribe that receives **no individual blessing** from Moses in Deuteronomy 33. The silence is conspicuous. Some scholars note that Simeon may be implicitly included under Judah's blessing, given that Simeon's territory lay within Judah's borders. Others see the omission as a reflection of the tribe's diminished standing — their census dropped dramatically from 59,300 (Numbers 1:23) to 22,200 (Numbers 26:14) between the first and second wilderness censuses, the largest decline of any tribe.

#### Camp Position and Census

Simeon camped in the southern camp under Reuben's standard with **59,300** men at the first census (Numbers 2:12–13).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Simeon's stone was *piteda* (פִּטְדָה). His flag was green, bearing the image of the city of Shechem — the place of Simeon's defining act with Levi, permanently marking the tribe's banner with that memory.

#### Historical Role

Simeon's inheritance was absorbed into Judah's territory, and the tribe's distinct identity faded earlier than most. Some of Simeon's descendants migrated to other areas (1 Chronicles 4:38–43), but the tribe did not produce notable leaders or distinct institutions in Israel's history. Simeon stands as the starkest example in the tribal narrative of how a patriarch's choices could shape a people's trajectory for generations.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Simeon's name means "hearing" — God heard Leah's affliction. Even a tribe whose identity was absorbed and whose blessing was passed over in silence was *heard* by God at its origin. In the Gathering, Simeon's story speaks to those who feel invisible, overlooked, or swallowed up by larger forces. The God who heard Leah's tears does not forget His covenant with any of Israel's children, no matter how scattered or diminished they appear.

### Gad — גָּד — "Fortune" / "A Troop"

#### The Brother

Gad was the seventh son of Jacob, the first son of Zilpah, Leah's handmaid. At his birth, Leah said, "A troop cometh" (Genesis 30:11), or in some readings, "Good fortune!" The name *Gad* (גָּד) carries the dual sense of *fortune* and *troop* or *raiding band* — both meanings shape the tribe's identity.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:19)

> "Gad, a troop shall overcome him: but he shall overcome at the last."

The Hebrew wordplay is striking — *Gad gedud yegudennu, vehu yagud aqev* — stacking the root *g-d-d* (גדד) five times in a single verse. Gad will be pressed, raided, and overcome, but will rally and pursue, overcoming in the end. This is a warrior's blessing, a promise of resilience under repeated assault.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:20–21)

> "Blessed be he that enlargeth Gad: he dwelleth as a lion, and teareth the arm with the crown of the head. And he provided the first part for himself, because there, in a portion of the lawgiver, was he seated; and he came with the heads of the people, he executed the justice of the LORD, and his judgments with Israel."

Moses expands Gad's identity beyond raiding: Gad dwells "as a lion" (military strength), claims the first portion east of the Jordan, and yet faithfully crosses over to fight with his brothers. The blessing honors both Gad's warrior courage and his covenant loyalty.

#### Camp Position and Census

Gad camped in the southern camp under Reuben's standard with **45,650** men (Numbers 2:14–15).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Gad's stone was *shevo* (שְׁבוֹ). His flag was a mixture of black and white (grey), bearing the image of a military camp — a tent encampment of soldiers, reflecting the tribe's warrior identity.

#### Historical Role

Gad settled east of the Jordan in the region of Gilead (Numbers 32), prime territory that was also exposed to constant raids from Ammonites, Moabites, and Arameans. True to Jacob's blessing, Gad endured repeated incursions but fought back fiercely. The Gadites were celebrated warriors — 1 Chronicles 12:8 describes Gadite soldiers who came to David as men "whose faces were like the faces of lions, and were as swift as the roes upon the mountains." The prophet Elijah is traditionally associated with Gilead, Gad's territory.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Gad embodies the promise that those who are overcome will overcome at the last. In the Gathering of Israel — a work that spans centuries of scattering, persecution, and loss — Gad's blessing is a covenant of final victory. The troops may be pressed, the raiding may be relentless, but the end is triumph. For those who feel battered by opposition in covenant life, Gad's banner promises that endurance will prevail.

## Camp of Ephraim — West (108,100)

The western camp was led by Ephraim, flanked by Manasseh and Benjamin — all descendants of Rachel. This is Rachel's camp, and it holds a special place in the story of the Gathering. The Camp of Ephraim marched third (Numbers 10:22).

---

### Ephraim — אֶפְרַיִם — "Doubly Fruitful"

#### The Brother

Ephraim was not a son of Jacob but a *grandson* — the younger son of Joseph, born in Egypt to Joseph and Asenath (Genesis 41:52). Joseph named him Ephraim, saying, "God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction." The name *Ephrayim* (אֶפְרַיִם) comes from the root *parah* (פָּרָה), "to be fruitful," in a dual form suggesting "double fruitfulness."

When Jacob blessed Joseph's sons, he deliberately crossed his hands and placed his right hand (the hand of the greater blessing) on Ephraim, the younger, over Joseph's objection (Genesis 48:13–20). Jacob declared, "his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his seed shall become a multitude of nations" — literally, *melo hagoyim* (מְלֹא הַגּוֹיִם), "the fullness of the nations."

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 48:19–20; 49:22–26)

Ephraim receives his blessing through Joseph's blessing:

> "Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall: The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him: But his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob … The blessings of thy father have prevailed above the blessings of my progenitors unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills: they shall be on the head of Joseph, and on the crown of the head of him that was separate from his brethren."

Joseph — and through him Ephraim — receives the most expansive territorial and spiritual blessing: fruitfulness that extends "over the wall" and blessings that reach to "the utmost bound of the everlasting hills." Latter-day Saints have long understood this as pointing to the Americas and to the covenant people who would be established in the mountains of the West.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:13–17)

> "Blessed of the LORD be his land, for the precious things of heaven, for the dew, and for the deep that coucheth beneath … for the precious things of the earth and fulness thereof, and for the good will of him that dwelt in the bush … His glory is like the firstling of his bullock, and his horns are like the horns of unicorns: with them he shall push the people together to the ends of the earth: and they are the ten thousands of Ephraim, and they are the thousands of Manasseh."

Moses confirms Ephraim's preeminence over Manasseh ("ten thousands" versus "thousands") and assigns the role of pushing — gathering — the people together to the ends of the earth.

#### Camp Position and Census

Ephraim led the western camp with **40,500** men (Numbers 2:18–19). Though the smallest of the three tribes in this camp, Ephraim held the leadership position — the younger over the older, exactly as Jacob had ordained.

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Ephraim's stone was *shoham* (שֹׁהַם). His flag was deep black, bearing the image of a bull (ox), drawn from the blessing "the firstling of his bullock" (Deuteronomy 33:17), which alludes to Joshua, who was from the tribe of Ephraim.

#### Historical Role

Ephraim became the dominant tribe of the northern kingdom. After the division under Rehoboam, the northern kingdom of Israel was often simply called "Ephraim" by the prophets (Hosea 4:17; Isaiah 7:2). Joshua, the successor to Moses who led Israel into the Promised Land, was an Ephraimite. The Tabernacle was set up at Shiloh, in Ephraim's territory, where it remained for centuries before the temple was built in Jerusalem.

When the northern kingdom fell to Assyria in 722 BC, the ten tribes — led by Ephraim — were scattered among the nations. They became the "lost tribes," seemingly absorbed into the peoples of the earth. Yet the prophets consistently promised their return: "I am a father to Israel, and Ephraim is my firstborn" (Jeremiah 31:9).

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Ephraim stands at the center of the Gathering of Israel in Latter-day Saint theology. The Book of Mormon identifies itself as coming through Joseph's lineage (2 Nephi 3:12; title page). Joseph Smith, through whom the restoration was accomplished, was of Ephraim. Patriarchal blessings overwhelmingly declare members of the Church as belonging to Ephraim — the tribe charged to "push the people together to the ends of the earth." Ephraim's mission is the missionary and covenant-gathering work of the last days: reaching into every nation, kindred, tongue, and people to bring God's children home. Jacob's blessing that Ephraim's seed would become *melo hagoyim* — "the fullness of the nations" — is understood as a prophecy that Ephraim's descendants would be scattered *among* all nations precisely so they could gather *from* all nations when the time of restoration came.

---

### Manasseh — מְנַשֶּׁה — "Causing to Forget"

#### The Brother

Manasseh was the firstborn son of Joseph, born in Egypt before the famine. Joseph named him Manasseh, saying, "God hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house" (Genesis 41:51). The name *Menasheh* (מְנַשֶּׁה) comes from *nashah* (נָשָׁה), "to forget." It speaks of God's healing — allowing Joseph to move forward from the trauma of betrayal and slavery without being consumed by it.

Despite being the firstborn, Manasseh received the lesser blessing when Jacob crossed his hands (Genesis 48). Joseph was troubled, but Jacob was deliberate: "I know it, my son, I know it" (Genesis 48:19). Manasseh would be great, but Ephraim would be greater.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 48:19; 49:22–26)

Manasseh shares in Joseph's overarching blessing of fruitfulness and strength. Jacob specifically promises that Manasseh "also shall become a people, and he also shall be great" — not diminished, but positioned differently from his younger brother.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:13–17)

Manasseh is included in Joseph's blessing and named specifically: "they are the thousands of Manasseh." The "horns of unicorns" (*re'em*, רְאֵם — a wild ox) that push the people together belong to both Ephraim and Manasseh, though Ephraim receives the "ten thousands."

#### Camp Position and Census

Manasseh camped in the western camp under Ephraim's standard with **32,200** men (Numbers 2:20–21).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Manasseh shared the deep black flag of Joseph's house. His specific emblem was a wild ox (*re'em*), drawn from the blessing "his horns are like the horns of a *re'em*" (Deuteronomy 33:17), alluding to Gideon son of Joash, the great judge who was from the tribe of Manasseh (Judges 6–8).

#### Historical Role

Manasseh was unique among the tribes in receiving a *split* inheritance — half the tribe settled east of the Jordan in Bashan and Gilead (some of the richest land in the region), while the other half settled west of the Jordan (Joshua 17). This division made Manasseh simultaneously an eastern and western tribe, stretching across the Jordan and bridging the two halves of Israel. Gideon, one of Israel's most celebrated judges, came from Manasseh and demonstrated that the tribe's strength lay in faithfully following God's direction, even with reduced numbers (Judges 7).

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Manasseh's split inheritance — half on each side of the Jordan — prefigures a people stretched across boundaries, present in multiple places at once. In the Gathering, Manasseh represents those scattered among many nations who retain their covenant identity even when separated from the main body. The name "Causing to Forget" also carries a healing promise: God enables His covenant people to move beyond the wounds of exile and scattering without forgetting who they are. Manasseh's partnership with Ephraim in the latter-day work means that the Gathering is not a solo mission — Ephraim leads, but Manasseh labors alongside as a great people in their own right.

---

### Benjamin — בִּנְיָמִין — "Son of the Right Hand"

#### The Brother

Benjamin was the twelfth and last son of Jacob — the only son born in the land of Canaan — and the second son of Rachel. His birth cost Rachel her life. As she was dying, she named him *Ben-Oni* (בֶּן אוֹנִי), "Son of My Sorrow." But Jacob renamed him *Binyamin* (בִּנְיָמִין), "Son of the Right Hand" (Genesis 35:18). In that renaming, Jacob transformed a name of grief into a name of honor and strength. The right hand is the hand of power, covenant, and blessing.

Benjamin was the youngest, the beloved, and the one Jacob could not bear to lose after Joseph's presumed death. The drama of Joseph's final test in Egypt revolves entirely around Benjamin — Judah's willingness to sacrifice himself for Benjamin is the moment that breaks Joseph's composure and triggers the great reunion (Genesis 44–45).

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:27)

> "Benjamin shall ravin as a wolf: in the morning he shall devour the prey, and at night he shall divide the spoil."

The image is fierce — a wolf, aggressive and effective. Benjamin is small but formidable. "In the morning" and "at night" suggest unceasing vigilance and action. This smallest tribe would produce some of Israel's fiercest warriors and most consequential leaders.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:12)

> "The beloved of the LORD shall dwell in safety by him; and the LORD shall cover him all the day long, and he shall dwell between his shoulders."

Moses' blessing is remarkably tender for the wolf-tribe. Benjamin is called "the beloved of the LORD" and promised safety and divine protection. "He shall dwell between his shoulders" is understood as a reference to the Temple — the Temple in Jerusalem was built on the border between Judah and Benjamin, with the Temple Mount itself in Benjamin's territory according to many traditions.

#### Camp Position and Census

Benjamin camped in the western camp under Ephraim's standard with **35,400** men (Numbers 2:22–23).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Benjamin's stone was *yashfeh* (יָשְׁפֶה). His flag was unique — it contained all twelve colors, representing Benjamin's connection to all the tribes. It bore the image of a wolf (Genesis 49:27).

#### Historical Role

Benjamin's territory was small but strategically vital — it included Gibeah (Saul's capital), Gibeon, and the border region where Jerusalem sits. Israel's first king, Saul, was a Benjaminite. After the kingdom divided, Benjamin remained with Judah in the southern kingdom, the only tribe besides Judah (and portions of Levi) to maintain an unbroken connection to Jerusalem and the temple. The apostle Paul identified himself as "of the tribe of Benjamin" (Philippians 3:5; Romans 11:1), demonstrating that Benjaminite identity persisted well into the first century.

The tribe also experienced one of the darkest episodes in Israel's history — the near-extermination of Benjamin after the atrocity at Gibeah (Judges 19–21). The other tribes swore not to give their daughters to Benjamin, then wept when they realized they had nearly destroyed an entire tribe. The rescue of Benjamin through creative covenant-keeping foreshadows the broader theme of Israel's preservation through crisis.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Benjamin's banner — the only one carrying all twelve colors — marks this tribe as a unifier. Benjamin dwells between Judah and Joseph (Ephraim), the two great houses of Israel, and bridges them. In the Gathering, Benjamin represents the connection between the Jewish world (Judah) and the restored covenant community (Ephraim). Paul, a Benjaminite, carried the gospel to the Gentile nations, prefiguring the work of bringing all of Israel's scattered children into one fold. Moses' promise that Benjamin is "the beloved of the LORD" who "shall dwell in safety" between God's shoulders is ultimately a promise about the Temple — the place where all Israel comes together in the presence of God.

## Camp of Dan — North (157,600)

The northern camp was the largest, led by Dan and joined by Asher and Naphtali — all sons of the handmaids (Dan and Naphtali from Bilhah, Asher from Zilpah). The Camp of Dan marched last, serving as the rear guard for all Israel (Numbers 10:25).

---

### Dan — דָּן — "Judge"

#### The Brother

Dan was the fifth son of Jacob and the first son of Bilhah, Rachel's handmaid. Rachel, unable to conceive, gave Bilhah to Jacob so that she could "have children through her." When Dan was born, Rachel said, "God hath judged me, and hath also heard my voice, and hath given me a son" (Genesis 30:6). The name *Dan* (דָּן) comes from the root *din* (דִּין), "to judge." Dan's very existence was Rachel's vindication — God had judged her cause and answered.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:16–18)

> "Dan shall judge his people, as one of the tribes of Israel. Dan shall be a serpent by the way, an adder in the path, that biteth the horse heels, so that his rider shall fall backward. I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD."

Dan receives the role of judge — he will ensure justice for his people. But the serpent image is ambiguous: it can mean cunning, strategic warfare (striking from a hidden position to topple a larger enemy), but it also carries an unsettling edge. Jacob's sudden exclamation — "I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD" — immediately after the serpent imagery has prompted interpreters across traditions to see a tension in Dan's future: the tribe capable of great deliverance but also susceptible to spiritual danger.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:22)

> "Dan is a lion's whelp: he shall leap from Bashan."

Moses replaces the serpent with a lion's whelp — young, vigorous, leaping. This is a more straightforwardly positive image: energy, courage, and initiative springing from a position of strength (Bashan was rich, elevated land).

#### Camp Position and Census

Dan led the northern camp with **62,700** men (Numbers 2:25–26) — the second-largest individual tribe after Judah.

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Dan's stone was *leshem* (לֶשֶׁם). His flag was sapphire blue, bearing the image of a serpent, drawn from Jacob's blessing: "Dan shall be a serpent by the way" (Genesis 49:17).

#### Historical Role

Dan's tribal territory was originally in the southwest, near the Philistine coast (Joshua 19:40–48), but the tribe struggled to hold it. Judges 18 records that the Danites migrated north and conquered the city of Laish, renaming it Dan. The city of Dan became the northernmost point of Israel — "from Dan to Beersheba" became the standard phrase for the entire land.

Dan produced one of Israel's most famous figures: Samson (Judges 13:2), whose story embodies the tribe's paradox — extraordinary strength and divine calling intertwined with personal compromise. After the kingdom divided, Jeroboam set up one of his golden calves at Dan (1 Kings 12:29), making the city a center of idolatry. This dark association has led some traditions to view Dan with suspicion, though the tribe is never formally rejected from Israel's covenant.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Dan's role as judge and rear guard speaks to God's justice in the Gathering — no one is left behind. The rear guard is the camp that ensures the stragglers, the slow, and the vulnerable are not abandoned on the march. Dan's complicated history — Samson's greatness alongside the golden calf at Dan — reflects the reality that covenant faithfulness is not a straight line for any people. The Gathering includes those whose story is messy, whose tribe has stumbled, and who nevertheless belong to Israel. Jacob's cry, "I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD," embedded in Dan's blessing, is ultimately a messianic hope: salvation comes not from human consistency but from God's faithfulness.

---

### Asher — אָשֵׁר — "Happy" / "Blessed"

#### The Brother

Asher was the eighth son of Jacob and the second son of Zilpah, Leah's handmaid. At his birth, Leah exclaimed, "Happy am I, for the daughters will call me blessed" (Genesis 30:13). The name *Asher* (אָשֵׁר) means "happy" or "blessed" — from the root *ashar* (אָשַׁר), "to be straight, to go forward, to be blessed."

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:20)

> "Out of Asher his bread shall be fat, and he shall yield royal dainties."

Asher's blessing is abundance and provision. "Fat bread" and "royal dainties" speak of a territory so rich it supplies the tables of kings. This is not a warrior's blessing or a ruler's blessing — it is the blessing of the provider, the one whose land overflows.

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:24–25)

> "Let Asher be blessed with children; let him be acceptable to his brethren, and let him dip his foot in oil. Thy shoes shall be iron and brass; and as thy days, so shall thy strength be."

Moses amplifies the blessing: Asher will be fruitful in children, favored among his brothers, so abundant in olive oil that he "dips his foot" in it. The final phrase — "as thy days, so shall thy strength be" — is one of the most beloved promises in all of Deuteronomy. Asher's strength will match every demand placed upon it, enduring as long as needed.

#### Camp Position and Census

Asher camped in the northern camp under Dan's standard with **41,500** men (Numbers 2:27–28).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Asher's stone was *tarshish* (תַּרְשִׁישׁ). His flag was pearlescent (the color of a precious stone used in women's ornaments), bearing the image of an olive tree — the source of Asher's legendary oil.

#### Historical Role

Asher's territory stretched along the fertile northwestern coast of Israel, including the rich agricultural lands of the western Galilee. The region was known for olive production — consistent with both Jacob's and Moses' blessings. In the Judges period, Asher is rebuked for failing to drive out the Canaanites from coastal cities like Acco and Sidon (Judges 1:31–32), though the tribe remained part of Israel. Anna the prophetess, who recognized the infant Jesus at the Temple, was "of the tribe of Aser" (Luke 2:36) — a remarkable detail showing that tribal identity and faithfulness persisted even in tribes often overlooked.

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Asher's mission is provision and sustenance. In the Gathering, the work requires not only those who preach and lead but those who nourish — who provide the material, emotional, and spiritual abundance that sustains the covenant community. "As thy days, so shall thy strength be" is a promise for every season of the Gathering: the strength will always be sufficient for the work. Anna of Asher, who spent decades in faithful temple service and was rewarded with witnessing the Messiah, embodies the patient, sustaining faith that Asher represents — blessed happiness rooted in long obedience.

### Naphtali — נַפְתָּלִי — "My Wrestling"

#### The Brother

Naphtali was the sixth son of Jacob and the second son of Bilhah, Rachel's handmaid. At his birth, Rachel said, "With great wrestlings have I wrestled with my sister, and I have prevailed" (Genesis 30:8). The name *Naphtali* (נַפְתָּלִי) comes from the root *patal* (פָּתַל), "to twist" or "to wrestle." The name reflects Rachel's struggle with Leah — a deeply personal contest that nevertheless produced a tribe whose character would be defined by swift, graceful movement rather than heavy combat.

#### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:21)

> "Naphtali is a hind let loose: he giveth goodly words."

A hind (*ayalah*, אַיָּלָה) is a female deer — swift, graceful, and free. "Let loose" suggests release from constraint, running freely. "Goodly words" (*imrei shafer*, אִמְרֵי שָׁפֶר) — beautiful speech, eloquent language. Naphtali is the tribe of swift messengers bearing beautiful tidings. The image resonates with Isaiah 52:7: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings."

#### Moses' Blessing (Deuteronomy 33:23)

> "O Naphtali, satisfied with favour, and full with the blessing of the LORD: possess thou the west and the south."

Naphtali is "satisfied with favour" — a tribe that knows divine goodness. The territory described ("the west and the south" of the Sea of Galilee) encompasses some of the most beautiful land in Israel, including the shores of the Kinneret (Sea of Galilee).

#### Camp Position and Census

Naphtali camped in the northern camp under Dan's standard with **53,400** men (Numbers 2:29–30).

#### The Banner (Midrash Rabbah)

Naphtali's stone was *achlama* (אַחְלָמָה) — amethyst. His flag was a pale wine color (diluted red), bearing the image of a deer (hind), reflecting Jacob's blessing of the swift and graceful messenger.

#### Historical Role

Naphtali's territory encompassed the eastern upper Galilee and the western shore of the Sea of Galilee — the region that would later become the heartland of Jesus' ministry. Capernaum, Bethsaida, Chorazin, and much of the landscape of the Gospels lay in Naphtali's inheritance. Isaiah's prophecy of a great light shining in "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah 9:1–2) was fulfilled in Naphtali's territory.

In the Judges period, Naphtali joined Zebulun in the great battle against Sisera, and both tribes are praised for risking their lives (Judges 5:18). Barak, the military commander who worked alongside the prophetess Deborah, was from Naphtali (Judges 4:6).

#### Gathering of Israel Significance

Naphtali is the swift messenger with beautiful words. In the Gathering of Israel, the gospel must move quickly and be proclaimed eloquently — the "hind let loose" carries tidings of redemption to people who have been waiting to hear. That Jesus chose to base His ministry in Naphtali's territory — fulfilling Isaiah's prophecy of light breaking into darkness — underscores this tribe's role: the place from which the good news goes forth. Every missionary, teacher, and bearer of testimony who carries the covenant message with beauty and urgency walks in Naphtali's legacy.

## The Camp as a Covenant Portrait

The arrangement of the tribes around the Tabernacle is not merely organizational — it is theological. Each tribe carries a piece of Israel's collective mission:

| Camp | Direction | Lead Tribe | Mission Theme |
|------|-----------|------------|---------------|
| **Judah** | East | Judah | Kingship, praise, covenant leadership — facing the entrance, first to march |
| **Reuben** | South | Reuben | Repentance, endurance, preservation by grace |
| **Ephraim** | West | Ephraim | Gathering, fruitfulness, restoration — the covenant reaches to the ends of the earth |
| **Dan** | North | Dan | Justice, rear guard, no one left behind |
| **Levi** | Center | (no single lead) | Priesthood, teaching, ordinance — joining Israel to God |

Together, the camp forms a living image of the Gathering itself: a people organized around the presence of God, each tribe contributing its unique gift to the whole, marching through the wilderness toward the promised land. The Tabernacle at the center is the point — everything in the camp exists in relation to the dwelling place of God.

In the latter days, the Gathering of Israel reassembles this portrait: Judah returns to the land and preserves Torah. Ephraim leads the missionary work and temple-building. Levi's priestly functions are restored through the ordinances of the temple. And every tribe — known or unknown, remembered or scattered — has a place in the camp because God keeps His covenant with all of Israel's children.

> "And he shall set up an ensign for the nations, and shall assemble the outcasts of Israel, and gather together the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth." — Isaiah 11:12
