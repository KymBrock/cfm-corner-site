<h2 style="text-align: center;">The Great Cities</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">&ldquo;Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh&hellip;&rdquo;</p>

<p>Assyria did not have a capital so much as a succession of them &mdash; and each move was a sermon in stone. Where Babylon poured its whole imagination into one city on the Euphrates, the Assyrian kings built and abandoned capitals up and down the Tigris, each new one a fresh king&rsquo;s statement that the empire had outgrown the last. Ashur, Kalhu, Dur-Sharrukin, Nineveh: four cities, four reigns, one relentless upward reach. To walk them in order is to watch a kingdom talk to itself about power &mdash; and to arrive, at the end, at the city the Bible knew best.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Ashur: The God&rsquo;s Own City</h3>

<p>The empire took its name from a city, and the city from a god &mdash; all three, in Assyrian, are the single word <em>A&scaron;&scaron;ur</em>. On a rocky spur above the Tigris stood the oldest of the capitals, a settlement already ancient when the others were open fields. Ashur was never chiefly a fortress or an administrative seat; it was a <strong>holy city</strong>, the earthly home of the national god, and for that reason it kept a hold on the Assyrian conscience long after the court had moved elsewhere. Its great temple of Ashur and its ziggurats anchored the skyline; nearby rose the temple of Anu and Adad with its twin towers, and the sanctuary of Ishtar, whose earliest levels reach back into the third millennium BC.</p>

<p>Above all, Ashur was where the kings came home to be buried. Beneath the Old Palace, German excavators under Walter Andrae (1903&ndash;1914) found the <strong>vaulted royal tombs</strong> &mdash; the resting place of Assyria&rsquo;s monarchs, who might reign from a glittering new capital but were laid at last in the city of the god who had made them kings. Even when Kalhu and then Nineveh eclipsed it in size and splendor, Ashur remained the coronation and burial heart of the realm &mdash; the place an Assyrian king could never quite leave, because it was where his legitimacy came from. The Bible remembers the name in the person of &ldquo;Asshur&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/10/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 10:11">Genesis 10:11</a>) &mdash; at once the ancestor, the land, and the god-city from which the northern power took its identity.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Kalhu (Biblical Calah): The City of the Winged Bulls</h3>

<p>In the ninth century BC, <strong>Ashurnasirpal II</strong> (r. 883&ndash;859 BC) did what Assyrian kings would do again and again: he refused to rule from his predecessors&rsquo; city and built his own. He chose <strong>Kalhu</strong> &mdash; the <strong>Calah</strong> of Genesis, modern <strong>Nimrud</strong> &mdash; and turned a provincial town into a capital fit for a world power. Scripture names it in the Table of Nations, in the very verses that catalogue Assyria&rsquo;s founding cities: Nineveh, &ldquo;the city Rehoboth, and Calah, and Resen between Nineveh and Calah: the same is a great city&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/10/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 10:11-12">Genesis 10:11&ndash;12</a>).</p>

<p>The centerpiece was the <strong>Northwest Palace</strong>, and it is one of the reasons the modern world knows Assyria at all. Its state apartments were lined, room after room, with carved <strong>alabaster relief cycles</strong> &mdash; slab upon slab of the king at war, at the hunt, and before the gods, the figures still crisp, many originally painted, several carrying the &ldquo;Standard Inscription&rdquo; of Ashurnasirpal running straight across the images. When Austen Henry Layard began digging here in the 1840s and shipped these reliefs to London, they reshaped Europe&rsquo;s picture of the biblical world overnight (the excavations are the story of <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/10-major-sites-and-discoveries/">Section 10</a>). At the palace and temple gateways stood the guardians that have become the face of Assyria: the <strong>lamassu</strong>.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f2f0ec 0%, #e6e1d8 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>What is a lamassu?</strong> The colossal figures flanking Assyrian gates are <em>lamassu</em> (also <em>&scaron;&#275;du</em>) &mdash; protective spirits carved as a composite of the empire&rsquo;s ideals: the <strong>body of a bull or lion</strong> (strength), the <strong>wings of an eagle</strong> (swiftness and the divine), and the <strong>bearded head of a man</strong> in the royal horned crown (wisdom and sovereignty). Cut in high relief from single blocks weighing many tons, they were given <strong>five legs</strong> &mdash; four when viewed head-on standing still, four when viewed from the side striding forward &mdash; so that from every angle the guardian is complete. They stood at thresholds to bless those who entered rightly and to terrify those who did not: stone doorkeepers of an empire that wanted every visitor to feel small.</p>
</div>

<p>Kalhu gave up another treasure that has become legendary among archaeologists: the <strong>Nimrud ivories</strong>. Thousands of delicately carved ivory panels &mdash; furniture inlays, plaques, and fittings, many in Phoenician and Syrian styles, gathered as tribute and booty from across the Levant &mdash; were found in the palace and storerooms, some tumbled into a well where the damp had preserved them. They are among the finest small-scale art to survive from the ancient Near East, and a vivid witness to how wealth flowed from the conquered west into the Assyrian heartland.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f0ece8 0%, #e2d8d0 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>A modern loss:</strong> in 2015 the archaeological site of Nimrud was deliberately destroyed &mdash; palace reliefs and lamassu that had stood for nearly three thousand years were demolished. Much survives in the world&rsquo;s museums, and painstaking documentation and reconstruction work continues; but the ground itself, where Ashurnasirpal&rsquo;s guardians once flanked their gate, suffered a real and irreversible wound. It is worth naming plainly, without spectacle: some of what this page describes can now be seen only in London, Baghdad, and the photographic record.</p>
</div>

<br>

<h3>Dur-Sharrukin (Khorsabad): A City Built and Abandoned</h3>

<p>The most extravagant capital was the one that barely lived. <strong>Sargon II</strong> (r. 721&ndash;705 BC) &mdash; the king who deported the northern kingdom of Israel &mdash; declined to inherit a city at all. On empty ground northeast of Nineveh he ordered an entire capital raised from nothing and named it for himself: <strong>Dur-Sharrukin</strong>, &ldquo;Fortress of Sargon&rdquo; (modern <strong>Khorsabad</strong>). A perfect walled square nearly two kilometers on a side went up in roughly a decade, with a palace on a raised terrace, temples, a ziggurat, and lamassu-guarded gates &mdash; the whole thing planned at once, paid for by the plunder of an empire at its height.</p>

<p>Sargon dedicated his city and moved in around 706 BC. The next year he was killed on campaign, and &mdash; catastrophically, in Assyrian eyes &mdash; his body was never recovered, an omen of divine displeasure so grave that his son read it as a verdict on the whole project. The court promptly abandoned Dur-Sharrukin and shifted the capital to Nineveh. The gleaming new city, scarcely broken in, was left to the wind. It is one of history&rsquo;s cleaner parables of imperial vanity: a monarch who built a metropolis to outlast the ages and did not survive to keep a single New Year in it. (The Khorsabad excavations, and the famous loss of a colossal lamassu in the Tigris, belong to <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/10-major-sites-and-discoveries/">Section 10</a>.)</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Nineveh: The Palace Without Rival</h3>

<p>And so to the last and greatest of them &mdash; the city whose name the Bible speaks more than any other Assyrian place. When <strong>Sennacherib</strong> (r. 705&ndash;681 BC) turned from his father&rsquo;s abandoned dream, he chose an old Tigris city with deep roots and made it the capital of the world. Nineveh under Sennacherib swelled to enclose roughly <strong>seven square kilometers</strong> behind a great wall pierced by fifteen named gates, with a population that may have approached the largest the world had yet seen. Its two mounds &mdash; <strong>Kuyunjik</strong> and <strong>Nebi Yunus</strong> (&ldquo;the Prophet Jonah&rdquo;) &mdash; still rise above the modern city of Mosul.</p>

<p>At its heart Sennacherib built what he himself called, without modesty, the <strong>&ldquo;Palace Without Rival.&rdquo;</strong> Its reliefs raised Assyrian carving to its peak: sprawling narrative panels of siege engines, marching armies, and captive peoples, executed with a documentary precision unmatched in the ancient world. The most famous of them shows the fall of a Judahite city &mdash; the <strong>Lachish reliefs</strong> &mdash; a scene we can read against the pages of Kings and Isaiah (the whole confrontation is the subject of <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/08-sennacherib-and-hezekiah/">Section 08</a>). It was in another wing of this same royal complex &mdash; and in the palace of his grandson Ashurbanipal &mdash; that the tens of thousands of cuneiform tablets of the great royal library were stored, the single richest hoard of Mesopotamian literature ever found (see <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/05-writing-and-the-great-library/">Section 05</a>).</p>

<h4>The Water Empire</h4>

<p>A city of that size on the Tigris floodplain faced a hard problem &mdash; enough water, reliably delivered, for a huge population and for the parks and orchards a great king was expected to plant. Sennacherib answered it with engineering that still astonishes. He built a network of <strong>canals</strong> drawing water from the hills to the north, and to carry one of them across a valley he raised the <strong>Jerwan aqueduct</strong> &mdash; a stone-built water bridge some hundreds of meters long, made of roughly two million dressed blocks and lined to hold the flow. It is among the earliest monumental aqueducts known anywhere, and it bore Sennacherib&rsquo;s own confident inscription. The king who could carry a river over a valley did not stop at defenses; he watered a paradise.</p>

<h4>The Gardens &mdash; and a Wonder in the Wrong City?</h4>

<p>Sennacherib&rsquo;s inscriptions describe a great <strong>royal garden</strong> beside his palace at Nineveh &mdash; a &ldquo;wonder for all peoples,&rdquo; planted with trees gathered from across the empire, laid out on raised terraces, and watered by a mechanism the king says he cast in bronze to lift water continually to the height of the plantings. It is a striking picture: terraced greenery rising in tiers above a Mesopotamian capital, fed by hidden hydraulic machinery.</p>

<p>That picture may sound familiar for the wrong reason. The classical world remembered the <strong>Hanging Gardens</strong> as one of the Seven Wonders &mdash; and attached them to <em>Babylon</em>. Yet the abundant Babylonian records never mention such gardens, and no site has been agreed upon there. The Assyriologist <strong>Stephanie Dalley</strong> has argued, in a detailed and much-discussed case, that the wonder was real but <strong>misfiled</strong>: that the terraced, machine-watered garden of the Greek tradition is in fact Sennacherib&rsquo;s garden at <strong>Nineveh</strong>, whose fame drifted over the centuries onto the more familiar name of Babylon &mdash; the two cities and empires easily confused by later writers. Her reading marshals Sennacherib&rsquo;s own texts, the water engineering that could actually have supplied such a garden, and a relief that appears to show terraced plantings watered from above.</p>

<p>It is a fascinating thesis &mdash; and it is <strong>genuinely contested</strong>. Other specialists are unpersuaded, holding to a Babylonian location or judging the classical accounts too tangled to pin down; the debate is live and unresolved. As with the gardens in the Babylon guide, honesty asks us to present this as an <strong>open question</strong> rather than a settled discovery: a serious, well-argued hypothesis that has not won the field. What is not in doubt is that Sennacherib built <em>a</em> spectacular terraced garden at Nineveh; whether it is <em>the</em> Hanging Gardens is exactly the kind of question the tablet and the spade keep the right to reopen.</p>

<h4>Nineveh in Scripture</h4>

<p>No Assyrian city looms larger in the Bible. Its founding stands at the head of the Table of Nations: &ldquo;Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/10/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 10:11-12">Genesis 10:11&ndash;12</a>). Centuries later it is the reluctant destination of a runaway prophet &mdash; and the book of Jonah gives it a phrase no reader forgets: &ldquo;Now Nineveh was an exceeding great city of three days&rsquo; journey&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jon/3/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jonah 3:3">Jonah 3:3</a>). Jonah is sent to &ldquo;that great city&rdquo; because &ldquo;their wickedness is come up before me&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jon/1/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jonah 1:2">Jonah 1:2</a>) &mdash; and the book ends, unexpectedly, with God&rsquo;s mercy on its &ldquo;more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jon/4/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jonah 4:11">Jonah 4:11</a>).</p>

<p>But the mercy Jonah resented did not last forever. A later prophet, <strong>Nahum</strong>, devotes his entire book to Nineveh&rsquo;s coming ruin &mdash; &ldquo;The burden of Nineveh&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/nah/1/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nahum 1:1">Nahum 1:1</a>), &ldquo;Woe to the bloody city! it is all full of lies and robbery&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/nah/3/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nahum 3:1">Nahum 3:1</a>) &mdash; and Zephaniah joins him, foretelling that God would &ldquo;make Nineveh a desolation, and dry like a wilderness&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/zep/2/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="Zephaniah 2:13">Zephaniah 2:13</a>). In 612 BC the great city fell to the Medes and Babylonians, and fell so completely that within a few centuries travelers could stand on its buried mounds without knowing a city was under their feet (the fall is <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/07-the-fall-of-the-north/">Section 07</a>; its scriptural echoes, <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/09-assyria-and-the-bible/">Section 09</a>).</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>Four capitals, one lesson:</strong> stand back from Ashur, Kalhu, Dur-Sharrukin, and Nineveh and a pattern emerges &mdash; each king reaching higher, building larger, insisting more loudly that his city would never end. Sargon&rsquo;s pristine capital outlived him by a year; Sennacherib&rsquo;s &ldquo;Palace Without Rival&rdquo; became a mound that hid a library no one knew was there; and the &ldquo;exceeding great city&rdquo; that Jonah walked, and that Nahum said would be &ldquo;laid waste&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/nah/3/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nahum 3:7">Nahum 3:7</a>), vanished so thoroughly that its very existence passed, for a time, out of memory. The prophets read these skylines the way they read Babylon&rsquo;s: as monuments to a glory that was always, from the first brick, on its way to becoming dust.</p>
</div>

<p>How those mounds were lost &mdash; and how, in the 1840s, spades and a few obstinate men brought the winged bulls back into the light &mdash; is the story that <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/10-major-sites-and-discoveries/">Section 10</a> takes up.</p>
