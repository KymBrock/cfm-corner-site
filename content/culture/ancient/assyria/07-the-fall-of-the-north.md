<h2 style="text-align: center;">The Fall of the North &amp; the Lost Tribes</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The night ten tribes marched out of history &mdash; and the hope that has refused, ever since, to let them stay lost</p>

<p>Of the twelve tribes of Israel, ten of them &mdash; the whole northern kingdom &mdash; disappear from the historical record in the space of a single generation. They did not die out in a plague or dissolve in a slow decline. They were <em>removed</em>, deliberately and administratively, by the Assyrian empire, and scattered so thoroughly across its provinces that no one has ever been able to point to their descendants with certainty since. This page tells how it happened, why the Bible says it happened, and what became of the peoples on both ends of the road &mdash; the Israelites marched out and the strangers marched in &mdash; including the tangled origin of the Samaritans and the long, stubborn hope that the lost tribes are only lost, not gone.</p>

<p>The mechanics of Assyrian deportation &mdash; the two-way population engineering that dissolved conquered nations &mdash; are the subject of <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/03-the-war-machine/">Section 03: The War Machine</a>. Here we watch that machine do its most consequential single piece of work, and we listen to how Israel&rsquo;s own prophets and historians made sense of the wreckage.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The North Falls in Pieces, Not at Once</h3>

<p>The end of the northern kingdom was not a single catastrophe but a decade-long amputation, limb by limb. It began under <strong>Tiglath-pileser III</strong>, the king who rebuilt the Assyrian war machine in the mid-eighth century BC. Around 733&ndash;732 BC, drawn into the region by an anti-Assyrian coalition, he sheared off Israel&rsquo;s entire northern and eastern territory. The record is unusually specific about which towns and regions went first:</p>

<p style="margin: 20px 0; padding-left: 20px; border-left: 3px solid #b8ccbb; font-style: italic; color: #444;">&ldquo;In the days of Pekah king of Israel came Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria, and took Ijon, and Abel-beth-maachah, and Janoah, and Kedesh, and Hazor, and Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali, and carried them captive to Assyria&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/15/29/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 15:29">2 Kings 15:29</a>).</p>

<p>In one campaign the north lost Galilee, the Jezreel corridor, and the whole of Transjordan &mdash; Gilead &mdash; leaving only the highland core around the capital. Tiglath-pileser&rsquo;s own annals confirm and extend the biblical note, listing the plunder of the region and the deportation of its people; the Assyrians had begun eating Israel from the edges inward. What remained was a rump state around <strong>Samaria</strong>, ruled by Hoshea, its last king, who paid tribute until he gambled on rebellion &mdash; and Assyria did not forgive a broken oath.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Three-Year Siege of Samaria, 722 BC</h3>

<p>When Hoshea withheld tribute and sent to Egypt for help, the empire came for the capital itself. Samaria was strongly sited, ringed with walls, and it held out with grim endurance:</p>

<p style="margin: 20px 0; padding-left: 20px; border-left: 3px solid #b8ccbb; font-style: italic; color: #444;">&ldquo;Then the king of Assyria came up throughout all the land, and went up to Samaria, and besieged it three years&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:5">2 Kings 17:5</a>).</p>

<p>Three years against a single city &mdash; the whole apparatus of Assyrian siegecraft (rams, ramps, sappers, and the slow strangulation of a blockade; see <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/03-the-war-machine/">Section 03</a>) grinding away at one hilltop. When the wall finally gave, the deportation followed the standard pattern, and the scribe of Kings records the destination with the precision of a shipping manifest:</p>

<p style="margin: 20px 0; padding-left: 20px; border-left: 3px solid #b8ccbb; font-style: italic; color: #444;">&ldquo;In the ninth year of Hoshea the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria, and placed them in Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:6">2 Kings 17:6</a>).</p>

<p>Those are real places, scattered on purpose. Halah and the Habor lay in the far north of Mesopotamia, around the headwaters of a tributary of the Euphrates; &ldquo;the cities of the Medes&rdquo; lay hundreds of miles farther east, on the Iranian plateau. The northern tribes were not resettled together in one exile colony where they might keep their cohesion, as Judah later would be in Babylonia. They were <em>dispersed</em> &mdash; split into fragments and planted among unrelated peoples at opposite ends of the empire, exactly the outcome Assyrian policy was designed to produce.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>Which king took Samaria?</strong> The Bible names the campaign but leaves the conqueror unnamed at the moment of capture, and the Assyrian sources let us see why. <strong>Shalmaneser V</strong> laid and pressed the siege &mdash; a second biblical account credits the fall to his reign (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/18/9/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 18:9-11">2 Kings 18:9&ndash;11</a>) &mdash; but he died just as the city fell in 722 BC. His successor, <strong>Sargon II</strong>, seized the throne and, in his own royal annals, claimed the victory for himself, boasting that he &ldquo;besieged and conquered Samaria&rdquo; and carried off its people. Sargon&rsquo;s inscriptions put a number on the deportation: <strong>27,290 inhabitants</strong> (some editions of the annals read 27,280), along with their chariots and gods. The figure is the empire&rsquo;s own accounting of a nation erased &mdash; and, like all such royal totals, it is a claim to be weighed, not a census to be trusted; but it gives the scale, and it names the human beings behind the Bible&rsquo;s bare phrase &ldquo;carried Israel away.&rdquo;</p>
</div>

<h3>Why the North Fell: the Chronicler&rsquo;s Verdict</h3>

<p>Here 2 Kings does something no Assyrian inscription ever does: it stops the narrative and delivers a long, unsparing explanation of <em>why</em>. The historian is not interested in Assyrian strategy as a cause. For him the armies were only the instrument; the cause was covenant failure, spelled out across seventeen verses (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:7-23">2 Kings 17:7&ndash;23</a>). The indictment opens plainly:</p>

<p style="margin: 20px 0; padding-left: 20px; border-left: 3px solid #b8ccbb; font-style: italic; color: #444;">&ldquo;For so it was, that the children of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, which had brought them up out of the land of Egypt&hellip; and had feared other gods&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:7">2 Kings 17:7</a>).</p>

<p>The charge sheet is specific: idolatry and the worship of &ldquo;other gods,&rdquo; the high places and the golden calves, and above all the founding sin of the northern kingdom &mdash; &ldquo;the sins of Jeroboam,&rdquo; the state-sponsored counter-worship set up when the north broke from the house of David. &ldquo;For the children of Israel walked in all the sins of Jeroboam which he did; they departed not from them,&rdquo; the chronicler writes (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/22/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:22">2 Kings 17:22</a>). And running underneath the whole account is a second charge that gives this guide its throughline: they would not listen to the prophets.</p>

<p style="margin: 20px 0; padding-left: 20px; border-left: 3px solid #b8ccbb; font-style: italic; color: #444;">&ldquo;Yet the LORD testified against Israel, and against Judah, by all the prophets, and by all the seers&hellip; Notwithstanding they would not hear, but hardened their necks&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:13-14">2 Kings 17:13&ndash;14</a>).</p>

<p>The theological summary is delivered without softening: &ldquo;Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel, and removed them out of his sight: there was none left but the tribe of Judah only&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/18/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:18">2 Kings 17:18</a>). The deportation, in this reading, was not Assyria&rsquo;s triumph but Israel&rsquo;s judgment &mdash; the covenant curses arriving on schedule, the warnings of a century of prophets coming true. It is important to hear this verdict for what it is: the northern kingdom&rsquo;s own scripture, written from Judah&rsquo;s standpoint, grieving and accusing at once. It is theology, not neutral chronicle &mdash; and it knows it, which is why it argues its case at such length instead of simply reporting the fall.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Strangers Who Moved In &mdash; and the Samaritan Question</h3>

<p>The land did not stay empty. The inbound half of Assyrian policy followed at once: as the Israelites went out, other conquered peoples were marched in to take their place.</p>

<p style="margin: 20px 0; padding-left: 20px; border-left: 3px solid #b8ccbb; font-style: italic; color: #444;">&ldquo;And the king of Assyria brought men from Babylon, and from Cuthah, and from Ava, and from Hamath, and from Sepharvaim, and placed them in the cities of Samaria instead of the children of Israel&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/24/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:24">2 Kings 17:24</a>).</p>

<p>What the chapter tells next is one of the strangest episodes in Kings. The new settlers &ldquo;feared not the LORD,&rdquo; and so &mdash; in the account&rsquo;s telling &mdash; &ldquo;the LORD sent lions among them, which slew some of them&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/25/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:25">2 Kings 17:25</a>). The colonists concluded they had offended &ldquo;the God of the land&rdquo; whose customs they did not know (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/26/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:26">2 Kings 17:26</a>), and Assyria sent back a deported Israelite priest to teach them how the LORD was to be worshipped. The result was a religious hybrid: &ldquo;They feared the LORD, and served their own gods&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/33/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:33">2 Kings 17:33</a>), each imported nation keeping its own idols alongside the God of Israel &mdash; a syncretism the chapter says persisted &ldquo;unto this day&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/17/41/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 17:41">2 Kings 17:41</a>).</p>

<p>This passage became, for later readers, the biblical charter for viewing the <strong>Samaritans</strong> as a people of foreign, half-pagan origin &mdash; and that is exactly where honesty requires care, because the passage is doing polemical work and should not be quietly turned into settled history.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>Whose account is this?</strong> The &ldquo;imported foreigners&rdquo; story of 2 Kings 17 is the <em>Judahite</em> account &mdash; written from Jerusalem&rsquo;s standpoint, in a centuries-long dispute between two communities that each claimed to be true Israel. The Samaritans themselves have never accepted it. They identify as descendants of <strong>Ephraim and Manasseh</strong> &mdash; the sons of Joseph, genuine Israelite tribes &mdash; who remained in the land and kept the ancient worship on Mount Gerizim, and who regard the Jerusalem temple, not their own faith, as the deviation. Modern scholarship complicates both older certainties. Assyrian deportation was selective, never total; a substantial Israelite population certainly remained in the land, and the community that emerged was very likely a <em>mixture</em> &mdash; surviving Israelites and resettled newcomers together &mdash; rather than either a pure remnant or a purely foreign transplant. Genetic and textual studies of the small Samaritan community that survives today (still worshipping on Gerizim near Nablus) point to deep and real Israelite roots. The wise course is to present 2 Kings faithfully as what it is &mdash; one side&rsquo;s theological history &mdash; while granting that the Samaritans&rsquo; own self-understanding, and the archaeology, tell a fuller and more human story than the polemic allows. The long enmity that flows from this moment, and its echo in the Gospels, belongs to <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/11-lds-connections/">Section 11</a>.</p>
</div>

<h3>The Lost Tribes: What History Knows, and What Hope Keeps</h3>

<p>So what actually became of the ten tribes carried into Halah, Habor, and the cities of the Medes? Here two honest answers sit side by side, and it does no service to either to collapse them into the other.</p>

<h4>What the historian can see</h4>

<p>The sober historical picture is assimilation. This is what Assyrian deportation was engineered to accomplish, and there is no reason to think the northern tribes escaped it. Cut off from their land, their sanctuary, and one another, scattered in fragments among Medes and Mesopotamians, the deportees within a generation or two would have been absorbed into the surrounding populations &mdash; their distinct identity dissolving into the imperial mass, exactly as Assyria intended (see <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/03-the-war-machine/">Section 03</a>). Meanwhile a significant number of northerners never left at all, or fled south into Judah before and after the fall &mdash; Jerusalem itself expanded sharply in this era, apparently absorbing refugees from the north &mdash; so that much of the &ldquo;lost&rdquo; population was, in plain fact, folded into Judah and preserved there. King Hezekiah&rsquo;s Passover appeal, sent north after Samaria fell, is addressed to precisely these survivors: &ldquo;turn again unto the LORD&hellip; and he will return to the remnant of you, that are escaped out of the hand of the kings of Assyria&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ch/30/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Chronicles 30:6">2 Chronicles 30:6</a>). On the historical evidence, the ten tribes were not spirited away to a hidden country; they were, for the most part, absorbed &mdash; some into the nations of the empire, many into Judah, which carried their blood and their memory forward.</p>

<h4>What the tradition keeps alive</h4>

<p>And yet the tribes were never simply written off. From the beginning, the prophets who announced the exile also promised a gathering &mdash; and Judaism and, later, the Restoration have held that promise open for twenty-seven centuries. The hope is not naive; it rests on the covenant. Amos, even as he foretold the captivity, insisted the scattering was a sifting, not an annihilation: &ldquo;I will sift the house of Israel among all nations, like as corn is sifted in a sieve, yet shall not the least grain fall upon the earth&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/amo/9/9/" target="_blank" data-ref="Amos 9:9">Amos 9:9</a>). The people would be dispersed, but not one grain would be lost. Later prophets built whole visions on that seed &mdash; the reuniting of the two sticks of Ephraim and Judah, the gathering of the outcasts of Israel &ldquo;from the four corners of the earth.&rdquo; Judaism preserved the ten tribes as a live expectation of the age to come; Latter-day Saints inherited and sharpened that hope, making the gathering of scattered Israel &mdash; including the lost tribes &mdash; a central article of faith and a defining work of the last days. That thread is followed in full in <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/11-lds-connections/">Section 11: LDS Connections</a>.</p>

<p>The two answers are not really rivals. History describes the <em>means</em> &mdash; assimilation, absorption, the quiet survival of a scattered people inside other peoples. Faith describes the <em>meaning</em> &mdash; that a God who counts grains does not lose them, and that a people mixed into all nations is a people positioned, not erased. What looks from the outside like the end of ten tribes can be read from within the covenant as their seeding across the whole earth, against a harvest still to come.</p>

<h3>The Prophets Who Saw It Coming</h3>

<p>The chronicler&rsquo;s charge that Israel &ldquo;would not hear&rdquo; the prophets is not a rhetorical flourish; we still have the sermons they refused. Two northern prophets in particular &mdash; <strong>Amos</strong> and <strong>Hosea</strong> &mdash; preached to Israel in its last decades and named the coming ruin with painful exactness, decades before it arrived.</p>

<p><strong>Amos</strong>, a Judean shepherd sent north to the prosperous, complacent court of Jeroboam II, announced captivity in a single unadorned sentence: &ldquo;Therefore will I cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus, saith the LORD, whose name is The God of hosts&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/amo/5/27/" target="_blank" data-ref="Amos 5:27">Amos 5:27</a>) &mdash; &ldquo;beyond Damascus&rdquo; meaning up the northern road and off into the empire, precisely the direction the deportees would go. He sang Israel&rsquo;s funeral while the nation still thought itself secure: &ldquo;The virgin of Israel is fallen; she shall no more rise&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/amo/5/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Amos 5:2">Amos 5:2</a>).</p>

<p><strong>Hosea</strong>, a northerner himself, prophesied straight through the chaos of Israel&rsquo;s final kings and diagnosed the fatal reflex &mdash; a nation lurching between foreign patrons instead of returning to God: &ldquo;Ephraim also is like a silly dove without heart: they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/hos/7/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Hosea 7:11">Hosea 7:11</a>). He foretold both the coming Assyrian master &mdash; &ldquo;the Assyrian shall be his king, because they refused to return&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/hos/11/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Hosea 11:5">Hosea 11:5</a>) &mdash; and the scattering that would follow: &ldquo;My God will cast them away, because they did not hearken unto him: and they shall be wanderers among the nations&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/hos/9/17/" target="_blank" data-ref="Hosea 9:17">Hosea 9:17</a>). Yet Hosea, the prophet of the broken marriage between God and Israel, also refused to make the verdict final. His most piercing line holds the whole tragedy and the whole hope in one breath: &ldquo;O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/hos/13/9/" target="_blank" data-ref="Hosea 13:9">Hosea 13:9</a>).</p>

<p>That is the fall of the north in a sentence. The ruin was self-inflicted and long foretold; the help was never withdrawn. Ten tribes marched out of history down the Assyrian road &mdash; and the same prophets who watched them go left, in the covenant, the promise that the road runs both ways. The reckoning Assyria itself would face, and the fuller scriptural accounting of the empire, come in <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/09-assyria-and-the-bible/">Section 09: Assyria &amp; the Bible</a>; the gathering still ahead, in <a href="/culture/ancient/assyria/11-lds-connections/">Section 11</a>.</p>
