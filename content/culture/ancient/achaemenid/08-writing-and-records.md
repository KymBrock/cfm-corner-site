<h2 style="text-align: center;">Writing &amp; Records</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">How the Persian empire wrote itself down &mdash; and how a cliff in Iran unlocked the ancient Near East</p>

<p>The Persians wrote in several scripts and languages at once, and that habit turns out to matter enormously &mdash; both for understanding the empire and, unexpectedly, for our ability to read the entire ancient Near East. The books of Ezra and Esther are steeped in this documentary world of decrees, archives, and multilingual correspondence, and their accuracy about it is one more sign of how close their authors stood to the events. This page follows the empire&rsquo;s writing from the king&rsquo;s monumental cuneiform to the humble Aramaic of everyday administration.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Old Persian Cuneiform &mdash; a King&rsquo;s Script</h3>

<p>For their grandest royal inscriptions, the Achaemenid kings used <strong>Old Persian cuneiform</strong> &mdash; and it is a curiosity worth pausing on. Unlike the sprawling, centuries-old Mesopotamian cuneiform of hundreds of signs (see the <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/05-writing-and-learning/">Babylon guide</a>), Old Persian cuneiform was a compact, mostly <em>alphabetic</em> system of only about forty signs, and it appears to have been more or less <strong>invented</strong> under Darius the Great for display purposes &mdash; a deliberate, prestige script for carving the king&rsquo;s words into stone. It was rarely used for daily business; for that the empire relied on Aramaic and on the older scripts of its subject peoples.</p>

<p>The great royal inscriptions almost always appear in <strong>three languages</strong> at once &mdash; Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian (Akkadian) &mdash; the empire announcing itself trilingually to its most important literate audiences. That trilingual habit was about to become one of the most consequential facts in the history of scholarship.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Behistun: The Cliff That Cracked the Code</h3>

<p>High on a limestone cliff on the old road between Babylon and Ecbatana, Darius the Great carved the most important inscription of the ancient Near East &mdash; the <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Behistun_Inscription/" target="_blank">Behistun Inscription</a></strong> (c. 520 BC). In a giant relief, Darius stands triumphant over the rebels he defeated to seize the throne, with the winged symbol of Ahura Mazda above him; around and below, a long text tells the story of his victory &mdash; in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian, the same account three times over.</p>

<p>Because it repeated one text in three scripts &mdash; and because scholars could begin to work out the alphabetic Old Persian &mdash; Behistun became the <strong>Rosetta Stone of cuneiform</strong>. In the 1830s&ndash;40s, Henry Rawlinson famously scaled the cliff to copy the inscription, and the trilingual key it provided allowed scholars to decipher not only Old Persian but, through it, the long-dead Babylonian and Assyrian writing of Mesopotamia. Nearly everything the <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/">Babylon guide</a> can tell you &mdash; the chronicles, the ration tablets, the flood epic &mdash; is legible today because Darius bragged, in triplicate, on a cliff. It is a striking providence of history that the empire which sent Judah <em>home</em> also, two and a half millennia later, handed the modern world the key to reading the empire that had carried Judah <em>away</em>.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>Darius&rsquo;s own testimony:</strong> the Behistun text is also the fullest first-person statement of Achaemenid ideology we have &mdash; Darius repeatedly crediting <strong>Ahura Mazda</strong> for his kingship and victories, and railing against &ldquo;the Lie&rdquo; (<em>drauga</em>) embodied in the rebels (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/04-religion-zoroastrianism/">Section 04</a>). It shows a king who genuinely believed a high creator-god had appointed him &mdash; the mindset that made a ruler able to fund the temple of a foreign &ldquo;God of heaven&rdquo; without discomfort.</p>
</div>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Persepolis Tablets &mdash; the Empire&rsquo;s Paperwork</h3>

<p>Grand inscriptions tell us what kings wanted remembered; administrative records tell us how the empire actually ran. Excavations at <strong>Persepolis</strong> recovered tens of thousands of clay tablets &mdash; the <strong>Persepolis Fortification Archive</strong> (c. 509&ndash;493 BC) and the smaller <strong>Treasury Archive</strong> &mdash; mostly in <strong>Elamite</strong>, with some in Aramaic. They are not glamorous: they record rations of grain, wine, and livestock issued to workers, officials, priests, and travelers moving on the king&rsquo;s business across the heartland. But that is precisely their value. They show a vast, literate, meticulous bureaucracy in motion &mdash; women in the workforce receiving rations, religious offerings for many gods funded side by side, authorized travelers drawing provisions along the road with sealed documents (exactly the kind of royal travel-authorization Nehemiah requests in <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/2/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 2:7">Nehemiah 2:7</a>). The world of Persian administration that Ezra&ndash;Nehemiah describes from the receiving end, these tablets document from the issuing end.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Aramaic: The Language That Carried the Empire &mdash; and Ezra</h3>

<p>Beneath the monumental Old Persian and the regional Elamite ran the real connective tissue of the empire: <strong>Imperial Aramaic</strong>, the chancery language in which decrees and letters actually traveled (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/03-how-the-empire-worked/">Section 03</a>). It is written on the Elephantine papyri (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/06-rebuilding-jerusalem/">Section 06</a>), on leather documents from Bactria at the empire&rsquo;s eastern edge, and on countless ostraca &mdash; the same standardized official Aramaic from Egypt to Afghanistan.</p>

<p>This is why the Bible itself goes bilingual in exactly the Persian-document sections. The books of Ezra reproduce official correspondence in <strong>Aramaic</strong> &mdash; the accusation letter to Artaxerxes and his reply, the exchange with Darius, and Artaxerxes&rsquo;s commission to Ezra (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/4/8/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 4:8">Ezra 4:8</a>&ndash;<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/6/18/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 6:18">6:18</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/7/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 7:12">7:12&ndash;26</a>) &mdash; preserving the documents in the very language they were issued in, then switching back to Hebrew for the narrative. Portions of Daniel are likewise in Aramaic. Far from a defect, this bilingual texture is a mark of authenticity: the compilers had real Persian-era documents in hand and quoted them as they stood. The Jews carried this Aramaic home, and it remained the daily speech of Judea for centuries &mdash; so that the language of the Persian bureaucracy became, in time, the mother tongue of Jesus of Nazareth.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The archive habit and the Bible:</strong> Persia was a civilization of <em>files</em>. When the Jews&rsquo; right to rebuild is challenged, the case is settled by a search of the royal archives, where Cyrus&rsquo;s decree is found on a scroll at Ecbatana (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/6/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 6:1-2">Ezra 6:1&ndash;2</a>). The court chronicle that saves Mordecai is a written record read back to the king (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/est/6/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Esther 6:1">Esther 6:1</a>). Decrees are drafted, sealed, copied, and dispatched in writing. The post-exilic books breathe this documentary air &mdash; and it is the same air that produced Ezra the <em>scribe</em>, and a community re-founded, more than ever before, on a written and studied text (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/06-rebuilding-jerusalem/">Section 06</a>).</p>
</div>
