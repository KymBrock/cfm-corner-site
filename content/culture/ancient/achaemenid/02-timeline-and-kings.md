<h2 style="text-align: center;">Timeline &amp; Kings</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The Persian kings, and the biblical books that unfold within their reigns</p>


<p>The Achaemenid Empire lasted about 230 years (559&ndash;330 BC) and was ruled by about a dozen kings. Several of these kings appear in the biblical accounts of exile, return, and rebuilding. Here is the great difficulty for readers of Ezra&ndash;Nehemiah: these books are <em>not</em> arranged in tidy chronological order, and several kings share throne-names. This timeline places those rulers alongside Daniel and his companions, Esther and Mordecai, and the people rebuilding Jerusalem.</p>

<p>Persian chronology is unusually firm: Babylonian astronomical records, dated business tablets, and the kings&rsquo; own inscriptions let historians date most events to the year, and some to the day.</p>

<h3>Which Darius?</h3>

<p>The name <strong>Darius</strong> belongs to several different rulers. The numbers I, II, and III are modern labels that help us distinguish them:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Darius the Mede</strong> appears after Belshazzar&rsquo;s death in <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/5/31/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 5:31">Daniel 5:31</a> and is the ruler in the lions&rsquo; den account. His identity outside Daniel remains uncertain; he should not be presented as Darius I.<sup id="achaemenid-note-ref-1-3"><a href="#achaemenid-note-1" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 1">1</a></sup></li>
<li><strong>Darius I, the Great (522&ndash;486 BC)</strong>, is the king in <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/5/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 5-6">Ezra 5&ndash;6</a>, Haggai, and Zechariah. The Second Temple is completed during his reign.</li>
<li><strong>Darius II (c. 423&ndash;404 BC)</strong> reigns much later, more than ninety years after the temple&rsquo;s completion.</li>
<li><strong>Darius III (336&ndash;330 BC)</strong> is the last Achaemenid king, defeated by Alexander. The Church&rsquo;s Bible Dictionary identifies him with &ldquo;Darius the Persian&rdquo; in <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/12/22/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 12:22">Nehemiah 12:22</a>; the verse itself does not supply a number.<sup id="achaemenid-note-ref-1-4"><a href="#achaemenid-note-1" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 1">1</a></sup></li>
</ul>

<h3>What the Royal Names Mean</h3>

<p>These names express ideals of kingship. Some rulers adopted a royal name when they took the throne, and later kings reused those names. They were <strong>royal names, not interchangeable words meaning &ldquo;king.&rdquo;</strong> Their meanings describe royal aspirations rather than proving how a king behaved.<sup id="achaemenid-note-ref-2"><a href="#achaemenid-note-2" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 2">2</a></sup></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Darius</strong>: approximately <strong>&ldquo;holding firm to the good.&rdquo;</strong></li>
<li><strong>Xerxes</strong>: approximately <strong>&ldquo;ruling over heroes.&rdquo;</strong> <strong>Ahasuerus</strong> is a related biblical rendering of the Iranian name. Esther&rsquo;s king is conventionally identified as Xerxes I.</li>
<li><strong>Artaxerxes</strong>: approximately <strong>&ldquo;whose rule is through truth.&rdquo;</strong> It is a distinct Iranian name, despite its resemblance to Xerxes in English.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Reading Ezra&rsquo;s Sequence</h3>

<p><a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/4/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 4">Ezra 4</a> gathers examples of opposition across different reigns. The letters involving Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes in <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/4/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 4:6-7">Ezra 4:6&ndash;7</a> begin a passage about later opposition to Jerusalem. Verse 24 returns to the earlier temple-building story. Reading every paragraph as the next event in time can mix up the kings.<sup id="achaemenid-note-ref-4"><a href="#achaemenid-note-4" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 4">4</a></sup></p>
<p><strong>The temple and the wall are separate projects.</strong> The temple is completed under Darius I in 515 BC. Esther&rsquo;s story belongs within Xerxes&rsquo;s reign. Ezra arrives in 458 BC on the usual reckoning, and Nehemiah rebuilds the wall in 445 BC under Artaxerxes I &mdash; seventy years after the temple&rsquo;s completion.<sup id="achaemenid-note-ref-4-2"><a href="#achaemenid-note-4" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 4">4</a></sup></p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The rebuilding sequence:</strong> Cyrus authorizes the return in <strong>538 BC</strong>. The altar is restored, then the foundation is laid around <strong>536 BC</strong>. Opposition delays the work. Construction resumes in <strong>520 BC</strong> under Darius I; the temple is completed and dedicated in <strong>515 BC</strong>. Ezra&rsquo;s teaching mission and Nehemiah&rsquo;s wall-building come later.</p>
</div>

<h3>Notes</h3>
<ol role="doc-endnotes">
<li id="achaemenid-note-1"><strong>Distinguishing the Dariuses and the names in Daniel.</strong> The Church&rsquo;s <a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/darius?lang=eng" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Bible Dictionary: Darius</a> distinguishes Darius the Mede, Darius the son of Hystaspes (Darius I), and Darius Codomannus (Darius III). It acknowledges that Darius the Mede cannot be securely matched to known rulers from the surviving historical record. Proposed identifications with Cyrus or a subordinate governor remain proposals. <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/9/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 9:1">Daniel 9:1</a> calls the Mede the son of an Ahasuerus; that father should not automatically be equated with Esther&rsquo;s later king. Daniel&rsquo;s court name <strong>Belteshazzar</strong> is also distinct from King <strong>Belshazzar</strong>, who appears in Daniel 5. <a href="#achaemenid-note-ref-1" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Back to note 1 reference">&#8617;</a></li>
<li id="achaemenid-note-2"><strong>Royal names and their meanings.</strong> R&uuml;diger Schmitt discusses the Iranian names of Darius, Xerxes, and Artaxerxes and their royal meanings in his encyclopaedia article <a href="https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/personal-names-iranian-i/personal-names-iranian-iii-achaemenid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">&ldquo;Personal Names, Iranian iii. Achaemenid Period&rdquo;</a>. His <a href="https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/xerxes-1-name/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Xerxes name study</a> gives the Old Persian form <em>Xšaya-ṛšā</em> and traces the name&rsquo;s forms across languages. His <a href="https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/artaxerxes-throne-name-of-several-persian-kings-of-the-achaemenid-dynasty/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Artaxerxes name study</a> explains that the familiar Greek form was influenced by the spelling of Xerxes, although the Iranian names are distinct. These are translations of Iranian names, not meanings derived from the English spelling. <a href="#achaemenid-note-ref-2" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Back to note 2 reference">&#8617;</a></li>
<li id="achaemenid-note-3"><strong>Dates and the long delay.</strong> <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/3/8/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 3:8">Ezra 3:8</a> places the start of construction in the second year after the return; about 536 BC is the working date used here. <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/4/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 4:4-5,24">Ezra 4:4&ndash;5, 24</a> describes opposition from Cyrus&rsquo;s reign into Darius&rsquo;s, followed by a stoppage lasting until Darius&rsquo;s second year. Work was delayed for about fifteen years; the foundation date and the renewal of construction do not independently fix the first year of a complete halt. Foundation to completion is about twenty-one years. <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/6/15/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 6:15">Ezra 6:15</a> gives the completion date as the third of Adar in Darius&rsquo;s sixth year; this guide retains 515 BC as its working conversion. The earlier destruction is commonly dated 587 or 586 BC. Approximate BC dates should not be mistaken for dates explicitly supplied by the biblical text. <a href="#achaemenid-note-ref-3" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Back to note 3 reference">&#8617;</a></li>
<li id="achaemenid-note-4"><strong>Temple, city, and chronology.</strong> <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/4/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 4">Ezra 4</a> combines the early opposition to the temple (verses 1&ndash;5), later opposition involving Jerusalem (verses 6&ndash;23), and a return to the earlier temple narrative (verse 24). The <a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/old-testament-instructors-guide/ezra-haggai?lang=eng" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Church&rsquo;s Ezra&ndash;Haggai guide</a> distinguishes the altar, foundation, opposition, renewed work, dedication, and Passover. This guide uses 458 BC for Ezra&rsquo;s mission; alternative reconstructions place it in 428 or 398 BC and can change its order relative to Nehemiah. Those alternatives do not change the distinction between the earlier temple project and the later wall project. <a href="#achaemenid-note-ref-4" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Back to note 4 reference">&#8617;</a></li>
</ol>
