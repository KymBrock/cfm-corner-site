<h2 style="text-align: center;">Major Sites</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The capitals and monuments of the empire that sent Judah home</p>

<p>The Achaemenid kings did not rule from one capital. They ruled from several, moving with the seasons and the rhythms of state among a handful of great centers scattered across the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia. Four of these &mdash; and one spectacular cliff &mdash; carry the story of Persia and the Bible. Several appear in Scripture by name. All can be visited today, most in Iran. And all reward knowing what happened there.</p>

<h3>Pasargadae &mdash; Cyrus&rsquo;s Capital and Tomb</h3>

<p><strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Pasargadae/" target="_blank">Pasargadae</a></strong>, in the highlands of Fars, was the first Achaemenid capital. <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Cyrus_the_Great/" target="_blank">Cyrus</a> the Great founded it on the site of his victory over the Medes. Its scattered palaces, gardens, and columned halls introduced ideas the later capitals would magnify &mdash; including the walled royal <em>paradise</em> garden (see Section 03). But Pasargadae&rsquo;s enduring monument is the <strong>tomb of Cyrus</strong>: a simple, dignified gabled stone chamber raised on six broad steps, standing alone on the plain. Why does it survive? Largely because, centuries later, <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Alexander_the_Great/" target="_blank">Alexander the Great</a> is said to have ordered it protected and restored &mdash; honoring the founder even as he ended the dynasty. That plain limestone tomb is the resting place of the man Isaiah called the LORD&rsquo;s &ldquo;shepherd&rdquo; and &ldquo;anointed&rdquo; (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/05-cyrus-and-the-return/">Section 05</a>) &mdash; the deliverer who ended the exile. Its restraint is itself a kind of eloquence.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Persepolis &mdash; the Ceremonial Heart</h3>

<p><strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/persepolis/" target="_blank">Persepolis</a></strong> (Old Persian <em>Parsa</em>) was the empire&rsquo;s great ceremonial capital. <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Darius_I/" target="_blank">Darius the Great</a> began it; <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Xerxes_I/" target="_blank">Xerxes</a> and their successors expanded it. Picture a vast terraced complex of audience halls, palaces, and a treasury, approached by monumental stairways. This was where the kings celebrated the New Year and received the empire. It was also where the <strong>Persepolis Fortification tablets</strong> were found (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/08-writing-and-records/">Section 08</a>).</p>

<p>Its most famous carvings show the <strong>procession of subject nations</strong> bringing tribute to the king. Medes, Elamites, Babylonians, Egyptians, Ionians, Bactrians, and more &mdash; each in distinctive dress, each bearing the gifts of their lands. It is a stone catalogue of the empire&rsquo;s astonishing diversity. It is also the visual embodiment of Esther&rsquo;s &ldquo;hundred and seven and twenty provinces&rdquo; (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/03-how-the-empire-worked/">Section 03</a>).</p>

<p>Then came the fire. In 330 BC <strong>Alexander the Great burned it</strong> &mdash; whether by drunken accident or deliberate revenge for Xerxes&rsquo;s burning of Athens, the sources dispute. Its blackened ruins mark the end of the empire &mdash; the closing of the age Daniel had glimpsed as the &ldquo;he goat&rdquo; charging from the west (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/8/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 8:5-7">Daniel 8:5&ndash;7</a>).</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>Naqsh-e Rustam:</strong> a short distance from Persepolis lies the cliff-face burial ground (necropolis) of <strong>Naqsh-e Rustam</strong>, holding the rock-cut tombs of Darius the Great and several successors. Each tomb is a huge cross-shaped facade carved into the cliff, showing the king worshipping before a fire altar under the winged emblem of Ahura Mazda (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/04-religion-zoroastrianism/">Section 04</a>). Darius&rsquo;s tomb inscription there is one of the noblest statements of Achaemenid kingship. It presents the king as a just ruler &mdash; one who rewards the faithful, punishes the wrongdoer, and holds the sprawling empire together by law.</p>
</div>

<h3>Susa (Shushan) &mdash; the Winter Capital</h3>

<p><strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Susa/" target="_blank">Susa</a></strong> &mdash; the &ldquo;Shushan&rdquo; of the Bible &mdash; was the administrative and winter capital, on the warm lowland plains. It is the biblical site <em>par excellence</em> of the Persian court. The entire book of <strong>Esther</strong> is set here (&ldquo;Shushan the palace,&rdquo; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/est/1/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Esther 1:2">Esther 1:2</a>; see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/07-esthers-world/">Section 07</a>). Here <strong>Nehemiah</strong> served the king and heard of Jerusalem&rsquo;s ruin (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/1/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 1:1">Nehemiah 1:1</a>). And here <strong>Daniel</strong> saw his vision of the ram and the he-goat &ldquo;by the river of Ulai&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/8/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 8:2">Daniel 8:2</a>). Excavations uncovered the great columned audience hall (<em>apadana</em>) of Darius and Xerxes, along with the glazed-brick reliefs of royal guards &mdash; the physical stage of Esther&rsquo;s drama. By tradition, Susa is also home to a shrine revered as the <strong>tomb of Daniel</strong>, honored by Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Ecbatana (Achmetha) &mdash; Where the Decree Was Filed</h3>

<p><strong>Ecbatana</strong> (biblical <strong>Achmetha</strong>) was the old Median capital, high in the Zagros mountains &mdash; modern Hamadan. It served as the Achaemenids&rsquo; cool <em>summer</em> residence. And it earns its biblical moment in one of the most satisfying scenes in Ezra. The Jews&rsquo; opponents challenge their permit to rebuild. Darius orders a search of the archives. And Cyrus&rsquo;s original decree is found &mdash; not in Babylon, but filed away at Ecbatana: &ldquo;there was found at Achmetha, in the palace&hellip; a roll, and therein was a record thus written&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/6/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 6:2">Ezra 6:2</a>). It was the exact document, in the exact kind of provincial archive, that vindicated the whole enterprise (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/06-rebuilding-jerusalem/">Section 06</a>). The detail that the decree turned up at the <em>summer</em> capital, where Cyrus may well have been residing when he issued it, is the kind of incidental precision that marks a writer who knew the empire&rsquo;s workings.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Behistun &mdash; the Empire&rsquo;s Cliff</h3>

<p>On the mountain road between Babylon and Ecbatana rises the <strong>Behistun</strong> cliff. There Darius the Great carved his trilingual monument of victory (see <a href="/culture/ancient/achaemenid/08-writing-and-records/">Section 08</a>). Behistun names no biblical figure. It belongs on any list of the empire&rsquo;s great sites anyway &mdash; both as the fullest statement of Achaemenid royal ideology and as the &ldquo;Rosetta Stone of cuneiform&rdquo; that reopened the writing of the entire ancient Near East to the modern world.</p>
