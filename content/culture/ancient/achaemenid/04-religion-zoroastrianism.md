<h2 style="text-align: center;">Religion: Zoroastrianism</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The faith of the Persian kings &mdash; and the careful question of what, if anything, it shared with Israel</p>

<p>When the Jews came home from exile, they found themselves under a new kind of ruler. These kings were not idol-worshippers of the Babylonian kind. They were not followers of the God of Israel either. They belonged to a third thing entirely: an Iranian religion centered on a single great god of light and truth &mdash; a faith with no temple statues, honoring fire on open-air altars. We call it <strong>Zoroastrianism</strong>. It was the faith of <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Cyrus_the_Great/" target="_blank">Cyrus</a>&rsquo;s heirs, and understanding it clears up much about the Persian world of Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel. It also raises one of the most delicate questions in the study of the Bible &mdash; one this page will handle carefully and honestly. Did Persian religion <em>influence</em> Judaism?</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Ahura Mazda and Zarathustra</h3>

<p>Open the inscriptions of the Persian kings, and one god stands at the center: <strong>Ahura Mazda</strong> (&ldquo;the Wise Lord&rdquo;). <a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Darius_I/" target="_blank">Darius the Great</a> opens his great Behistun inscription by crediting him for everything: &ldquo;By the favor of Ahura Mazda I am king; Ahura Mazda bestowed the kingdom upon me.&rdquo; And notice what Ahura Mazda is <em>not</em>. He is not a statue. He is portrayed as the maker of heaven and earth, of humankind, and of happiness for humankind &mdash; a high creator-god of <strong>truth</strong> and <strong>light</strong>. His opposite is <em>drauga</em>, &ldquo;the Lie.&rdquo;</p>

<figure class="fg-figure">
  <img src="/images/culture/achaemenid/photos/faravahar-persepolis.jpg" alt="The winged Faravahar symbol carved at Persepolis" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The winged figure carved above the doorways of Persepolis &mdash; the emblem long associated with <b>Ahura Mazda</b> and the royal glory (its precise meaning is still debated). <span class="fg-credit">Persepolis &middot; photo Napishtim, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)</span></figcaption>
</figure>

<p>The prophet behind this faith was <strong>Zarathustra</strong> (Greek <em>Zoroaster</em>), an Iranian religious teacher. When did he live? Nobody is sure &mdash; estimates range widely, from around 1200 BC to around 600 BC. His hymns, the <em>Gathas</em>, are preserved in Avestan, the faith&rsquo;s sacred language. They call people to worship Ahura Mazda and to choose truth (<em>asha</em>) over the Lie, in a cosmos understood as a moral struggle. Classic Zoroastrianism came to frame that struggle as a great cosmic contest: Ahura Mazda on one side, and a hostile spirit of darkness, <strong>Angra Mainyu</strong> (Ahriman), on the other. This is <em>dualism</em> &mdash; good and evil locked in conflict, moving toward a final resolution in which good triumphs.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>A hard problem of dating:</strong> almost every question about early Zoroastrianism runs into the same wall. The sources are late. The <em>Gathas</em> may be very old, but they were passed down orally for centuries and written down far later. The fuller Zoroastrian scriptures, and the systematic theology, survive mainly in texts from the Sasanian era &mdash; a later Persian empire, centuries after Christ &mdash; and after (3rd&ndash;9th centuries AD), a thousand years past the Achaemenids. So we often cannot be sure exactly what a Persian in Ezra&rsquo;s day believed, as opposed to what later Zoroastrian tradition taught. Hold onto that uncertainty. It is the single most important thing in the influence debate below &mdash; and the honest scholar keeps returning to it.</p>
</div>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>What Persian Worship Looked Like</h3>

<p>Persian religion in the Achaemenid period had a distinctive shape. To a Judean, some of it would have felt familiar &mdash; and some of it strange:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>No cult statues.</strong> Like Israel, and unlike Babylon, the Persians did not worship carved images of their gods in temples. Herodotus noted with interest that the Persians &ldquo;have no images of the gods, no temples nor altars, and consider the use of them a sign of folly.&rdquo; A returning exile who had grown up amid Babylon&rsquo;s idol-processions (see the <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/04-religion-and-mythology/">Babylon guide</a>) would have noticed the difference immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Fire and open-air altars.</strong> Worship centered on tending a sacred <strong>fire</strong> &mdash; a symbol of Ahura Mazda&rsquo;s purity and truth, not a god itself. It burned on raised altars, often on hilltops, out in the open rather than inside a temple. That open-air setting is why Herodotus reported that the Persians had &ldquo;no altars&rdquo;: to a Greek expecting a roofed temple with a statue inside, a bare fire-platform simply did not look like an altar. The priests who tended the fire were called <strong>magi</strong> &mdash; the word behind &ldquo;magic,&rdquo; and behind the &ldquo;wise men&rdquo; of <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/mat/2/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Matthew 2">Matthew 2</a> &mdash; the Gospel&rsquo;s <em>magoi</em>, eastern priest-astronomers of just this kind, though it never says exactly where in the east they came from.</li>
<li><strong>Purity and ethics.</strong> Zoroastrian practice put great weight on ritual purity and on the moral triad &ldquo;good thoughts, good words, good deeds.&rdquo;</li>
</ul>

<p>The Bible never describes Persian worship directly. But it does show the priesthood at work: Daniel is set over &ldquo;the wise men of Babylon&rdquo; including &ldquo;the magicians&rdquo; and &ldquo;the Chaldeans&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/2/48/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 2:48">Daniel 2:48</a>) &mdash; the Babylonian scholar-priests, whose Persian counterparts were the magi &mdash; without ever adopting their arts.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Influence Question &mdash; Handled Honestly</h3>

<p>Here is the delicate part. Some scholars have proposed that during and after the exile, Jewish thought absorbed ideas from Persian religion. Four candidates come up again and again: angels, a battle between good and evil, resurrection, and the end of the world. Named more fully: a sharpened sense of <strong>angels and demons</strong>, a cosmic <strong>opposition between good and evil</strong>, a developed <strong>afterlife, resurrection, and final judgment</strong>, and an <strong>end-times</strong> hope of a world set right. Why suspect Persia? Because these themes are more prominent in post-exilic and later Jewish writings than in the earliest ones &mdash; so, the argument goes, Persia may have shaped them.</p>

<p>That argument deserves a careful, layered answer, not a slogan &mdash; because this topic is often handled badly in both directions.</p>

<p><strong>First, the honest difficulties with the &ldquo;Persia taught Israel&rdquo; claim:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The dating problem (again).</strong> The Jewish writings that supposedly copied these ideas are older than the Zoroastrian texts that spell the ideas out &mdash; so who copied whom? Much of what is confidently called &ldquo;Zoroastrian&rdquo; &mdash; the systematic dualism, the detailed resurrection doctrine &mdash; is firmly attested only in texts <em>later</em> than the Jewish writings it supposedly influenced. When the borrower&rsquo;s document is older than the lender&rsquo;s, the direction of borrowing is not obvious. Some scholars argue the influence, if any, ran the other way, or from a shared older stock.</li>
<li><strong>Internal roots.</strong> Many of these themes have clear seeds <em>within</em> Israel&rsquo;s own earlier scriptures. The divine council &mdash; the heavenly court of God &mdash; and its members are already there (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/22/19/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 22:19">1 Kings 22:19</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/job/1/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Job 1:6">Job 1:6</a>). So is the hope of resurrection (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/26/19/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 26:19">Isaiah 26:19</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/12/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 12:2">Daniel 12:2</a>), and so is a coming day of the Lord in the pre-exilic prophets. Take resurrection as the test case: it appears already in Isaiah. A theme becoming <em>more explicit</em> over time is not the same as a theme being <em>imported</em>.</li>
<li><strong>The decisive difference.</strong> Israel&rsquo;s faith was never dualist in the Zoroastrian sense. There are not two co-eternal powers. There is one God, who is sovereign even over the adversary (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/job/1/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="Job 1:12">Job 1:12</a>) &mdash; and who declares, in words spoken through Isaiah <em>to Cyrus himself</em>, &ldquo;I form the light, and create darkness&hellip; I the LORD do all these things&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/45/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 45:7">Isaiah 45:7</a>). Many read that very verse as a deliberate rejection of a two-power cosmos &mdash; a monotheistic answer aimed straight at the Persian worldview, in the same breath that names the Persian king.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Second, what can be said fairly:</strong> Jews and Persians lived side by side for two centuries &mdash; in Babylon, in Susa, in Yehud &mdash; and cultures in contact do share vocabulary and imagery. It would be strange if two peoples so long entangled had learned <em>nothing</em> from one another&rsquo;s ways of speaking about God, the heavens, and the end of days. So careful scholars speak of a <em>shared environment</em>, and of possible mutual influence at the level of imagery and emphasis. What they resist is the flat claim that Judaism&rsquo;s core doctrines were &ldquo;borrowed from Zoroastrianism.&rdquo; The evidence simply does not support so strong a statement.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>A Latter-day Saint frame:</strong> Latter-day Saints have a distinctive way of holding this. We understand truth to have been revealed from the beginning &mdash; that Adam, Enoch, Noah, and Abraham knew of the plan of salvation, the adversary, the resurrection, and the final judgment long before any of these peoples (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/6?lang=eng&amp;id=p51-p68#p51" target="_blank">Moses 6:51&ndash;68</a>). On that view, genuine echoes of these truths <em>anywhere</em> in the ancient world &mdash; including in Persia &mdash; are not a threat to be explained away. They are scattered light: fragments and memories of an original knowledge, dimmed and reshaped in transmission. Where Zoroastrian teaching resembles biblical truth, a Latter-day Saint can see a distant echo of what was once revealed plainly. Where it diverges into dualism or the veneration of the elements, we can see how far a true seed can drift. This lets us take the historical parallels seriously and calmly &mdash; without forcing a borrowing, and without fearing one.</p>
</div>

<h3>The Bottom Line for Bible Study</h3>

<p>For reading Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel, the practical takeaways are simple. The Persian kings sincerely believed a high creator-god had given them their empire. That is why they could hear a request to honor &ldquo;the God of heaven&rdquo; (Ezra and Nehemiah&rsquo;s favorite title for the LORD, <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/1/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 1:2">Ezra 1:2</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/1/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 1:4">Nehemiah 1:4</a>) as something familiar &mdash; and fund a temple to Him without feeling they had betrayed their own. The influence debate, meanwhile, should be presented to students the way the best scholarship holds it: as a real and interesting <em>question</em>, not a settled fact &mdash; with the sober reminder that the sources are late and the direction of any borrowing is genuinely uncertain. And on the deepest point there is no ambiguity at all. Israel&rsquo;s witness to <em>one</em> sovereign God, maker of both light and dark, stands in clear and deliberate contrast to Persian dualism &mdash; and says so in the very chapter that blesses the Persian king.</p>
