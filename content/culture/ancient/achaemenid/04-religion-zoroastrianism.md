<h2 style="text-align: center;">Religion: Zoroastrianism</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The faith of the Persian kings &mdash; and the careful question of what, if anything, it shared with Israel</p>

<p>The Jews who returned from exile lived, for the first time, under rulers who were neither idol-worshippers of the Babylonian kind nor followers of the God of Israel, but adherents of a third thing: an Iranian religion centered on a single great god of light and truth, with no temple statues, honoring fire on open-air altars. That religion &mdash; what we call <strong>Zoroastrianism</strong> &mdash; is the faith of Cyrus&rsquo;s heirs, and understanding it clears up much about the Persian world of Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel. It also raises one of the most delicate questions in the study of the Bible, which this page will handle carefully and honestly: did Persian religion <em>influence</em> Judaism?</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Ahura Mazda and Zarathustra</h3>

<p>At the center of the Persian kings&rsquo; inscriptions stands one god: <strong>Ahura Mazda</strong> (&ldquo;the Wise Lord&rdquo;). Darius the Great opens his great Behistun inscription by crediting him for everything: &ldquo;By the favor of Ahura Mazda I am king; Ahura Mazda bestowed the kingdom upon me.&rdquo; Ahura Mazda is portrayed not as a statue but as the maker of heaven and earth, of humankind, and of happiness for humankind &mdash; a high creator-god of <strong>truth</strong> and <strong>light</strong>, opposed by <em>drauga</em>, &ldquo;the Lie.&rdquo;</p>

<p>The prophet behind this faith was <strong>Zarathustra</strong> (Greek <em>Zoroaster</em>), an Iranian religious teacher who lived at a much-debated date &mdash; estimates range widely from around 1200 BC to around 600 BC. His hymns, the <em>Gathas</em>, preserved in the sacred language Avestan, call people to worship Ahura Mazda and to choose truth (<em>asha</em>) over the Lie in a cosmos understood as a moral struggle. Classic Zoroastrianism came to frame that struggle as a great cosmic contest between Ahura Mazda and a hostile spirit of darkness, <strong>Angra Mainyu</strong> (Ahriman) &mdash; a <em>dualism</em> of good and evil moving toward a final resolution in which good triumphs.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>A hard problem of dating:</strong> almost every question about early Zoroastrianism runs into the same wall &mdash; the sources are late. The <em>Gathas</em> may be very old, but they were transmitted orally for centuries and written down far later; the fuller Zoroastrian scriptures and the systematic theology survive mainly in texts from the Sasanian era and after (3rd&ndash;9th centuries AD), a thousand years past the Achaemenids. So we often cannot be sure exactly what a Persian in Ezra&rsquo;s day believed, as opposed to what later Zoroastrian tradition taught. This uncertainty is the single most important thing to hold onto in the influence debate below &mdash; and the honest scholar keeps returning to it.</p>
</div>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>What Persian Worship Looked Like</h3>

<p>Persian religion in the Achaemenid period had a distinctive shape that a Judean would have found in some ways familiar and in some ways strange:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>No cult statues.</strong> Like Israel, and unlike Babylon, the Persians did not worship carved images of their gods in temples. Herodotus noted with interest that the Persians &ldquo;have no images of the gods, no temples nor altars, and consider the use of them a sign of folly.&rdquo; A returning exile who had grown up amid Babylon&rsquo;s idol-processions (see the <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/04-religion-and-mythology/">Babylon guide</a>) would have noticed the difference immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Fire and open-air altars.</strong> Worship centered on tending a sacred <strong>fire</strong> &mdash; a symbol of Ahura Mazda&rsquo;s purity and truth, not a god itself &mdash; on raised altars, often on hilltops, tended by priests called <strong>magi</strong> (the same word behind &ldquo;magic&rdquo; and behind the &ldquo;wise men&rdquo; of Matthew 2, who were Persian-tradition priest-astronomers).</li>
<li><strong>Purity and ethics.</strong> Zoroastrian practice put great weight on ritual purity and on the moral triad &ldquo;good thoughts, good words, good deeds.&rdquo;</li>
</ul>

<p>The Bible does not describe Persian worship directly, but it shows the priesthood at work: Daniel is set over &ldquo;the wise men of Babylon&rdquo; including &ldquo;the magicians&rdquo; and &ldquo;the Chaldeans&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/2/48/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 2:48">Daniel 2:48</a>) &mdash; the learned priestly class the magi belonged to &mdash; without ever adopting their arts.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Influence Question &mdash; Handled Honestly</h3>

<p>Here is the delicate part. Some scholars have proposed that during and after the exile, Jewish thought absorbed ideas from Persian religion &mdash; and the usual candidates are named: a sharpened sense of <strong>angels and demons</strong>, a cosmic <strong>opposition between good and evil</strong>, a developed <strong>afterlife, resurrection, and final judgment</strong>, and an <strong>end-times</strong> hope of a world set right. Because these themes are more prominent in post-exilic and later Jewish writings than in the earliest ones, the argument goes, Persia may have shaped them.</p>

<p>This deserves a careful, layered response rather than a slogan &mdash; because the topic is often handled badly in both directions.</p>

<p><strong>First, the honest difficulties with the &ldquo;Persia taught Israel&rdquo; claim:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The dating problem (again).</strong> Much of what is confidently called &ldquo;Zoroastrian&rdquo; &mdash; the systematic dualism, the detailed resurrection doctrine &mdash; is firmly attested only in texts <em>later</em> than the Jewish writings it supposedly influenced. When the borrower&rsquo;s document is older than the lender&rsquo;s, the direction of borrowing is not obvious. Some scholars argue the influence, if any, ran the other way, or from a shared older stock.</li>
<li><strong>Internal roots.</strong> Many of these themes have clear seeds <em>within</em> Israel&rsquo;s own earlier scriptures: the divine council and its members (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/22/19/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 22:19">1 Kings 22:19</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/job/1/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Job 1:6">Job 1:6</a>), the hope of resurrection (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/26/19/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 26:19">Isaiah 26:19</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/12/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 12:2">Daniel 12:2</a>), and a coming day of the Lord in the pre-exilic prophets. A theme becoming <em>more explicit</em> over time is not the same as a theme being <em>imported</em>.</li>
<li><strong>The decisive difference.</strong> Israel&rsquo;s faith was never dualist in the Zoroastrian sense. There are not two co-eternal powers; there is one God, who is sovereign even over the adversary (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/job/1/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="Job 1:12">Job 1:12</a>) and who declares, in words spoken through Isaiah <em>to Cyrus himself</em>, &ldquo;I form the light, and create darkness&hellip; I the LORD do all these things&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/45/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 45:7">Isaiah 45:7</a>). Many read that very verse as a deliberate rejection of a two-power cosmos &mdash; a monotheistic answer aimed straight at the Persian worldview, in the same breath that names the Persian king.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Second, what can be said fairly:</strong> Jews and Persians lived side by side for two centuries, in Babylon, Susa, and Yehud, and cultures in contact do share vocabulary and imagery. It would be strange if two peoples so long entangled had learned <em>nothing</em> from one another&rsquo;s ways of speaking about God, the heavens, and the end of days. Careful scholars therefore speak of a <em>shared environment</em> and possible mutual influence at the level of imagery and emphasis, while resisting the flat claim that Judaism&rsquo;s core doctrines were &ldquo;borrowed from Zoroastrianism.&rdquo; The evidence simply does not support so strong a statement.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>A Latter-day Saint frame:</strong> Latter-day Saints have a distinctive way of holding this. We understand truth to have been revealed from the beginning &mdash; that Adam, Enoch, Noah, and Abraham knew of the plan of salvation, the adversary, the resurrection, and the final judgment long before any of these peoples (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/6?lang=eng&amp;id=p51-p68#p51" target="_blank">Moses 6:51&ndash;68</a>). On that view, genuine echoes of these truths <em>anywhere</em> in the ancient world &mdash; including in Persia &mdash; are not a threat to be explained away but scattered light: fragments and memories of an original knowledge, dimmed and reshaped in transmission. Where Zoroastrian teaching resembles biblical truth, a Latter-day Saint can see a distant echo of what was once revealed plainly; where it diverges into dualism or the veneration of the elements, we can see how far a true seed can drift. This lets us take the historical parallels seriously and calmly, without either forcing a borrowing or fearing one.</p>
</div>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Bottom Line for Bible Study</h3>

<p>For reading Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel, the practical takeaways are simple. The Persian kings sincerely believed a high creator-god had given them their empire &mdash; which is why they could hear a request to honor &ldquo;the God of heaven&rdquo; (Ezra and Nehemiah&rsquo;s favorite title for the LORD, <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/1/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 1:2">Ezra 1:2</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/1/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 1:4">Nehemiah 1:4</a>) as something familiar and fund a temple to Him without feeling they had betrayed their own. The influence debate, meanwhile, should be presented to students the way the best scholarship holds it: as a real and interesting <em>question</em>, not a settled fact &mdash; with the sober reminder that the sources are late and the direction of any borrowing is genuinely uncertain. On the deepest point there is no ambiguity at all: Israel&rsquo;s witness to <em>one</em> sovereign God, maker of both light and dark, stands in clear and deliberate contrast to Persian dualism &mdash; and says so in the very chapter that blesses the Persian king.</p>
