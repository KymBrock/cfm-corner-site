<h2 style="text-align: center;">The Calendar &amp; the Names of Time</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">How Babylon taught the world to count time &mdash; and named it after gods</p>

<p>Every time you glance at a clock and read <em>sixty</em> minutes, or a compass and read <em>three hundred sixty</em> degrees, you are using a system Babylon gave the world. Its astronomers kept the longest watch in the ancient world, and their base-60 arithmetic still runs quietly beneath every hour we count.</p>

<p>But Israel did not arrive in Babylon without a calendar. Long before the exile it kept its own reckoning of time &mdash; and kept it, pointedly, <em>without</em> the names of gods. Where the nations named their days and months for the sun, the moon, and the planets they worshipped, Israel mostly counted: &ldquo;the second month,&rdquo; &ldquo;the seventh month, on the first day.&rdquo; What the exiles met on the Processional Way (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/06-the-city-itself/">Section 06</a>) was not a blank to be filled but a science to be adapted &mdash; the calendar-craft of the world&rsquo;s greatest astronomers, which they took up without taking up its star-worship.</p>

<p>This page is about both halves of that story: the genuine brilliance of Babylonian time-science, the mathematics that passed from clay tablets into ours &mdash; and the older Israelite instinct that could borrow Babylon&rsquo;s calendar and still resist naming time after gods. It is a story of discernment as much as adoption. Only one seam shows the strain &mdash; a single borrowed month-name, <em>Tammuz</em>, carrying the name of a god Ezekiel had once seen mourned. And before anyone shakes a head at it, this page holds up a mirror: our own week is named, almost entirely, for gods we no longer even notice.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Long Watch: Babylonian Time-Science</h3>

<p>Babylon kept a <strong>lunisolar</strong> calendar &mdash; the hardest kind to run well. The month began with the first sighting of the new crescent moon, giving twelve months of roughly 29 or 30 days; but twelve lunar months fall about eleven days short of a solar year, so a purely lunar calendar drifts backward through the seasons, and a barley festival slides into winter within a decade. The Babylonians solved the drift by <strong>intercalation</strong> &mdash; periodically inserting a thirteenth &ldquo;leap month&rdquo; to snap the calendar back into step with the sun. For centuries the decision to add a month was made ad hoc by royal decree; but by the Persian period the astronomers had reduced it to a rule, fixing the pattern on a <strong>19-year cycle</strong> (seven leap-months every nineteen years) so precise that the lunar and solar calendars realign almost perfectly. That same 19-year cycle still governs the Jewish calendar &mdash; and, through it, the date of Easter &mdash; running quietly underneath the modern world.</p>

<p>The engine beneath all of this was a way of counting. Babylonian arithmetic ran on a <strong>sexagesimal</strong> (base-60) place-value system (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/05-writing-and-learning/">Section 05</a>), and sixty turned out to be a magnificent choice: it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, and 30, which makes fractions and angles behave. We never let it go. The <strong>360-degree circle</strong>, the <strong>60 minutes</strong> in an hour, the <strong>60 seconds</strong> in a minute, the sixtieths of a degree we still call minutes and seconds of arc &mdash; all of it is Babylon, preserved for four thousand years because base-60 was simply too good at dividing the sky to abandon.</p>

<p>And the sky is what they watched. Because the heavens were read as a running message from the gods (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/04-religion-and-mythology/">Section 04</a>), the heavens were observed with a discipline no ancient culture matched. From the mid-eighth century BC, Babylonian scholars kept the <strong>Astronomical Diaries</strong> &mdash; nightly records of the moon, the planets, eclipses, weather, river levels, and grain prices &mdash; maintained for something like seven centuries, one of the longest continuous observational records in human history. Out of that mountain of data came real predictive power: they mastered the roughly <strong>18-year eclipse cycle</strong> (later called the <em>Saros</em>) that lets you forecast eclipses; they built mathematical models of lunar and planetary motion; and, around the fifth century BC, they carved the ecliptic into the familiar <strong>twelve-sign zodiac</strong> &mdash; Aries, Taurus, Gemini, and the rest &mdash; which is Babylonian in origin, inherited by the Greeks and by us. When Isaiah taunts &ldquo;the astrologers, the stargazers, the monthly prognosticators&rdquo; of Babylon (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/05-writing-and-learning/">Section 05</a>), the taunt only bites because these were, in cold fact, the best astronomers alive.</p>

<h3>The Mathematics &mdash; Carefully</h3>

<p>The reputation is deserved. Babylonian mathematics was genuinely advanced: true place-value notation two thousand years before the decimal system reached Europe, the systematic solving of what we would call quadratic equations, square roots computed to several decimal places, and vast reference tables of reciprocals, squares, and cubes that let a scribe multiply and divide by lookup. This was working mathematics &mdash; for surveying fields, splitting inheritances, digging canals, and provisioning armies &mdash; and it was very good.</p>

<p>It is tempting to go one step further and credit Babylon with <em>trigonometry</em>, and here we should slow down. The famous tablet <strong>Plimpton 322</strong> &mdash; a Old Babylonian table of Pythagorean-style number triples, written a thousand years before Pythagoras &mdash; is real and remarkable. In 2017 two mathematicians, <strong>Daniel Mansfield and Norman Wildberger</strong>, argued that it is more than a list of triples: that it is, in effect, the world&rsquo;s oldest trigonometric table, a ratio-based tool for solving triangles that predates Greek trigonometry by over a millennium. It is a striking claim, and it may well be right &mdash; <strong>but it is contested</strong>. Many Assyriologists read the same tablet as a teacher&rsquo;s exercise in generating number pairs, part of the scribal-school curriculum, with no trigonometric intent at all. So it belongs in this guide honestly labeled: <em>a live scholarly debate, not a settled fact.</em></p>

<p>What is <em>not</em> in doubt is the lineage that runs forward from Babylon into the exact sciences of the West. Greek astronomers did not build in a vacuum: <strong>Hipparchus</strong> in the second century BC inherited Babylonian eclipse records, Babylonian planetary parameters, and the Babylonian sexagesimal degree, and it was on that foundation &mdash; measured Babylonian data organized by Greek geometry &mdash; that Greek trigonometry and Ptolemy&rsquo;s astronomy were raised. The solid, uncontested story is not &ldquo;Babylon invented trigonometry&rdquo; but something almost as impressive: <strong>Babylon supplied the centuries of observation and the base-60 arithmetic on which Greek exact science was built.</strong> The line runs Babylon &rarr; Greece &rarr; us, and it runs right through your wristwatch.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Abraham the Astronomer &mdash; and the Star-Gods He Left</h3>

<p>The instinct to take the sky&rsquo;s mathematics without its gods did not begin in exile; the tradition traces it to Abraham himself. Abraham came out of Ur of the Chaldees &mdash; the heartland of Babylonian star-science &mdash; and when God takes him out to &ldquo;tell the stars, if thou be able to number them&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/15/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 15:5">Genesis 15:5</a>), He addresses a man already fluent in the heavens. The Hebrew for &ldquo;tell&rdquo; is <bdi>סָפַר</bdi> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h5608/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank"><em>saphar</em></a>) &mdash; &ldquo;to count,&rdquo; but also &ldquo;to recount, to record&rdquo; &mdash; the same root behind <bdi>סֵפֶר</bdi> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h5612/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank"><em>sepher</em></a>, &ldquo;book&rdquo;). To <em>number</em> the stars was, in the same breath, to <em>keep the record</em> of them.</p>

<p>Later tradition remembered exactly this. The first-century historian Josephus credits Abraham with carrying astronomy &ldquo;from the Chaldeans into Egypt, and from thence to the Greeks also&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=J.+AJ+1.167&amp;fromdoc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0146" target="_blank"><em>Antiquities</em> 1.8.2</a>) &mdash; the same Chaldea-to-Greece road this page has been tracing, now with a patriarch walking it. And the Book of Jubilees remembers the decisive turn: Abraham watching the night sky for omens until &ldquo;a word came into his heart&rdquo; &mdash; &ldquo;All the signs of the stars &hellip; are in the hand of the Lord&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.sefaria.org/Book_of_Jubilees.12" target="_blank">Jubilees 12:16&ndash;17</a>) &mdash; and breaking from the star-worship of his Chaldean kin, taking up instead &ldquo;the books of his fathers&rdquo; (Jubilees 12:27). <em>(Jubilees and Josephus are later Jewish tradition, not scripture &mdash; offered here as the tradition&rsquo;s memory of Abraham, not as established history.)</em></p>

<p>That is the seed of a standard Israel would not always keep &mdash; and the next section watches it both held and, in one telling instance, let go. When the returning exiles took up Babylon&rsquo;s calendar, most of what they adopted was harmless arithmetic and agricultural month-names; but one adopted name, <strong>Tammuz</strong>, carried a god Ezekiel had condemned (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/eze/8/14/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezekiel 8:14">Ezekiel 8:14</a>). The calendar that came home from Babylon was a <em>compromise</em> of the older instinct, not its fulfillment.</p>

<h3>The Calendar That Changed in Exile</h3>

<p>Now the quieter story &mdash; the one that touches scripture directly. <strong>Before the exile, Israel mostly numbered its months.</strong> The Bible&rsquo;s default is ordinal: things happen &ldquo;in the second month&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/7/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 7:11">Genesis 7:11</a>), or &ldquo;in the seventh month, in the first day of the month&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/lev/23/24/" target="_blank" data-ref="Leviticus 23:24">Leviticus 23:24</a>) &mdash; the day we now call Rosh Hashanah, described purely by number. Only a small handful of <em>named</em> months survive from the older period, and they are Canaanite, not Babylonian: <strong>Abib</strong>, the spring month of the Exodus (&ldquo;this day came ye out in the month Abib,&rdquo; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/13/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 13:4">Exodus 13:4</a>); <strong>Zif</strong>, when Solomon began the temple (&ldquo;the month Zif, which is the second month,&rdquo; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/6/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 6:1">1 Kings 6:1</a>); <strong>Ethanim</strong>, when he dedicated it (&ldquo;the month Ethanim, which is the seventh month,&rdquo; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/8/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 8:2">1 Kings 8:2</a>); and <strong>Bul</strong>, when it was finished (&ldquo;the month Bul, which is the eighth month,&rdquo; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1ki/6/38/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Kings 6:38">1 Kings 6:38</a>). Notice how the narrator gently glosses even these &mdash; &ldquo;which is the second month&rdquo; &mdash; as if the number, not the name, is the thing the reader is sure to know.</p>

<p>After Babylon, everything changed. The Jews adopted the <strong>Babylonian month-names still in use today</strong>: Nisan, Iyar, Sivan, <strong>Tammuz</strong>, Av, Elul, Tishrei, Marcheshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat, and Adar. And you can watch the shift happen in the text itself, because the post-exilic books suddenly start using these very names. Nehemiah dates his cupbearing to &ldquo;the month <strong>Nisan</strong>&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/2/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 2:1">Nehemiah 2:1</a>), and Esther explains for her readers that &ldquo;the first month&rdquo; is now &ldquo;the month Nisan&rdquo; and &ldquo;the twelfth month&rdquo; is &ldquo;the month <strong>Adar</strong>&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/est/3/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Esther 3:7">Esther 3:7</a>) &mdash; the very gloss reversed, name explaining number. Nehemiah also dates events to &ldquo;the month <strong>Chisleu</strong>&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/neh/1/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Nehemiah 1:1">Nehemiah 1:1</a>), the Kislev of Hanukkah, as does Zechariah (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/zec/7/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Zechariah 7:1">Zechariah 7:1</a>), who elsewhere dates a vision to &ldquo;the eleventh month, which is the month <strong>Sebat</strong>&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/zec/1/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Zechariah 1:7">Zechariah 1:7</a>) &mdash; Shevat. The Jewish tradition itself is completely candid about where these names came from. The Jerusalem Talmud (<em>Rosh Hashanah</em> 1:2) states it plainly: <strong>the names of the months came up with them from Babylon.</strong></p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The name in the list:</strong> look again at that roster of adopted months and one entry should stop you &mdash; <em>Tammuz</em>. Tammuz was not a neutral label; he was a Babylonian god, the dying-and-rising deity of the seasons, and his mourning rites were among the abominations Ezekiel was shown at the very temple gate: &ldquo;there sat women weeping for <strong>Tammuz</strong>&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/eze/8/14/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezekiel 8:14">Ezekiel 8:14</a>). Within a generation of that vision, the returning exiles were dating their own summers by a month named for him &mdash; and the Jewish calendar has carried the name of Ezekiel&rsquo;s pagan grief, unremarked, ever since.</p>

<h3>The Irony, and the Honest Caution</h3>

<p>There is a small, careful Book of Mormon note to make here, and it is worth making precisely. Lehi&rsquo;s family left Jerusalem around 600 BC &mdash; <em>before</em> the Babylonian month-names were adopted into Jewish use. And the Nephite record, cut off from that later development, never picks up the Babylonian names; it keeps counting time the older way, by regnal years, by the years since Lehi left Jerusalem, and by event-anchored reckoning rather than by Nisan and Tammuz and Adar. It is a modest point, not a proof of anything grand &mdash; but it is a quiet, internally consistent witness that the record&rsquo;s roots really do lie in the <em>pre-exilic</em> Israelite way of keeping time, on the far side of the Babylonian shift (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/04-religion-and-mythology/">Section 04</a>).</p>

<p>Why did pre-exilic Israel prefer bare numbers in the first place? One reading &mdash; and it deserves to be flagged as <strong>a reasonable interpretation rather than an established fact</strong> &mdash; connects the habit to a command: &ldquo;make no mention of the name of other gods, neither let it be heard out of thy mouth&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/exo/23/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="Exodus 23:13">Exodus 23:13</a>). If the names of pagan deities were not to cross your lips, then a month called simply &ldquo;the seventh&rdquo; is safer ground than a month called Tammuz. On this view the numbering was a deliberate theology of time, a small daily act of not naming the gods. It is an attractive idea and it may be true &mdash; but we cannot prove it was the motive; the plainer numbering may simply be older and more prosaic. Either way, the contrast with the post-exilic calendar is real.</p>

<p>And here is where honesty cuts toward us rather than away. It would be easy to shake a head at the exiles for keeping &ldquo;Tammuz&rdquo; &mdash; until you look at your own week. The <strong>seven-day planetary week is not, strictly, Babylonian at all; it is Greco-Roman</strong>, assembled in the Hellenistic world from the seven classical &ldquo;planets,&rdquo; each carrying a god&rsquo;s name that had itself been handed down Babylon &rarr; Greece &rarr; Rome. Rome named the days for its gods, and when the Germanic peoples borrowed the scheme they simply swapped in their own: <strong>Tuesday</strong> for Tiw, <strong>Wednesday</strong> for Woden, <strong>Thursday</strong> for Thor, <strong>Friday</strong> for Frigg &mdash; with <strong>Saturday</strong> still openly wearing Saturn&rsquo;s name. The months are no better: <strong>January</strong> for two-faced Janus, <strong>March</strong> for Mars the war-god, and <strong>July</strong> and <strong>August</strong> for the deified emperors Julius and Augustus, whose names we speak as easily as breathing.</p>

<p>Put it side by side and the arithmetic is uncomfortable. The post-exilic Jewish calendar carries essentially <em>one</em> god&rsquo;s name, Tammuz, an inheritance the tradition never hid and openly credited to Babylon. Our own everyday calendar is saturated with gods and god-kings &mdash; a pagan deity in most days of the week and several months of the year &mdash; and we invoke them without a flicker of thought. Said as generously as it should be said: the exiles who kept &ldquo;Tammuz&rdquo; deserve far less of our judgment than the modern speaker who says &ldquo;Thursday&rdquo; and never once hears Thor. Babylon taught the world to count time with a brilliance we still rely on every hour. It also taught the world to <em>name</em> time after gods &mdash; and on that second lesson, the students who learned it best were not the returning Jews, but us.</p>
