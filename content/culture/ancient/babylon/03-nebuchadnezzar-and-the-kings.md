<h2 style="text-align: center;">Nebuchadnezzar &amp; the Kings of Babylon</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">The men behind the names in Kings, Jeremiah, and Daniel</p>

<p>Scripture gives Babylon&rsquo;s kings some of the Old Testament&rsquo;s most vivid scenes &mdash; envoys bearing flattering letters, a golden image on the plain of Dura, a hand writing on a palace wall. Babylonian records give the same men building inscriptions, chronicles, ration lists, and contracts. This page puts the two portraits side by side. Remarkably often, they agree &mdash; sometimes down to the day.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Merodach-baladan: The Rebel Who Came Calling (r. 721&ndash;710, 703 BC)</h3>

<p>The first Babylonian king to step onto the Bible&rsquo;s stage was not an emperor at all, but a survivor. <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Merodach-Baladan/" target="_blank">Marduk-apla-iddina II</a></strong> &mdash; the Bible&rsquo;s Merodach-baladan &mdash; was chieftain of Bit-Yakin, the greatest of the Chaldean tribes of the southern marshes. Twice he seized the throne of Babylon out from under Assyria: for over a decade beginning in 721 BC, and again briefly in 703. Between and after his reigns he did what marsh chieftains do &mdash; vanished into the reeds, out of Assyria&rsquo;s reach, to organize the next round.</p>

<p>His embassy to Hezekiah (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/20/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 20:12-19">2 Kings 20:12&ndash;19</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/39/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 39:1">Isaiah 39</a>) belongs to this lifelong project. Officially the envoys came with letters and a gift for a king recovering from illness; in reality, a rebel against Assyria was taking inventory of a potential western ally &mdash; and Hezekiah showed him everything: &ldquo;the silver, and the gold, and the spices&hellip; there was nothing in his house, nor in all his dominion, that Hezekiah shewed them not&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/20/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 20:13">2 Kings 20:13</a>). Isaiah&rsquo;s response is one of prophecy&rsquo;s long arcs: everything the envoys had seen would one day be carried to Babylon, along with royal sons to serve in its palace (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/20/17/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 20:17-18">2 Kings 20:17&ndash;18</a>). At the time, the words must have sounded almost absurd &mdash; Babylon was the hunted party, Assyria the hunter. A century later, Daniel and his companions stood in Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s court (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/1/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 1:3-4">Daniel 1:3&ndash;4</a>).</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>One king, two spellings:</strong> the King James Version calls him <em>Berodach</em>-baladan in 2 Kings 20:12 and <em>Merodach</em>-baladan in Isaiah 39:1. The Isaiah form matches his Akkadian name &mdash; <em>Marduk-apla-iddina</em>, &ldquo;Marduk has given an heir&rdquo; &mdash; and the Kings form likely reflects an ancient copyist&rsquo;s slip between two similar Hebrew letters (<em>b</em> and <em>m</em>). It is a tiny, humanizing glimpse of how carefully &mdash; and how humanly &mdash; these texts were transmitted.</p>
</div>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Nabopolassar: The Founder (r. 626&ndash;605 BC)</h3>

<p>Of the man who founded the Neo-Babylonian empire we know surprisingly little &mdash; he tells us himself that he was &ldquo;the son of a nobody,&rdquo; a general of Chaldean stock who took Babylon&rsquo;s throne in 626 BC as Assyria&rsquo;s grip failed. What followed was sixteen years of grinding warfare up the Tigris and Euphrates, in alliance with the Medes, ending in the destruction of the Assyrian capitals &mdash; Assur in 614, <strong>Nineveh in 612</strong> &mdash; and the extinction of the world&rsquo;s first great empire. The prophet Nahum&rsquo;s entire book is a thunderclap over Nineveh&rsquo;s fall; Zephaniah saw it coming too (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/zep/2/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="Zephaniah 2:13">Zephaniah 2:13</a>).</p>

<p>Nabopolassar&rsquo;s own inscriptions strike a note rare among ancient conquerors: he presents the war not as glory-seeking but as Marduk&rsquo;s justice on Assyria for its sacrileges against Babylon. He spent his last years rebuilding what Assyria had broken &mdash; including beginning the restoration of the great ziggurat Etemenanki (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/06-the-city-itself/">Section 06</a>) &mdash; while his crown prince ran the army. He died in August 605 BC, weeks after that crown prince&rsquo;s victory at Carchemish changed the map of the world.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Nebuchadnezzar II: The Empire in One Man (r. 605&ndash;562 BC)</h3>

<p><strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Nebuchadnezzar_II/" target="_blank">Nebuchadnezzar</a></strong> &mdash; Akkadian <em>Nabu-kudurri-usur</em>, &ldquo;O Nabu, protect my heir&rdquo; &mdash; ruled for forty-three years and left a deeper mark on scripture than any foreign king: he appears by name well over a hundred times, more than any other Gentile ruler. Judah knew him first as a general (Carchemish, 605 BC), then as a tax collector, then as a destroyer, and finally &mdash; in Daniel &mdash; as something stranger: a tyrant God insisted on teaching.</p>

<h4>The general and the destroyer</h4>

<p>His own chronicle for 597 BC reads almost like a verse of Kings: in his seventh year, the king of Akkad mustered his troops, marched to Hatti-land, <em>&ldquo;encamped against the city of Judah,&rdquo;</em> captured it on the second day of the month Adar (March 16, 597 BC by our calendar), seized its king, appointed a king of his own choosing, and took its heavy tribute back to Babylon. That is 2 Kings 24:10&ndash;17 told from the other side of the siege wall &mdash; the deposed king Jehoiachin, the appointed king Zedekiah, agreeing in every particular (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/08-babylon-and-the-bible/">Section 08</a> for the full text-against-text comparison). A decade later, after Zedekiah&rsquo;s Egyptian-backed revolt, Nebuchadnezzar returned and ended everything: an eighteen-month siege, the walls breached, the temple burned in the summer of 586 BC, and the king of David&rsquo;s line forced to watch his sons die before his eyes were put out (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/25/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 25:6-7">2 Kings 25:6&ndash;7</a>). Jeremiah, who had counseled surrender for years, calls Nebuchadnezzar &mdash; three times &mdash; &ldquo;my servant&rdquo; in the Lord&rsquo;s own voice (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/25/9/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 25:9">Jeremiah 25:9</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/27/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 27:6">27:6</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/43/10/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 43:10">43:10</a>): the empire as instrument, never as excuse.</p>

<h4>The builder</h4>

<p>Yet in his own inscriptions Nebuchadnezzar barely mentions war. What he wanted remembered was <strong>construction</strong>: the double walls of Babylon, the Ishtar Gate and its Processional Way gleaming with glazed lapis-blue brick, the completed ziggurat Etemenanki, the vast palace complexes, temples renewed in a dozen cities, canals, and quays. Nearly every fired brick in his Babylon &mdash; and archaeologists have moved millions of them &mdash; is stamped with his name. Daniel&rsquo;s portrait of the king pacing his palace roof &mdash; &ldquo;Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for the house of the kingdom by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/4/30/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 4:30">Daniel 4:30</a>) &mdash; matches the voice of his own bricks with almost uncomfortable precision. (The city he built is toured in <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/06-the-city-itself/">Section 06</a>.)</p>

<h4>The king in Daniel</h4>

<p>The book of Daniel dwells longer inside Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s head than any Babylonian source does &mdash; the troubled dreams (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/2/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 2:1">Daniel 2</a>), the golden image and the furnace (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/3/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 3">Daniel 3</a>), and the seven years of madness that ended in the pride-broken doxology of a pagan emperor (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/4/37/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 4:37">Daniel 4:37</a>). Babylonian records preserve no account of a royal madness &mdash; though court scribes would hardly have advertised one. Intriguingly, a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment (the &ldquo;Prayer of Nabonidus,&rdquo; 4Q242) preserves a Jewish tradition of a Babylonian king &mdash; there named Nabonidus &mdash; afflicted by God for seven years and healed through a Jewish exile&rsquo;s counsel, which many scholars see as a related tradition; others defend the Daniel account as it stands, noting how well its portrait of the king&rsquo;s builder-pride fits the man&rsquo;s own words. However one resolves the historical question, the theological one is the point of the chapter, and it is stated by the king himself: &ldquo;those that walk in pride he is able to abase&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/4/37/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 4:37">Daniel 4:37</a>).</p>

<br>

<h3>Amel-Marduk: Two Years and One Act of Mercy (r. 562&ndash;560 BC)</h3>

<p>Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s son and successor reigned barely two years before he was overthrown (and likely killed) by his brother-in-law. Babylonian tradition remembered him poorly. The Bible remembers him for a single act &mdash; and chose that act as the final scene of the entire book of Kings: &ldquo;Evil-merodach king of Babylon in the year that he began to reign did lift up the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah out of prison&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/25/27/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 25:27-30">2 Kings 25:27&ndash;30</a>), gave him a seat above the other captive kings, and granted him a daily allowance for life.</p>

<p>Astonishingly, the paperwork survives. Cuneiform administrative tablets excavated from a storeroom in Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s Southern Palace &mdash; published by Ernst Weidner in 1939 &mdash; record rations of oil issued to <em>&ldquo;Yaʾu-k&#299;nu, king of the land of Yahudu&rdquo;</em> &mdash; Jehoiachin, king of Judah &mdash; and his five sons, alongside other detained royalty and craftsmen. The book of Kings ends its long tragedy on this deliberately quiet note of grace: the house of David, captive but alive, fed at the king&rsquo;s table &mdash; a seed of hope the exiles could hold, and the thread through which the royal line runs on toward the genealogy of Matthew 1 (where Jehoiachin appears as &ldquo;Jechonias&rdquo;). (More on the ration tablets in <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/07-the-exile-experience/">Section 07</a>.)</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Neriglissar and Labashi-Marduk: The Short Reigns (560&ndash;556 BC)</h3>

<p>The usurper <strong>Neriglissar</strong> (Nergal-shar-usur) was a court grandee who had married Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s daughter &mdash; and who is very probably the same &ldquo;Nergal-sharezer&rdquo; who sat among the Babylonian officials in the gate of conquered Jerusalem in 586 BC (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/39/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 39:3">Jeremiah 39:3</a>) &mdash; one of several officials in that verse whom Babylonian records independently attest (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/08-babylon-and-the-bible/">Section 08</a> for the remarkable case of Nebo-Sarsekim). He reigned four years and died; his young son <strong>Labashi-Marduk</strong> lasted only weeks before the court removed him in favor of an elderly outsider named Nabonidus. Four kings in seven years: the dynasty of Nebuchadnezzar was eating itself, exactly the instability Jeremiah had implied when he set a limit on Babylon&rsquo;s season &mdash; the nations would serve &ldquo;him, and his son, and his son&rsquo;s son, until the very time of his land come&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/27/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 27:7">Jeremiah 27:7</a>).</p>

<br>

<h3>Nabonidus &amp; Belshazzar: The Absentee and the Regent (556&ndash;539 BC)</h3>

<figure class="fg-figure">
  <img src="/images/culture/babylon/photos/nabonidus-stele-bm.jpg" alt="The stele of Nabonidus, British Museum" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, worshipping the symbols of the moon, sun, and Venus. He left the capital in the hands of his son Belshazzar. <span class="fg-credit">British Museum &middot; Wikimedia Commons (CC0)</span></figcaption>
</figure>

<p>The last king of Babylon was the strangest. <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Nabonidus/" target="_blank">Nabonidus</a></strong> was no Chaldean prince but the son of a priestess of the moon god Sin from Harran, in the old Assyrian north &mdash; already elderly at his accession, an antiquarian who excavated the foundations of ancient temples to recover their original dedications (he has been called, half-seriously, the first archaeologist). His devotion to Sin over Marduk put him on a collision course with Babylon&rsquo;s powerful priesthood; and then, inexplicably to his contemporaries, he left. For roughly ten years the king of Babylon lived at the desert oasis of <strong>Teima</strong> in northwest Arabia, while the New Year festival &mdash; which required the king&rsquo;s presence &mdash; simply could not be celebrated. In his place at the capital he left his eldest son: <strong>Belshazzar</strong>.</p>

<p>For centuries, skeptical readers treated Daniel&rsquo;s Belshazzar as a fiction &mdash; every classical historian named Nabonidus as Babylon&rsquo;s last king, and no Belshazzar appeared anywhere. Then the cuneiform archives began to speak: the <em>Nabonidus Cylinder</em> from Ur names &ldquo;Belshazzar, the eldest son, my offspring&rdquo; in the king&rsquo;s own prayer, and administrative texts show the crown prince exercising royal authority in Babylon during his father&rsquo;s long absence &mdash; a co-regency in fact if not in formal title. Daniel&rsquo;s scene even preserves the arrangement&rsquo;s odd arithmetic: Belshazzar offers the interpreter of the writing on the wall promotion to <em>third</em> ruler in the kingdom (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/5/29/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 5:29">Daniel 5:29</a>) &mdash; the highest rank actually available in a realm whose first two places were already occupied by an absent king and his regent son.</p>

<figure class="fg-figure" style="max-width:640px;">
  <img src="/images/culture/babylon/photos/nabonidus-cylinder-ur-belshazzar.jpg" alt="Clay foundation cylinder of Nabonidus from Ur, British Museum, that names Belshazzar" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The clay cylinder that answered the skeptics: a foundation record of <b>Nabonidus</b> from the moon-god&rsquo;s temple at <b>Ur</b> (c.&nbsp;540 BC), whose closing prayer asks the gods to watch over the king &ldquo;and <b>Belshazzar</b>, the eldest son, my offspring&rdquo; &mdash; the first attestation of Daniel&rsquo;s Belshazzar outside the Bible. <a href="https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabonidus-cylinder-from-ur/" target="_blank">Read the inscription &rarr;</a> <span class="fg-credit">British Museum, BM 91128 &middot; photo Jastrow, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.5)</span></figcaption>
</figure>

<p>The end came in October 539 BC. Cyrus defeated the Babylonian army at Opis on the Tigris; days later his forces entered Babylon &mdash; by the account of both the Babylonian chronicle and Cyrus&rsquo;s own cylinder, without a battle, welcomed by a priesthood and populace long alienated from their king. Daniel remembers the last night inside the palace: the profaned temple vessels, the fingers of a man&rsquo;s hand against the plaster, and the verdict &mdash; &ldquo;Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/5/27/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 5:27">Daniel 5:27</a>). &ldquo;In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/5/30/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 5:30">Daniel 5:30</a>). Nabonidus himself was captured and, per one late tradition, pensioned into exile &mdash; the fate he had, in effect, already chosen for himself years before.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>Two royal houses, one lesson:</strong> set Babylon&rsquo;s kings beside Judah&rsquo;s last kings &mdash; Jehoiakim the opportunist, Jehoiachin the three-month king, Zedekiah the weathervane &mdash; and the book of Kings&rsquo; argument comes into focus. Judah did not fall because its enemy was strong; empires had been strong before. It fell, the text insists, because its own covenant was broken (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/24/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 24:3">2 Kings 24:3</a>) &mdash; and even so, the story is threaded with mercies: a prophet at every stage, seventy years with a fixed end, and a Davidic king alive at a Babylonian table in the final verses. Judgment, in Kings, is never the last word.</p>
