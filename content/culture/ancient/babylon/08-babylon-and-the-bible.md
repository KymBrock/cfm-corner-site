<h2 style="text-align: center;">Babylon &amp; the Bible</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">Where the tablets and the text tell the same story &mdash; and where Babylon becomes more than a city</p>

<p>No event in the Old Testament is better documented from outside the Bible than Judah&rsquo;s collision with Babylon. For the patriarchs we have cultural parallels; for the Exodus, argued inferences; for David, a stele naming his dynasty. But for the last twenty years of the kingdom of Judah we have something of a different order: a <em>Babylonian state chronicle</em> narrating the capture of Jerusalem, palace records naming Judah&rsquo;s exiled king, a tax receipt naming one of the officials who sat in Jerusalem&rsquo;s gate, and letters scratched on potsherds by Judah&rsquo;s own soldiers as the end closed in. This page gathers the evidence &mdash; and then follows Babylon as it grows, inside scripture, from a city into a symbol.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Two Accounts of One Siege: 597 BC</h3>

<p>The <strong>Babylonian Chronicle</strong> series records each year of the king&rsquo;s deeds in terse officialese. The tablet covering Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s early years (British Museum, BM 21946) reaches his seventh year and reports &mdash; in its entirety for that year &mdash; a campaign to the west:</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic;">&ldquo;In the seventh year, the month of Kislev, the king of Akkad mustered his troops, marched to Hatti-land, and encamped against the city of Judah, and on the second day of the month of Adar he seized the city and captured the king. He appointed there a king of his own choice, received its heavy tribute, and sent [them] to Babylon.&rdquo;</p>
<p style="margin: 12px 0 0 0; font-size: 0.85em; color: #666;">&mdash; Babylonian Chronicle, BM 21946 (&ldquo;Jerusalem Chronicle&rdquo;), trans. after Wiseman/Grayson</p>
</div>

<p>Set that beside <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/24/10/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 24:10-17">2 Kings 24:10&ndash;17</a>: Nebuchadnezzar comes against Jerusalem; the young king Jehoiachin surrenders (&ldquo;the king was captured&rdquo;); the treasure of the house of the LORD and the king&rsquo;s house is carried off (&ldquo;its heavy tribute&rdquo;); and Babylon &ldquo;made Mattaniah his father&rsquo;s brother king in his stead, and changed his name to Zedekiah&rdquo; (&ldquo;appointed there a king of his own choice&rdquo;). Every clause matches &mdash; and the chronicle adds what no biblical author could: the date. The second of Adar in Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s seventh year converts to <strong>March 16, 597 BC</strong>. We know the day of the week the kingdom of Judah first fell (it was a Saturday). The chronicle series also fixes the battle of Carchemish to 605 BC (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/46/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 46:2">Jeremiah 46:2</a> dates it identically, by Jehoiakim&rsquo;s fourth year) and records the drawn, bloody Babylon&ndash;Egypt battle of 601 BC that explains why Jehoiakim chose that moment to rebel (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/24/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 24:1">2 Kings 24:1</a>). The surviving chronicle tablets break off before 586, so the final destruction has no Babylonian narration &mdash; but by then the pattern of agreement is established.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Last Days, In Judah&rsquo;s Own Handwriting</h3>

<p>From the Judahite side of the trenches come the <strong>Lachish letters</strong> &mdash; ostraca (ink-on-potsherd messages) excavated in the burned gate-chamber of Lachish, Judah&rsquo;s great southwestern fortress, written during Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s final invasion. In Letter 4, an officer at an outpost reports to his commander: <em>&ldquo;we are watching for the fire-signals of Lachish&hellip; for we cannot see Azekah.&rdquo;</em> <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/34/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 34:7">Jeremiah 34:7</a> freezes the same moment from inside Jerusalem: the Babylonian army fighting &ldquo;against Lachish, and against Azekah: for these defenced cities remained of the cities of Judah&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/34/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 34:7">Jeremiah 34:7</a>). In the letter, Azekah&rsquo;s signal has already gone dark. Another ostracon complains of a prophet whose words &ldquo;weaken the hands&rdquo; of the people &mdash; the very charge Jerusalem&rsquo;s officers laid against Jeremiah, in nearly the same Hebrew idiom (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/38/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 38:4">Jeremiah 38:4</a>).</p>

<p>Archaeology in Jerusalem itself has added the officials. Among bullae (clay seal impressions) excavated in the City of David&rsquo;s burned debris is one reading <strong>&ldquo;belonging to Gemaryahu son of Shaphan&rdquo;</strong> &mdash; matching &ldquo;Gemariah the son of Shaphan the scribe,&rdquo; in whose temple chamber Baruch read Jeremiah&rsquo;s scroll aloud (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/36/10/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 36:10">Jeremiah 36:10</a>) &mdash; and excavations in the same quarter have yielded bullae of <strong>Jehucal son of Shelemiah</strong> and <strong>Gedaliah son of Pashur</strong>, the pair named side by side among Jeremiah&rsquo;s adversaries (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/38/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 38:1">Jeremiah 38:1</a>). These are the fingerprints &mdash; literally, in some cases, preserved in the clay &mdash; of the minor characters of the book of Jeremiah.</p>

<h4>The official on the tax receipt</h4>

<p>The most delightful confirmation is the most recent. <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/39/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 39:3">Jeremiah 39:3</a> lists the Babylonian officials who &ldquo;sat in the middle gate&rdquo; of captured Jerusalem &mdash; a roster of odd foreign names long garbled in translation. In 2007, Assyriologist <strong>Michael Jursa</strong>, reading routine temple receipts in the British Museum, identified a tablet from 595 BC in which one <em>Nabu-&scaron;arrussu-uk&#299;n, chief eunuch</em> (rab &scaron;a-r&#275;&scaron;i), donates gold to the Esagila temple. Re-divide the Hebrew consonants of Jeremiah&rsquo;s list and there he is: <strong>&ldquo;Nebo-Sarsechim, Rab-saris&rdquo;</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/39/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 39:3">Jeremiah 39:3</a>) &mdash; same name, same title, same decade, banking at the same temple. A clerk&rsquo;s receipt for 1.5 minas of gold, filed eight years before Jerusalem fell, preserves a man whom otherwise only Jeremiah remembered. (Neriglissar, the future king, appears in the same verse &mdash; see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/03-nebuchadnezzar-and-the-kings/">Section 03</a>; and for Jehoiachin&rsquo;s ration tablets, see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/07-the-exile-experience/">Section 07</a>.)</p>

<p>None of this &ldquo;proves the Bible&rdquo; wholesale, and honest study should not claim so &mdash; the tablets are silent on prophecy, covenant, and meaning, which are scripture&rsquo;s actual concerns. What the convergence does establish is that the books of Kings and Jeremiah are, at minimum, <strong>superbly informed about the world they describe</strong> &mdash; written from real knowledge of real events, down to the names of middle managers. For readers asked to trust these books&rsquo; theological testimony, it matters that wherever their historical testimony can be checked, it checks out.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Prophets Take On the Empire</h3>

<p>Scripture&rsquo;s engagement with Babylon is not mainly archival &mdash; it is prophetic argument, running on two tracks at once:</p>

<p><strong>Babylon as instrument.</strong> Habakkuk, watching the Chaldean rise, states the scandal plainly: the LORD himself is &ldquo;raising up&rdquo; this &ldquo;bitter and hasty nation&rdquo; to judge Judah (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/hab/1/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="Habakkuk 1:6">Habakkuk 1:6</a>) &mdash; and then wrestles, for three chapters, with how a just God can use an unjust empire. Jeremiah makes it policy: resistance to Babylon is resistance to God&rsquo;s announced judgment; the yoke must be worn (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/27/8/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 27:8">Jeremiah 27:8</a>), the exile invested in (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/29/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 29:5-7">Jeremiah 29:5&ndash;7</a>), and the term is fixed: seventy years (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/25/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 25:11-12">25:11&ndash;12</a>).</p>

<p><strong>Babylon as defendant.</strong> The same prophets then turn and indict the instrument &mdash; for pride, cruelty beyond commission, and idolatry. Isaiah&rsquo;s oracles (chapters 13&ndash;14, 21, 46&ndash;47) give the empire&rsquo;s fall its enduring poetry: the taunt-song over a fallen tyrant &mdash; &ldquo;How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/14/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 14:12">Isaiah 14:12</a>), words so large that later readers heard in them the fall of Satan himself &mdash; and the watchman&rsquo;s cry that echoes to the last page of the New Testament: <strong>&ldquo;Babylon is fallen, is fallen&rdquo;</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/21/9/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 21:9">Isaiah 21:9</a>). <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/50/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 50">Jeremiah 50</a>&ndash;51 delivers the full-length verdict, then stages scripture&rsquo;s most theatrical footnote: the oracle is written in a book, carried to Babylon by the quartermaster Seraiah, read aloud by the river, tied to a stone, and sunk in the Euphrates &mdash; &ldquo;Thus shall Babylon sink&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/51/63/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 51:63-64">Jeremiah 51:63&ndash;64</a>).</p>

<p>Daniel holds the two tracks together in narrative: the empire is real, God&rsquo;s people can serve faithfully within it and even rise in it &mdash; and every chapter ends with the empire&rsquo;s throne relativized: &ldquo;the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/4/17/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 4:17">Daniel 4:17</a>). Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s dream-statue makes Babylon merely the <em>head</em> of a sequence of kingdoms, all of them destined to be crushed by &ldquo;a stone cut out without hands&rdquo; that grows to fill the earth (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/2/34/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 2:34-35">Daniel 2:34&ndash;35</a>) &mdash; a prophecy Latter-day Saints read as reaching to the Restoration and the final triumph of God&rsquo;s kingdom (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/65?lang=eng&amp;id=p2#p2" target="_blank">D&amp;C 65:2</a>).</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>From City to Symbol</h3>

<p>Because Babylon was the power that burned the temple, it became &mdash; permanently &mdash; scripture&rsquo;s name for something larger than itself: organized human pride and worldliness in opposition to God, from the tower &ldquo;whose top may reach unto heaven&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/11/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 11:4">Genesis 11:4</a>) onward. By the New Testament era, with the city itself already fading into mounds, &ldquo;Babylon&rdquo; could name Rome (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/1pe/5/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="1 Peter 5:13">1 Peter 5:13</a>), and the book of Revelation gives the symbol its apocalyptic finale &mdash; &ldquo;Babylon the great&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/rev/17/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Revelation 17:5">Revelation 17:5</a>), the world-city of luxury and persecution, whose fall reuses Isaiah&rsquo;s watchman-cry doubled (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/rev/18/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Revelation 18:2">Revelation 18:2</a>) and whose collapse is mourned by the merchants of the earth in a lament modeled on the prophets&rsquo; dirges. The Restoration inherits the symbol intact: modern revelation names the world&rsquo;s way &ldquo;Babylon, even Babylon the great, which shall fall&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/1?lang=eng&amp;id=p16#p16" target="_blank">D&amp;C 1:16</a>) and issues the exile&rsquo;s summons in reverse: &ldquo;Go ye out from Babylon&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/133?lang=eng&amp;id=p5,p7,p14#p5" target="_blank">D&amp;C 133:5, 7, 14</a>) &mdash; no longer a change of address, but a change of heart, made from within any city on earth. (Section 10 follows this thread into Latter-day Saint life; see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/10-lds-connections/">LDS Connections</a>.)</p>
