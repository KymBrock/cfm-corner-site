<h2 style="text-align: center;">The City Itself</h2>

<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">&ldquo;Is not this great Babylon, that I have built?&rdquo;</p>

<p>The Babylon the exiles saw was the largest city in the world &mdash; by several estimates the largest city the world had yet known, covering roughly 850&ndash;900 hectares (well over 2,000 acres) with a population plausibly in the low hundreds of thousands. Nothing in Judah could have prepared a deportee for it. Jerusalem in 597 BC covered perhaps a twentieth of Babylon&rsquo;s area; the villages of Judah would have fit inside a single temple precinct. The Greek visitors of the next century simply gave up on measured prose &mdash; Herodotus declared that Babylon &ldquo;surpasses in splendour any city in the known world,&rdquo; and Aristotle later grumbled that it had &ldquo;the circuit of a nation rather than a city.&rdquo; This page walks the city Nebuchadnezzar built &mdash; the one his own bricks still describe, and the one archaeology has confirmed in remarkable detail.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Walls: A Fortress the Size of a County</h3>

<p>Babylon&rsquo;s defenses were its signature. The inner city, straddling the Euphrates, was ringed by a <strong>double wall</strong> &mdash; the ancient names, lovingly preserved in the building inscriptions, were <em>Imgur-Enlil</em> (&ldquo;Enlil approved&rdquo;) and its outer companion <em>Nimitti-Enlil</em> &mdash; fronted by a broad moat fed from the river. East of the river, Nebuchadnezzar added a vast <strong>outer triangle of wall</strong> enclosing summer palaces and suburbs, so that the total fortified circuit ran to roughly 18 kilometers. The walls were mudbrick cored and fired-brick faced, set in bitumen, punctuated by towers every few dozen meters, and thick enough that ancient writers claimed chariots could turn on the rampart walk. Herodotus&rsquo;s figures (walls 90 meters high) are cheerful exaggeration, but the reality &mdash; perhaps 25 meters of wall above a water-filled moat, kilometer after kilometer &mdash; needed no exaggerating: no army of the age could storm it. That is why every conqueror of Babylon, from Cyrus onward, came through the gates rather than over the walls &mdash; and why Jeremiah&rsquo;s image of Babylon&rsquo;s &ldquo;broad walls&rdquo; being &ldquo;utterly broken&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/51/58/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 51:58">Jeremiah 51:58</a>) was, to contemporaries, the least likely sentence in prophecy.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Ishtar Gate and the Processional Way</h3>

<p>Eight great gates pierced the inner walls, each named for a god. The most magnificent &mdash; because it carried the New Year procession (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/04-religion-and-mythology/">Section 04</a>) &mdash; was the <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Ishtar_Gate/" target="_blank">Ishtar Gate</a></strong> in the north: a double gatehouse rising over 14 meters, faced entirely in <strong>glazed brick of deep lapis blue</strong>, its surfaces marching with hundreds of animals in molded relief &mdash; white-and-gold <strong>aurochs bulls</strong> for the storm god Adad and the strange composite <strong>mu&scaron;&#7723;u&scaron;&scaron;u dragon</strong> of Marduk: scaled body, lion&rsquo;s forelegs, eagle&rsquo;s talons behind, serpent&rsquo;s neck and horned head. Through the gate ran the <strong>Processional Way</strong>, a paved avenue up to 20 meters wide, its flanking walls glazed with <strong>striding lions</strong> &mdash; the beast of Ishtar &mdash; sixty on a side, white-maned and yellow-maned on blue. Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s dedication survives, boasting that he made the gate&rsquo;s splendor &ldquo;a wonder for all peoples to behold.&rdquo;</p>

<p>He turned out to be right in a way he could not have imagined: excavated brick by glazed brick by Robert Koldewey&rsquo;s German expedition (1899&ndash;1917) and reassembled in Berlin, the Ishtar Gate today fills a museum hall two stories high, and it remains the single most overwhelming survival of the biblical world in any museum on earth (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/09-major-sites-and-discoveries/">Section 09</a>). The blue that awed the exiles still saturates the room. When Ezekiel &mdash; a deportee of 597 &mdash; describes the divine throne vision &ldquo;as the appearance of a sapphire stone&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/eze/1/26/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezekiel 1:26">Ezekiel 1:26</a>) and later sees idolatrous images &ldquo;pourtrayed upon the wall&rdquo; in vermilion, &ldquo;after the manner of the Babylonians of Chaldea&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/eze/23/14/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezekiel 23:14-15">Ezekiel 23:14&ndash;15</a>), he is speaking as a man who had walked past exactly this kind of glazed magnificence.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Etemenanki: The Tower Behind the Tower</h3>

<p>South of the Ishtar Gate, inside its own vast walled precinct, stood the structure that dominated the skyline and the imagination of the ancient world: <strong>Etemenanki</strong>, &ldquo;House of the Foundation-Platform of Heaven and Earth&rdquo; &mdash; the ziggurat of Marduk. A cuneiform document (the &ldquo;Esagila tablet&rdquo;) and the excavated foundations agree on its base: a square of about <strong>91 meters on a side</strong>, with a height in the same range &mdash; a stepped mountain of mudbrick sheathed in fired brick, mounted by a monumental triple stairway, crowned with a high temple whose bricks were glazed blue to dissolve into the sky. Ruined by the neglect and violence of the Assyrian century, it was rebuilt by Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar with imperial thoroughness &mdash; the king records that he made its head &ldquo;rival the heavens.&rdquo;</p>

<p>The phrase should sound familiar. Genesis 11 describes a tower on the plain of Shinar &ldquo;whose top may reach unto heaven&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/11/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 11:4">Genesis 11:4</a>), built of burned brick with bitumen for mortar (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/11/3/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 11:3">Genesis 11:3</a>) &mdash; exactly the materials, exactly the plain, and exactly the boast. Scholars from Andrew George (the leading modern authority on the tower&rsquo;s textual record) onward broadly agree that the <strong>Tower of Babel narrative has Etemenanki and its tradition in view</strong>: a real, datable, excavated building whose own theology &mdash; a man-made mountain &ldquo;with its head in heaven,&rdquo; built to make a name &mdash; Genesis answers with devastating irony (the LORD must &ldquo;come down&rdquo; even to see it, <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/11/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 11:5">Genesis 11:5</a>). Readers differ on the relationship&rsquo;s direction &mdash; whether Genesis 11 preserves the ancient memory that such tower-building embodied, or was shaped as a polemic against the rebuilt tower the exiles saw &mdash; but the target is not in doubt: the tower of the Babel story is Babylon&rsquo;s tower, and its name-pun (<em>Babel</em>/&ldquo;confusion,&rdquo; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/11/9/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 11:9">Genesis 11:9</a>) is aimed at the city&rsquo;s own proud name, &ldquo;Gate of the Gods&rdquo; (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/01-the-land-between-the-rivers/">Section 01</a>).</p>

<p>At the ziggurat&rsquo;s foot sprawled <strong>Esagila</strong>, &ldquo;House whose Head is Raised,&rdquo; Marduk&rsquo;s temple proper &mdash; the Vatican of Mesopotamia, its inner shrine reportedly lavished with tons of gold. It was here that captured temple treasures were deposited, following ancient custom (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ch/36/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Chronicles 36:7">2 Chronicles 36:7</a>); the vessels of the LORD&rsquo;s house sat in &ldquo;the house of his god&rdquo; until the night a regent served wine in them (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/5/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 5:2-3">Daniel 5:2&ndash;3</a>) &mdash; and until Cyrus sent them home (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/ezr/1/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezra 1:7">Ezra 1:7</a>).</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>Palaces, the Bridge, and the Museum of Empires</h3>

<p>Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s <strong>Southern Palace</strong> filled the corner between the Processional Way and the river &mdash; five courtyards deep, containing a throne room 52 meters long whose fa&ccedil;ade was glazed blue and patterned with palmettes and stylized trees (this fa&ccedil;ade, too, is reconstructed in Berlin). Somewhere in this complex the young Judean nobles of Daniel 1 were schooled; in its storerooms Koldewey&rsquo;s team found the ration tablets naming <strong>Jehoiachin, king of Judah</strong> (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/07-the-exile-experience/">Section 07</a>); in its throne room, presumably, an unnerved court watched a hand write on plaster (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/5/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 5:5">Daniel 5:5</a> &mdash; the text notes, with an eyewitness&rsquo;s eye for lamplight on glazed and whitewashed walls, that the hand wrote &ldquo;upon the plaister of the wall of the king&rsquo;s palace, over against the candlestick&rdquo;); and in 323 BC, in this same palace, Alexander the Great died. The kings of Babylon also kept what can only be called a museum: excavators found, gathered in and near the palace, antiquities of vanished empires &mdash; ancient statues, inscribed monuments of Akkadian kings, trophies dedicated over fifteen centuries &mdash; curated by rulers who knew precisely how old their city&rsquo;s claim to glory was.</p>

<p>The city&rsquo;s engineering ran right through the river: a <strong>permanent bridge on stone-and-timber piers</strong> &mdash; among the earliest attested over a great river anywhere &mdash; linked the two halves of Babylon, its piers boat-shaped against the current, exactly where Koldewey found them. Streets ran on a rough grid; houses turned blank walls to the street around cool internal courtyards; and the whole city, in Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s telling, was one continuous act of piety &mdash; his inscriptions inventory temples restored in Babylon and its sister cities the way Kings inventories Solomon&rsquo;s works.</p>

<h3>The Hanging Gardens: A Wonder in the Wrong City?</h3>

<p>And the famous gardens? Classical authors listed the <strong>Hanging Gardens of Babylon</strong> among the Seven Wonders &mdash; terraces of trees raised on vaulted galleries, watered by hidden machinery, built (the story went) by a king to console a queen homesick for mountains. The puzzle is that the abundant Babylonian records of Nebuchadnezzar&rsquo;s building &mdash; a king who itemized everything &mdash; never mention them, Herodotus never mentions them, and excavation has produced no agreed trace. Assyriologist <strong>Stephanie Dalley</strong> has argued in detail that the wonder belongs to a different king and city altogether: <strong>Sennacherib&rsquo;s palace garden at Nineveh</strong>, which the king himself describes as a &ldquo;wonder for all peoples,&rdquo; raised on vaults and watered by bronze water-raising screws &mdash; with later tradition transferring the marvel to the more famous name of Babylon. Others maintain the traditional attribution with an unexcavated riverside location. It is an honest open question &mdash; and a useful reminder, for readers of scripture and history alike, that &ldquo;everyone knows&rdquo; is not evidence, and that the spade and the tablet keep the right to surprise us.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The view from the wall:</strong> stand a Judean exile on the Processional Way in 570 BC &mdash; blue-glazed lions to the horizon, the ziggurat &ldquo;rivaling heaven,&rdquo; the walls that could not fall, the temple treasuries of a dozen conquered nations. Every visible fact argued that Marduk had won and the LORD of a burned hilltop temple had lost. The prophets&rsquo; astonishing wager was to read the skyline backwards: <em>this</em> &mdash; all of it &mdash; is the thing that passes. &ldquo;And Babylon, the glory of kingdoms, the beauty of the Chaldees&rsquo; excellency, shall be as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/13/19/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 13:19">Isaiah 13:19</a>). Within a lifetime the gates stood open to a Persian; within centuries the city was mounds. The exiles who believed the prophets over their own eyes are the reason there was a people left to come home.</p>
