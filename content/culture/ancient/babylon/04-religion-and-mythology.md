<p>When the exiles of Judah arrived in Babylonia, they entered the most religiously saturated landscape on earth &mdash; a civilization that had been worshipping, cataloguing, feeding, clothing, and consulting its gods for over two thousand years. Understanding that world matters for reading the Bible&rsquo;s exile-era books: the prophets&rsquo; polemics against idols, Daniel&rsquo;s contests with the &ldquo;wise men,&rdquo; and Israel&rsquo;s own hard-won clarity about the God who is not a statue were all forged against this backdrop.</p>

<br>

<hr>

<br>

<h3>The Pantheon: A Bureaucracy of Heaven</h3>

<p>A Babylonian name was not a mere label but a tiny sentence &mdash; a god&rsquo;s name wrapped around a request &mdash; which made royal names miniature prayers to this pantheon: <em>Nabu-kudurri-usur</em> (Nebuchadnezzar), &ldquo;O Nabu, protect my heir&rdquo;; <em>Bel-shar-usur</em> (Belshazzar), &ldquo;O Bel, protect the king.&rdquo; The exiles&rsquo; captors renamed Daniel and his friends into this same theology &mdash; Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, Abed-nego (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/1/7/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 1:7">Daniel 1:7</a>) &mdash; names invoking Babylon&rsquo;s gods pressed onto young men who pointedly declined to invoke them.</p>

<p>Marduk began as the modest patron of a modest town. As Babylon rose, so did he &mdash; absorbing the offices and epithets of older gods until, by the later second millennium BC, Babylonian theology crowned him king of the entire pantheon. The classic statement of that kingship is the epic modern scholars call the <strong><a href="https://www.worldhistory.org/Enuma_Elish---The-Babylonian-Epic-of-Creation---Fu/" target="_blank">En&#363;ma Eli&scaron;</a></strong> (&ldquo;When on high&hellip;&rdquo;, from its opening words), often labeled the &ldquo;Babylonian creation epic&rdquo; &mdash; though it is really an enthronement text: creation is the evidence, Marduk&rsquo;s kingship the verdict.</p>

<p>The story, across seven tablets: the primeval waters &mdash; fresh-water Apsu and salt-water <strong>Tiamat</strong> &mdash; mingle and beget the gods; the younger gods&rsquo; noise provokes a war; when Tiamat, the ocean-mother turned monster, marshals an army of dragons, only young Marduk dares face her &mdash; and names his price: supreme kingship. He kills Tiamat with wind and arrow, <em>splits her carcass in two, and builds the cosmos from it</em> &mdash; half her body arched as the sky, holding back the upper waters; the Tigris and Euphrates set flowing from her eyes. He organizes the stars and calendar, and then, from the blood of the rebel god Kingu, has humanity created &mdash; explicitly as a labor force, &ldquo;that the gods may rest.&rdquo; The epic closes with the gods building Marduk his city &mdash; Babylon &mdash; and reciting his fifty names of honor.</p>

<h4>En&#363;ma Eli&scaron; and Genesis 1</h4>

<p>Since the epic&rsquo;s discovery in the ruins of Nineveh&rsquo;s royal library (published by George Smith in 1876), readers have noticed both the echoes and the gulf between this text and Genesis 1. The points of contact are real: a beginning in watery formlessness; the Hebrew word for the &ldquo;deep&rdquo; over which God&rsquo;s spirit moves &mdash; <em>tehom</em> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h8415/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank">תְּהוֹם</a>, <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/1/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 1:2">Genesis 1:2</a>) &mdash; being the linguistic cousin of <em>Tiamat</em>; a firmament dividing waters above from waters below; and a seven-part literary structure ending in rest. Early scholarship (Hermann Gunkel&rsquo;s famous 1895 study, followed by many others) concluded Genesis borrowed from Babylon; the leading modern authority on the epic, W. G. Lambert, pushed back hard, showing the En&#363;ma Eli&scaron; is itself late and idiosyncratic rather than &ldquo;the&rdquo; standard Near Eastern creation story, and that direct literary borrowing is unlikely. Most scholars today speak instead of Genesis engaging a <em>shared ancient cosmological vocabulary</em> &mdash; and using it polemically.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The differences are the sermon.</strong> Read against Babylon&rsquo;s epic, Genesis 1 is quietly devastating. Creation is no war-prize: there is no cosmos wrenched from a slain god, no rival God must conquer to build it &mdash; God speaks, and it is so. The sea is not a slain goddess but a creature, gathered and bounded on command. The sun and moon &mdash; mighty gods in Babylon &mdash; are not even named in Genesis; they are &ldquo;the greater light&rdquo; and &ldquo;the lesser light,&rdquo; lamps hung by their Maker (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/1/16/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 1:16">Genesis 1:16</a>). And humanity is not an afterthought brewed from rebel blood to relieve the gods of chores, but the summit of creation, made in God&rsquo;s own image and blessed (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/1/27/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 1:27">Genesis 1:27</a>). For Latter-day Saints, who read creation as the founding act of a plan centered on human beings as God&rsquo;s children (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/1?lang=eng&amp;id=p39#p39" target="_blank">Moses 1:39</a>), the contrast could hardly be sharper.</p>
</div>

<h3>The Flood Before the Flood Story</h3>

<p>Babylon also told of a great deluge &mdash; and told it long before any part of the Bible was written down. In the <strong>Atrahasis epic</strong> (Old Babylonian, c. 17th century BC), the gods send a flood to silence noisy humanity, but Ea warns one man to build a boat; in <strong>Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh</strong> &mdash; the tablet whose 1872 decipherment by George Smith famously stunned Victorian London &mdash; the flood hero Utnapishtim tells his story: precise boat-building instructions, the animals loaded, the storm so terrible the gods themselves cower, the grounding on a mountain, and the birds &mdash; a dove, a swallow, a raven &mdash; sent out to find land, before the survivor offers sacrifice and the gods crowd round it.</p>

  <img src="/images/culture/babylon/photos/gilgamesh-flood-tablet.jpg" alt="The Gilgamesh flood tablet (Tablet XI), British Museum" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh &mdash; the Babylonian flood story, whose 1872 decipherment by George Smith stunned Victorian London. <span class="fg-credit">British Museum, via Wikimedia Commons (CC0)</span></figcaption>
</figure>

<p>The parallels with <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/6/14/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 6-9">Genesis 6&ndash;9</a> are unmistakable and unsurprising: both traditions remember the same event-shaped story in the same landscape &mdash; a flood plain civilization that kept flood layers in its very soil and a &ldquo;mound of the flood&rdquo; in its place names. What differs, again, is the theology. Babylon&rsquo;s gods send the flood out of irritation, lie about it to each other, panic when it comes, and swarm &ldquo;like flies&rdquo; to the post-flood sacrifice because they are hungry &mdash; no one has fed them in a week. Genesis grounds the flood in moral judgment (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/6/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 6:5">Genesis 6:5</a>), narrates one sovereign God who both judges and remembers (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/8/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 8:1">Genesis 8:1</a>), and ends not with divine snacking but with covenant: a bow set in the cloud (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/9/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 9:13">Genesis 9:13</a>). Latter-day Saint readers, who accept revealed knowledge of these events reaching back to the patriarchs, need not be troubled that Mesopotamia remembered a flood too &mdash; a real event leaves many memories, and the differences show which tradition remembered its meaning.</p>

<h3>Feeding the Gods: Temples, Statues, and the Akitu Festival</h3>

<p>Babylonian worship was, at its core, <strong>statue care</strong>. The god was understood to be really (if mysteriously) present in the cult image &mdash; a wooden core plated with gold, dressed in garments, crowned, and enthroned in a dark inner shrine. Twice daily the statue was served full meals behind a curtain; it was washed, clothed, perfumed, entertained with music, and on festival days carried in procession so the public could see its face. A newly made statue underwent the <em>m&#299;s p&icirc;</em> &mdash; &ldquo;washing of the mouth&rdquo; &mdash; a ritual held to transform the artifact into a living vessel of the god; the incantations themselves protest, revealingly, that the statue was made &ldquo;not by human hands.&rdquo;</p>

<p>The temples that housed the gods were economic institutions on a national scale &mdash; landowners, granaries, workshops, banks, and archives. Esagila, Marduk&rsquo;s temple in Babylon, commanded estates and prebends (shares in temple income) that made its priesthood a political force kings crossed at their peril &mdash; as Nabonidus learned (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/03-nebuchadnezzar-and-the-kings/">Section 03</a>). Beside each great temple rose the <strong>ziggurat</strong> &mdash; not a temple for congregations but a stairway-mountain, a pedestal connecting heaven and earth, with a small high shrine at its summit. Babylon&rsquo;s ziggurat, <strong>Etemenanki</strong> (&ldquo;House of the Foundation-Platform of Heaven and Earth&rdquo;), stood some ninety meters square at the base and figures directly in the background of Genesis 11 (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/06-the-city-itself/">Section 06</a>).</p>

<p>The high point of the religious year was the <strong>Akitu</strong>, the New Year festival, twelve days at the spring equinox in the month Nisannu (the very month name Judah&rsquo;s calendar borrowed as <em>Nisan</em>). The En&#363;ma Eli&scaron; was recited before Marduk&rsquo;s statue; the gods of other cities &mdash; Nabu foremost &mdash; came to Babylon by barge and chariot for a divine assembly; destinies were decreed for the coming year; and in the festival&rsquo;s most extraordinary rite, the <em>king himself</em> was stripped of his regalia before Marduk, slapped across the face by the high priest, and made to declare on his knees that he had not sinned against the city &mdash; then reinvested with crown and scepter, slapped once more, hard enough (the ritual texts specify) to bring tears, which counted as a good omen. Even the emperor, one day a year, was reminded he held his throne on sufferance.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>The prophets in the idol workshop:</strong> Isaiah 44 and Jeremiah 10 read like eyewitness reports of the statue industry &mdash; the carpenter with his rule and compass, the goldsmith&rsquo;s plating, the nails so the god &ldquo;move not&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/44/13/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 44:13">Isaiah 44:13</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/10/4/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 10:4">Jeremiah 10:4</a>). And Isaiah 46 turns the Akitu procession itself upside down: instead of gods carried in triumph, &ldquo;Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth&hellip; they are a burden to the weary beast&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/isa/46/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Isaiah 46:1-4">Isaiah 46:1&ndash;4</a>) &mdash; idols hauled away as refugee baggage, against the God who says, in the same breath: <em>I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you.</em> Babylon&rsquo;s gods must be carried. Israel&rsquo;s God carries.</p>
</div>

<h3>Reading the Future: Divination and the &ldquo;Wise Men&rdquo;</h3>

<p>No civilization ever invested more in knowing the future. The Babylonians held that the gods wrote their intentions into the fabric of the world &mdash; into sheep livers, birth anomalies, oil on water, smoke, dreams, and above all the night sky &mdash; and that trained experts could read the script. The result was divination as a national science: enormous omen compendia (the celestial series <em>En&#363;ma Anu Enlil</em> ran to some seventy tablets), clay models of livers for training diviners, and centuries of systematically recorded astronomical observation that &mdash; ironically &mdash; laid real foundations for astronomy itself (see <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/05-writing-and-learning/">Section 05</a>).</p>

<p>Ezekiel gives a field report of the method: &ldquo;the king of Babylon stood at the parting of the way&hellip; he made his arrows bright, he consulted with images, he looked in the liver&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/eze/21/21/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezekiel 21:21">Ezekiel 21:21</a>) &mdash; Nebuchadnezzar deciding, by lot, liver, and teraphim, whether to march first on Jerusalem or on Rabbah. Daniel&rsquo;s &ldquo;magicians, and the astrologers, and the sorcerers, and the Chaldeans&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/2/2/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 2:2">Daniel 2:2</a>) are the guilds of this establishment &mdash; and the book&rsquo;s recurring joke at their expense is precise: the world&rsquo;s most sophisticated future-reading industry cannot tell the king his own dream, because &ldquo;there is a God in heaven that revealeth secrets&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/dan/2/28/" target="_blank" data-ref="Daniel 2:28">Daniel 2:28</a>) &mdash; and He is not on the payroll. Israel&rsquo;s law had banned these arts centuries earlier (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/deu/18/10/" target="_blank" data-ref="Deuteronomy 18:10-12">Deuteronomy 18:10&ndash;12</a>), and Manasseh&rsquo;s embrace of them &mdash; &ldquo;observed times, and used enchantments&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/21/6/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 21:6">2 Kings 21:6</a>) &mdash; is listed among the sins that sealed Judah&rsquo;s fate.</p>

<p>Alongside the great state religion ran a humbler layer: incantations against demons (the night-hag Lamashtu, the wind-demon Pazuzu), protective amulets, household rituals, and personal gods &mdash; minor divine patrons who represented an individual before the great gods. Babylonian prayer literature can be genuinely moving; the laments of the righteous sufferer in <em>Ludlul b&#275;l n&#275;meqi</em> (&ldquo;I will praise the lord of wisdom&rdquo;) walk some of Job&rsquo;s ground centuries before Job &mdash; though, tellingly, the sufferer&rsquo;s question is never <em>whether</em> the god is just, only <em>which ritual was missed</em>.</p>

<h3>What the Exile Cured &mdash; and What It Didn&rsquo;t</h3>

<p>Judah went into Babylon a people repeatedly seduced by exactly this religious world &mdash; high places, host-of-heaven worship on the rooftops (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/zep/1/5/" target="_blank" data-ref="Zephaniah 1:5">Zephaniah 1:5</a>; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/2ki/23/12/" target="_blank" data-ref="2 Kings 23:12">2 Kings 23:12</a>), Ishtar-cakes for &ldquo;the queen of heaven&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/44/17/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 44:17">Jeremiah 44:17</a>). It came home changed. Seeing the gods of the empire up close &mdash; fed by cooks, carried by porters, helpless before their own king&rsquo;s neglect &mdash; turned the prophets&rsquo; satire into settled national conviction: &ldquo;The gods that have not made the heavens and the earth, even they shall perish from the earth&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/jer/10/11/" target="_blank" data-ref="Jeremiah 10:11">Jeremiah 10:11</a>) &mdash; a verse, fittingly, written in Aramaic, the language of the empire, as if for the neighbors to read. The old wholesale idolatry &mdash; the bowing to graven images &mdash; lost its grip.</p>

<br>

<p>But &ldquo;cured&rdquo; would overstate it. The exile <em>curbed</em> Israel&rsquo;s idolatry; it did not scrub the empire off entirely. Babylon rode home in the very reckoning of time: the returning exiles adopted the Babylonian month-names the Jewish calendar still keeps &mdash; and one of them is <strong>Tammuz</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/eze/8/14/" target="_blank" data-ref="Ezekiel 8:14">Ezekiel 8:14</a>), the dying-and-rising god whose worshippers Ezekiel had watched weeping at the gate of the Temple itself. A people finished with feeding statues still carried home, in subtle and small ways, a memory of the empire they never entirely shook &mdash; a thread picked up in <a href="/culture/ancient/babylon/05b-the-calendar-and-the-names-of-time/">The Calendar and the Names of Time</a>.</p>
