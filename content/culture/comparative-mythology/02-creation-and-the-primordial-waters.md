<p style="text-align: center; color: #666; font-style: italic; margin-bottom: 2rem;">Two nations looked into the same dark water. One saw an enemy to be killed. The other saw a creature that had been made.</p>

<p>Every ancient creation story has to begin somewhere &mdash; and a surprising number of them begin in the <strong>same place</strong>: not with solid ground, but with water. A dark, formless, boundless sea, older than the world, out of which everything else is somehow drawn. Babylon began there. Egypt began there. And so, strikingly, does <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/1/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 1:1-2">Genesis&nbsp;1</a>.</p>

<p>This is the first and clearest place to test how this guide reads a resemblance. The shared image &mdash; the primordial deep &mdash; is real, and it is not a coincidence. But the moment you set the accounts side by side and read the <em>actual words</em>, the likeness and the difference arrive together. Genesis is not repeating Babylon. As we will see, it is answering it.</p>

<br>
<hr>
<br>

<h3>The World Genesis&rsquo; First Readers Knew</h3>

<p><span style="background: #eef1ee; padding: 1px 7px; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.82em; font-weight: 600; color: #4a6b52; letter-spacing: 0.02em;">RECONSTRUCTION</span></p>

<p>The great Babylonian creation epic is the <em>Enūma Eliš</em> &mdash; named, like Genesis, for its opening words: <em>&ldquo;When on high.&rdquo;</em> It survives on seven clay tablets recovered from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, and it opens before anything has a name:</p>

<blockquote style="border-left: 4px solid var(--sage-dark, #4a6b52); padding: 12px 20px; margin: 20px 0; background: #fafaf8;">
<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic;">When in the height heaven was not named,<br>
And the earth beneath did not yet bear a name,<br>
And the primeval Apsû, who begat them,<br>
And chaos, Tiamat, the mother of them both,&mdash;<br>
Their waters were mingled together,<br>
And no field was formed, no marsh was to be seen&hellip;</p>
<p style="margin: 10px 0 0 0; font-size: 0.85em; color: #666;">&mdash; <em>Enūma Eliš</em>, Tablet I.1&ndash;6, trans. L.W. King (1902)</p>
</blockquote>

<p>Before the world exists, there are two waters: <strong>Apsû</strong>, the fresh water beneath the earth, and <strong>Tiamat</strong>, the salt sea &mdash; and their waters are &ldquo;mingled together.&rdquo; These are not yet neutral elements. They are gods, and Tiamat especially is a person: a mother, and soon a monster. When the younger gods disturb her, she raises an army of &ldquo;monster-serpents&rdquo; and &ldquo;fierce monster-vipers&rdquo; and sets the god Kingu over them. Only the young storm-god <strong>Marduk</strong> will face her &mdash; and he does so on the condition that, if he wins, he will be made king of the gods.</p>

<p>What follows is a battle, and then something stranger. Marduk catches Tiamat in a net, drives the winds into her body, and &ldquo;pierced (her) heart&hellip; and cut off her life.&rdquo; Then he builds the world out of her corpse:</p>

<blockquote style="border-left: 4px solid var(--sage-dark, #4a6b52); padding: 12px 20px; margin: 20px 0; background: #fafaf8;">
<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic;">He split her up like a flat fish into two halves;<br>
One half of her he stablished as a covering for heaven.<br>
He fixed a bolt, he stationed a watchman,<br>
And bade them not to let her waters come forth.</p>
<p style="margin: 10px 0 0 0; font-size: 0.85em; color: #666;">&mdash; <em>Enūma Eliš</em>, Tablet IV.137&ndash;140, trans. L.W. King (1902)</p>
</blockquote>

<p>The sky, in this telling, is the upper half of a dead goddess&rsquo;s body, pegged in place with a bolt and a guard so that the waters trapped above it cannot spill back down. Marduk then measures out the deep, sets the gods in their stations, and &mdash; in the tablets that follow &mdash; fashions humanity from the blood of the slain rebel Kingu, to do the labor of the gods. Having built the cosmos, he is proclaimed king.</p>

<div style="background: #f7f6f3; padding: 16px 20px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 22px 0; font-size: 0.94em;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>A note on the translation.</strong> The public-domain rendering above is L.W. King&rsquo;s (1902), quoted so readers can follow the whole text freely online. Its load-bearing lines hold up against the standard modern edition: Stephanie Dalley&rsquo;s <em>Myths from Mesopotamia</em> (Oxford, rev. 2009) reads the same passage, <em>&ldquo;He sliced her in half like a fish for drying: Half of her he put up to roof the sky, Drew a bolt across&hellip; Her waters he arranged so that they could not escape.&rdquo;</em> One caution: King&rsquo;s line&nbsp;4 glosses Tiamat as &ldquo;chaos, Tiamat&rdquo;; the word &ldquo;chaos&rdquo; is King&rsquo;s interpretive addition &mdash; Dalley renders it plainly, &ldquo;Tiamat, who bore them all.&rdquo;</p>
</div>

<p>This was not an obscure text. In Babylon the <em>Enūma Eliš</em> was <strong>recited every New Year</strong>. The comparative-religion scholar Mircea Eliade summarized the festival this way: at the <em>akitu</em> celebration &ldquo;a series of rites re-enacted the fight between Marduk and Tiamat (the Dragon symbolizing the primordial Ocean), the victory of the God, and his cosmogonic labors. The &lsquo;Poem of Creation&rsquo; (<em>Enuma elish</em>) was recited in the Temple&rdquo; <span style="font-size: 0.85em; color: #666;">(Eliade, <em>Myth and Reality</em>, 1963, p.&nbsp;48)</span>. This is the mental furniture the first hearers of Genesis carried: the world begins in a divine sea, and order is won by killing it.</p>

<h3>The Same Deep, a Different Story</h3>

<p><span style="background: #e8eef4; padding: 1px 7px; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.82em; font-weight: 600; color: #3d5a7a; letter-spacing: 0.02em;">THE BIBLICAL TEXT</span></p>

<p>Now hear the Hebrew account, and listen for the water:</p>

<blockquote style="border-left: 4px solid #3d5a7a; padding: 12px 20px; margin: 20px 0; background: #fafaf8;">
<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic;">In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of <strong>the deep</strong>. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.</p>
<p style="margin: 10px 0 0 0; font-size: 0.85em; color: #666;">&mdash; <a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/gen/1/1/" target="_blank" data-ref="Genesis 1:1-2">Genesis 1:1&ndash;2</a> (KJV)</p>
</blockquote>

<p>&ldquo;The deep&rdquo; here is the Hebrew word <strong>təhôm</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h8415/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank" style="font-size: 1.15em;">תְּהוֹם</a>) &mdash; and it sounds, to the ear, remarkably like <strong>Tiamat</strong>. That resemblance is genuine, and it has launched a century of argument. It is also the exact place where careful reading matters most, because the easy conclusion &mdash; &ldquo;Genesis borrowed the Babylonian goddess&rdquo; &mdash; turns out to be the wrong one.</p>

<p>The technical point is worth getting right, because everything downstream depends on it. <strong>Təhôm is not a loanword from Tiamat.</strong> The Assyriologist Alexander Heidel showed on philological grounds that Hebrew <em>təhôm</em> is not derived from the Babylonian <em>Tiamat</em>; rather, both descend from a common, much older Semitic word for the sea and its depths (reconstructed roughly as <em>tihām&#8209;</em>), which also produced the Akkadian <em>tiāmtu / tâmtu</em>, &ldquo;ocean.&rdquo; The Hebrew scholar David T. Tsumura confirmed the same conclusion from the word&rsquo;s grammar: the sound-changes required to turn <em>Tiamat</em> into <em>təhôm</em> simply do not work &mdash; they are cousins, not parent and child. Notably, the Blue Letter Bible&rsquo;s lexicon traces <em>təhôm</em> to a Hebrew root (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1949/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank">hûm</a>, &ldquo;to make a noise, to surge&rdquo;) and makes no mention of the goddess at all.</p>

<p>So the two words are relatives who went very different directions. In Babylon, the deep became a <em>person</em> &mdash; a mother-goddess, a dragon, a corpse. In Genesis, the same ancestral word stays exactly what it is: <strong>water</strong>. Dark, formless, unlit &mdash; but inert. It does not fight. It does not speak. It has no army. When the account opens, the deep is simply <em>there</em>, and over it &ldquo;the Spirit of God&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h7307/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank">rûaḥ</a> אֱלֹהִים) is already &ldquo;moving&rdquo; &mdash; hovering, brooding &mdash; not in combat, but in creative readiness.</p>

<h3>Genesis as Counter-Statement</h3>

<p><span style="background: #f0ebf2; padding: 1px 7px; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.82em; font-weight: 600; color: #6b4a72; letter-spacing: 0.02em;">INTERPRETATION</span></p>

<p>Read against the <em>Enūma Eliš</em>, Genesis 1 stops sounding like a bare list of days and starts sounding like a <strong>deliberate reply</strong>. This is not a fringe reading. The Old Testament scholar Gerhard Hasel argued it in detail: the creation narrative of Genesis&nbsp;1, &ldquo;far from being dependent on the &lsquo;creation&rsquo; stories of Babylonia and other ancient Near Eastern cosmogonies, designedly polemicizes against them&rdquo; <span style="font-size: 0.85em; color: #666;">(&ldquo;The Polemic Nature of the Genesis Cosmology,&rdquo; <em>The Evangelical Quarterly</em> 46, 1974, pp.&nbsp;81&ndash;102)</span>. The likeness is the point: Genesis picks up the shared vocabulary precisely in order to turn it inside out.</p>

<p>The parallel that lands hardest is the <strong>dividing of the waters</strong>. Both texts know the picture of a watery upper region held back above the sky. In Babylon it takes a slain goddess, a bolt, and a posted guard. In Genesis it takes a sentence. The Hebrew word for that barrier is <strong>rāqîaʿ</strong> (<a href="https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h7549/kjv/wlc/0-1/" target="_blank">רָקִיעַ</a>, &ldquo;firmament&rdquo; / expanse) &mdash; rendered στερέωμα (<em>steréōma</em>, &ldquo;a firm thing&rdquo;) in the Greek Septuagint and <em>firmamentum</em> in the Latin Vulgate, from which we get the English &ldquo;firmament.&rdquo; And where God &ldquo;divided&rdquo; the waters, the verb is <em>bādal</em>, simply &ldquo;to separate.&rdquo; As Hasel puts it, in the biblical account of the separated waters &ldquo;separation takes place without struggle whatever. It is achieved by divine fiat.&rdquo;</p>

<p>This is why the technical point about <em>təhôm</em> mattered. Because the word is a cousin of Tiamat and not a borrowing, Genesis is free to use it in what Hasel calls a &ldquo;depersonalized&rdquo; and &ldquo;non-mythical&rdquo; sense &mdash; and to do so <em>pointedly</em>. The old word for the primeval sea is kept; the goddess inside it is gone. The result, in Hasel&rsquo;s phrase, is a text &ldquo;not only non-mythical in content but antimythical in purpose.&rdquo;</p>

<h3>What the Comparative Study Adds</h3>

<p><span style="background: #f0ebf2; padding: 1px 7px; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.82em; font-weight: 600; color: #6b4a72; letter-spacing: 0.02em;">INTERPRETATION &mdash; ONE LENS AMONG SEVERAL</span></p>

<p>Babylon was not alone in beginning with water. Egypt&rsquo;s Heliopolitan theology opened with <strong>Nun</strong>, the pre-existent primeval ocean out of which the creator first emerged &mdash; a &ldquo;watery chaos&rdquo; personified as a god (Hasel surveys the Egyptian material alongside the Babylonian). The picture of a formless sea that precedes and surrounds the ordered world turns up far too widely to be an accident of one culture.</p>

<p>It is exactly this recurrence that the comparative-mythology podcast <a href="https://theancienttradition.com/11-the-mysterious-primordial-waters/" target="_blank"><em>The Ancient Tradition</em></a> (Jack Logan) presses on in its episode &ldquo;The Mysterious Primordial Waters&rdquo;: that peoples across the globe described the pre-creative state as water, and that this is better read as a shared symbolic language than as many unrelated inventions. Logan leans on Eliade&rsquo;s category of <em>aquatic symbolism</em> &mdash; the sense in which water stands for the undifferentiated fullness that precedes every form.</p>

<div style="background: #faf8f5; padding: 14px 20px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 22px 0; font-size: 0.9em; border: 1px solid #ece7df;">
<p style="margin: 0;"><strong>How this guide uses that.</strong> The podcast is a <em>pointer and a lens</em>, never a source of fact. Its &ldquo;one original tradition&rdquo; thesis is a resonance to sit with, not a proven history &mdash; and the specific claims it draws from any given myth are anchored here to the primary texts above (the <em>Enūma Eliš</em> itself, Genesis in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin), not to the episode. Where the podcast reaches for grand synthesis, this page keeps to what the tablets and the text actually say.</p>
</div>

<p>Held at that distance, the comparative work earns its keep. It shows that Genesis was written into a world already soaked in the imagery of the primordial deep &mdash; which is precisely what makes its restraint so loud. The nations remembered the <em>waters</em>. Israel&rsquo;s account remembers Someone standing over them who was never born from them.</p>

<div style="background: linear-gradient(135deg, #f5f3f0 0%, #ebe7e1 100%); padding: 20px 24px; border-radius: 8px; margin: 24px 0;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 12px 0;"><strong>A Latter-day Saint reading.</strong> If, as this guide proposes, the nations carry the &ldquo;scattered fragments&rdquo; of an older worship &mdash; each granted a portion of truth &ldquo;in their own&hellip; tongue&rdquo; (<a href="https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/29?lang=eng&amp;id=p8#p8" target="_blank" data-ref="Alma 29:8">Alma 29:8</a>) &mdash; then the primordial deep is one of those fragments, remembered nearly everywhere. Babylon kept the memory of the waters but lost the God, and filled the gap with a slain goddess and a warrior-king. Genesis keeps both: the ancient dark sea, <em>and</em> the unrivaled Creator who needs no corpse to build with and no war to win, because the deep was His to begin with. The parallel does not shrink Genesis. It shows what Israel preserved that the others let slip.</p>
</div>

<p style="margin: 0 0 12px 0;"><strong>Four layers, kept honest.</strong></p>
<ul style="margin: 0; padding-left: 22px;">
<li><strong>The biblical text</strong> &mdash; Genesis 1: the deep (<em>təhôm</em>) is inert; God divides the waters by a word.</li>
<li><strong>The reconstruction</strong> &mdash; the <em>Enūma Eliš</em> (recovered at Nineveh, recited each Babylonian New Year) and Egypt&rsquo;s Nun: creation from a divine, contested sea.</li>
<li><strong>The interpretation</strong> &mdash; Genesis as a deliberate counter-statement (Hasel); the primordial-waters motif as a shared symbolic language (Eliade; and, as one labeled lens, <em>The Ancient Tradition</em>).</li>
<li><strong>The Latter-day Saint frame</strong> &mdash; a fragment of an original tradition kept whole in Israel and diluted among the nations; held as resonance, not proof.</li>
</ul>
