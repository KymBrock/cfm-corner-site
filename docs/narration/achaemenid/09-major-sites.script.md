# SCRIPT

[warmly] Let me open with a trick question. Where was the capital of the Persian Empire?

Take your time. Because whatever single city you just named — that's the wrong answer. And not because you picked badly. Because the question itself is wrong.

The Achaemenid kings didn't rule from *a* capital. They ruled from capitals, plural — a handful of great centers scattered across the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia — and the court moved among them with the seasons and with the rhythms of state. When the lowland plains warmed, the king was there. In the heat of summer, he was up in the cool of the mountains. We'll see both.

Four of those centers — plus one spectacular cliff — carry the story of Persia and the Bible. Several of them appear in Scripture by name. All of them can be visited today, most of them in Iran. And every one of them rewards knowing what happened there.

So let's take the tour.

[brightly] First stop: the highlands of Fars, and a place called Pasargadae. This was the first Achaemenid capital — founded by Cyrus the Great himself, on the very site of his victory over the Medes. The battlefield became the seat of an empire. Its scattered palaces, gardens, and columned halls introduced ideas the later capitals would magnify — including the walled royal garden the Persians called a *paradise*.

But Pasargadae's enduring monument is not a palace, and not a garden. It is something much quieter. The tomb of Cyrus.

It is a simple, dignified gabled stone chamber, raised on six broad steps, standing alone on the plain. That's it. That's all of it. And it survives largely because, centuries later, Alexander the Great — the very man who ended the dynasty — is said to have ordered it protected and restored. Honoring the founder, even as he closed the book on everything the founder built.

[thoughtfully] Now stand in front of that plain limestone tomb, and remember who is resting there. This is the man Isaiah called the LORD's "shepherd." The LORD's "anointed." The deliverer who ended the exile. No boast. No mountain of masonry. Six steps and a stone room. Its restraint is itself a kind of eloquence.

[brightly] Second stop — and brace yourself, because this one is anything *but* restrained. Persepolis. In Old Persian, *Parsa*. This was the empire's great ceremonial capital, begun by Darius the Great and expanded by Xerxes and their successors: a vast terraced complex of audience halls, palaces, and a treasury, approached by monumental stairways.

And on its walls is the most famous carving in the whole empire: the procession of subject nations, bringing tribute to the king. Medes. Elamites. Babylonians. Egyptians. Ionians. Bactrians. And more — each people in its own distinctive dress, bearing the gifts of its own lands. It is a stone catalogue of the empire's astonishing diversity. When the book of Esther describes a kingdom of "a hundred and seven and twenty provinces" — *this* is what that looks like. Carved in rock. Filing up the stairways forever.

Persepolis is where the kings celebrated the New Year and received the empire. It's also where the Persepolis Fortification tablets were found — the administrative archive we met earlier in this guide, in the section on writing and records.

And it's where the story ends. In three thirty BC, Alexander the Great burned Persepolis. Was it a drunken accident? Was it deliberate revenge for Xerxes's burning of Athens? The ancient sources themselves dispute it. Either way, its blackened ruins mark the end of the empire — the closing of the age Daniel had glimpsed as the "he goat" charging from the west.

[curious] Before you leave Persepolis, though, walk a short distance to a cliff face called Naqsh-e Rustam. Carved into it are the rock-cut tombs of Darius the Great and several of his successors — huge cross-shaped facades cut straight into the cliff, each one showing the king worshipping before a fire altar, under the winged emblem of Ahura Mazda. And Darius's tomb inscription there is one of the noblest statements of Achaemenid kingship anywhere: the king as a just ruler, who rewards the faithful and punishes the wrongdoer — holding a sprawling empire together by law.

Third stop: down out of the mountains, onto the warm lowland plains. Susa. Or, as your Bible calls it — Shushan.

[warmly] If you read the Old Testament, this is *the* Persian site. Susa was the administrative and winter capital, and it is the biblical home of the Persian court more than anywhere else on earth. The entire book of Esther is set here — "Shushan the palace." This is where Nehemiah served the king, and where the news reached him of Jerusalem's ruin. And this is where Daniel saw his vision of the ram and the he-goat, "by the river of Ulai."

And the ground has given the story back. Excavations at Susa uncovered the great columned audience hall — the *apadana* — of Darius and Xerxes, and the glazed-brick reliefs of the royal guards. The physical stage of Esther's drama. And by tradition, Susa holds one thing more: a shrine revered as the tomb of Daniel — honored by Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike.

Fourth stop: back up into the mountains — high in the Zagros this time — to Ecbatana. The Bible calls it Achmetha; today it is Hamadan. This was the old Median capital, and the Achaemenids kept it as their cool *summer* residence.

[curious] And Ecbatana earns its biblical moment in one of the most satisfying scenes in the book of Ezra. The Jews' opponents challenge their permit to rebuild the temple. Darius orders a search of the archives. And Cyrus's original decree turns up — not in Babylon, but filed away at Ecbatana. "There was found at Achmetha, in the palace… a roll, and therein was a record thus written." The exact document, in the exact kind of provincial archive, that vindicated the whole enterprise.

[thoughtfully] And notice the detail hiding inside that verse. The decree was found at the *summer* capital — which is where Cyrus would have been residing when he issued it. That is the kind of incidental precision that marks a writer who knew the empire's workings from the inside.

Last stop. On the mountain road between Babylon and Ecbatana, a cliff rises. Behistun. And on that cliff, Darius the Great carved his trilingual monument of victory.

Behistun names no biblical figure. Not one. And it still belongs on any list of the empire's great sites — for two reasons. It is the fullest statement of Achaemenid royal ideology we have. And it became the "Rosetta Stone of cuneiform" — the key that made Babylon's records readable, and reopened the writing of the entire ancient Near East to the modern world.

[warmly] So there is your map of the empire. And if you ever go looking for these places on a modern map, know that the names have shifted. Persepolis is Takht-e Jamshid. Susa is Shush. Ecbatana is Hamadan. Behistun is Bisotun. But the sites are all still there — five places, one story.

A plain stone tomb on six steps, holding the deliverer Isaiah called the LORD's anointed. A ceremonial stairway where the nations of the empire climb forever in stone — until the fire came. A winter palace where Esther's whole drama played out, and where Nehemiah wept for Jerusalem. A summer archive in the mountains, where one rolled-up decree vindicated the temple builders. And a cliff that gave the ancient world back its voice.

[thoughtfully] The Achaemenid kings moved with the seasons — mountains in the summer, plains in the winter, the court forever on the road. But for readers of the Bible, every one of their capitals sits on the same road. The road that sent Judah home.
