# SCRIPT

[warmly] Let me tell you about the fastest rise in ancient history.

When Judah went into exile — when Jerusalem burned and the temple came down — Persia was nobody. A cluster of herding and farming clans in the mountains of southwestern Iran. Subjects of their more powerful cousins, the Medes. Barely a footnote in the archives of Babylon.

And within the lifetime of a *single man*, that footnote became the master of the entire Near East — from the Aegean Sea all the way to the Indus — and the deliverer who sent Judah home.

[thoughtfully] That man was Cyrus the Great. And the empire he founded — the *Achaemenid* Empire, named for his ancestor Achaemenes — is the world that Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and the later chapters of Daniel all move within.

Here's why this matters for your scripture study. This guide is background for the return from exile — the second half of the story the Babylon guide begins. Babylon carried Judah *away*. Persia brought Judah *back*. And understanding Persia is understanding how the Old Testament ends — not in the ashes of Jerusalem, but in a rebuilt temple, a restored wall, and a Jewish queen in a foreign palace.

So. Who *were* these Persians?

[curious] Start with what they were not. They were not Babylonians — not even close. The Persians, and their close kin the Medes, were *Iranian* peoples — speakers of an Indo-European language, distantly related to Sanskrit, and Greek, and Latin — and quite unrelated to the Semitic languages of Mesopotamia and Canaan: Akkadian, Aramaic, Hebrew. Their ancestors had migrated down onto the Iranian plateau from the Eurasian steppe back in the second millennium BC — herders and horsemen, settling in among older peoples.

By the time they step into history, the Medes had built a real kingdom, centered on Ecbatana — that's modern Hamadan — and they had helped Babylon destroy Assyria in 612 BC. The Persians? They held the southern region of *Persis* — modern Fars, around Shiraz — as Median vassals. The junior partners. The little cousins in the hills.

[brightly] And here's something I love: the Bible knows this family relationship. Knows it *precisely*. It files the two peoples together from the very start — "the law of the Medes and Persians" in Daniel, "the Persians and the Medes" in Esther. And Daniel's vision of the two-horned ram makes the relationship exact: "The ram which thou sawest having two horns are the kings of Media and Persia." One horn higher than the other... and the higher one coming up *last*. Persia, the junior partner, rising to dominate the senior.

[thoughtfully] That is exactly what happened. Watch.

Cyrus the Second became king of Persis around 559 BC — still a vassal of the Median king Astyages, who, by later Greek tradition, was his own *grandfather*. Around 553, Cyrus rebelled. And in a stroke that stunned the age, the Median army defected to him — *mid-battle*. By 550 BC he had taken Ecbatana, and the whole Median empire with it. Medes and Persians, united under one crown.

[warmly] And he was only beginning.

Over the next decade, Cyrus conquered in every direction. West, to Lydia, around 547 BC — the fabulously wealthy kingdom of King Croesus, in western Anatolia. That one conquest handed Persia the Greek cities of the Aegean coast — and quietly set up the Greco-Persian wars that would come later. East, a great sweep across the Iranian plateau, out to the edges of Central Asia and the Indus.

And then... the great prize. Babylon. 539 BC.

[curious] Here's the remarkable thing about the fall of Babylon: it barely *was* a fall. The Neo-Babylonian empire was ruled by an unpopular, absentee king named Nabonidus, with his son Belshazzar as regent — and it fell to Cyrus almost without a fight. One battle, upriver, at a place called Opis... and then the greatest walled city on earth simply *opened its gates.*

And with Babylon fell everything Babylon had ruled. Including a community of exiles from a little country called Judah — who woke up one morning with a brand-new master.

[warmly] And this master, in his very *first* regnal year, issued a decree that would change their history forever: go home. Return, and rebuild the house of your God. That's Ezra chapter one.

Now pause on that, because it's the hinge of everything. Persia was a *different kind of empire*. Assyria and Babylon had ruled by terror and deportation — smashing rebellious peoples, uprooting whole populations, Judah among them, and planting them far from home. Persia, by deliberate policy, did the opposite. It let subject peoples keep their gods. Their customs. Their local leaders. And — famously — it sent the deported *home*, to rebuild their own temples.

[thoughtfully] Was that kindness? Not exactly. It wasn't sentimentality — it was a shrewd governing philosophy, one that bought loyalty cheaply across an impossibly diverse empire. But hold both truths at once. Because for one small people, that policy was *salvation*. And their scriptures never forgot it.

Which brings us to the strangest, most wonderful thing in this whole section.

The biblical text does something here that it does nowhere else. It names a pagan emperor — *by name* — and calls him the LORD's chosen instrument. In language written, on the traditional reading, generations before he was born.

Isaiah calls Cyrus "my shepherd" — who "shall perform all my pleasure." And then — astonishingly — Isaiah calls him "his anointed." The LORD's *mashiach*. [warmly] That's the Hebrew word behind "Messiah." "Whose right hand I have holden, to subdue nations before him." It is the *only* time in all of Scripture that title is applied to a Gentile.

And the Bible frames Persia's entire rise around one single purpose: "he shall build my city, and he shall let go my captives."

[curious] Now — because Isaiah 44 and 45 name Cyrus so precisely, there's a long scholarly conversation here, and it's worth being honest about it. Critical scholars have long argued that Isaiah 40 through 66 was written by a later, exile-era author — a "Second Isaiah" — writing *after* Cyrus had already appeared on the stage. Others — including many Latter-day Saint readers, and the Book of Mormon's own witness that Isaiah's words were on the brass plates Lehi carried out of Jerusalem around 600 BC — hold that this is genuine predictive prophecy.

[thoughtfully] But notice something. Either way, the theological claim the text is making is *the same* — and it is unmistakable. The rise of Persia was not an accident of history. It was the hand of God, arranging deliverance for His covenant people. That's the lens this guide reads Persia through — while presenting the historical evidence honestly, on its own terms.

[brightly] So Cyrus founded the empire. Four of his successors carry the rest of the biblical story — and it helps to meet them now. Think of this as the cast list.

Cyrus the Great — 559 to 530 BC. The decree of return. The temple foundation.

Cambyses the Second — 530 to 522. The Bible never names him — but he conquered Egypt, which is the background to the Jewish community at Elephantine.

Darius the First — 522 to 486. The temple is *completed* in his sixth year. That's the world of Ezra five and six — and the prophets Haggai and Zechariah.

Xerxes the First — 486 to 465. The Bible calls him Ahasuerus. He is the king of the book of Esther.

And Artaxerxes the First — 465 to 424 — who sends Ezra, and then Nehemiah, up to Jerusalem.

[warmly] Five kings. Five biblical books touched. And one long thread running through them all: the God of a captive people, quietly steering the greatest empire on earth toward their restoration.

[thoughtfully] From a cluster of highland clans on the edge of the map... to the instrument of the return. That's the story ahead of us in this guide. And it began with a footnote that nobody in Babylon saw coming.
