# SCRIPT

[warmly] We have spent nine sections inside the Persian Empire. Now let me tell you why, for Latter-day Saints, none of it is a footnote.

Because the Persian period is not a distant appendix to the Old Testament. It is a *working model* — a working model of how God gathers His people, how He prepares kings and nations for His purposes, and how He rebuilds what has fallen. And the Restoration reads its own mission in exactly these terms: a gathering of scattered Israel. A building of temples. A re-centering on scripture. And a divine hand moving quietly through the politics of empires.

So this final section draws the threads together. There are five of them.

[thoughtfully] Start with the boldest claim the Bible makes about Persia — that the LORD deliberately raised up a *pagan emperor* to accomplish the restoration of His covenant people. Isaiah names Cyrus "my shepherd." He calls him "his anointed." And then comes the astonishing qualifier: "though thou hast not known me." A king who did not know the God of Israel — raised up by that very God, to send Israel home.

Now listen to latter-day scripture teaching the same principle, about the founding of the modern nation that would cradle the Restoration: "I established the Constitution of this land, by the hands of wise men whom I raised up unto this very purpose."

The pattern is identical. God prepares the political conditions — a tolerant empire then, a nation of religious liberty now — so that His people can build, and worship, and gather. And He does it working through the free choices, and even the self-interest, of people who do not know Him. Cyrus's decree of return is, in this sense, an Old Testament rehearsal of the way the Lord raises up rulers and frameworks to open the door for His work.

[curious] And set Persia beside Babylon, and the lesson sharpens. Babylon's Nebuchadnezzar was called the Lord's "servant" — to *judge* Judah. Persia's Cyrus was called the Lord's "anointed" — to *restore* her. The same God who used one empire to scatter used the very next empire to gather. Sovereign over both. Bending the rise and fall of superpowers to the good of His covenant. Which is precisely the doctrine of Daniel's dream: kingdoms come and kingdoms go, but "the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will."

Second thread — and it is the single richest one. The gathering of Israel.

The tenth Article of Faith declares: "We believe in the literal gathering of Israel and in the restoration of the Ten Tribes." And the return from Babylon under Persia is the Old Testament's clearest working example of *exactly that* — a scattered, exiled people brought home by the hand of God, to rebuild their worship and their city.

The Book of Mormon frames the whole sweep of covenant history in these same terms — scattering, and gathering. The Lord God, it says, "has led away from time to time from the house of Israel, according to his will and pleasure," and He remembers "all them who have been broken off." And it promises that "the Lord will set his hand again the *second* time to restore his people."

[thoughtfully] Hear that phrase. The second time. The return under Cyrus was the *first* time — in miniature. The latter-day gathering is that same covenant faithfulness, on a global scale.

And for Latter-day Saints, this is not an abstraction. The keys that direct the latter-day gathering were restored in the Kirtland Temple, when "Moses appeared… and committed unto us the keys of the gathering of Israel from the four parts of the earth, and the leading of the ten tribes." Which means Ezra's caravans on the road from Babylon, and Nehemiah's builders on the walls of Jerusalem, are not merely ancient history. They are the ancestors — in type — of the missionary work and the temple-gathering the Restoration understands itself to be carrying out right now.

Third thread: the temple.

Because the heart of the return was not a city, and not even a homeland. It was a *temple*. Everything in the book of Ezra bends toward one goal — rebuilding the house of the Lord. And over that modest Second Temple, the prophet Haggai spoke a promise worth sitting with: "the glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former."

[warmly] That promise speaks directly to a people whose own restoration began with the command to build temples — a people whose scripture calls this work "a marvelous work and a wonder." And the parallels a Latter-day Saint teacher can draw here are natural and unforced. Opposition and discouragement, stalling the work. Prophets — Haggai and Zechariah — stirring the people to finish. Sacrifice and scarcity, given to build God's house before one's own "cieled" houses. And the tears of those who remembered the former glory, giving way to the promise of a *greater* glory to come. The Second Temple restoration is a template for every gathered people who has ever had to rebuild the house of the Lord against the odds.

Fourth thread: the word.

The exile turned Israel into a people of the book. And the return produced that people's exemplary teacher — Ezra, who "prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach." Notice the order there. Seek. Do. Teach. Ezra read the scriptures aloud to the whole assembled people, and gave "the sense," so they could understand.

[thoughtfully] Picture that scene. An entire covenant community, gathered to hear the word of God, to understand it, and to covenant around it. For a church built on scripture study — on seek, do, teach as a pattern of discipleship — on the conviction that a people are renewed when they return to the word — that scene lands very close to home. Ezra's covenant-renewal assembly is, in its way, an ancient general conference.

And the fifth thread is Esther — who teaches something none of the others do. How God works when He is *not visibly working*.

The book of Esther never names God. Not once. And yet its every turn testifies of His hidden hand. Mordecai's question to the hidden queen — "who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" — has become one of the most-quoted summons to purpose in Latter-day Saint teaching. Because it speaks to the doctrine that God places His children in particular times, particular families, particular circumstances — for reasons that matter. That we are "come to the kingdom" on purpose. And that we are asked, like Esther, for the courage to act when our moment comes.

And the deliverance her book records — the preservation of the covenant people from the first empire-wide attempt to destroy them — is one more instance of the promise. The Lord remembers "them who have been broken off." And He will not let His covenant fail.

[warmly] So if you teach this material — if you carry one thing out of this whole guide into a classroom or a family study — here are the three through-lines that hold the entire Persian period together.

One: God governs the nations. He raised up Cyrus exactly as He later raised up "wise men" for the Constitution — preparing the world for His work.

Two: scattering is never the end. The return from Babylon is the pattern of the gathering the Restoration is still carrying out — temple by temple, convert by convert.

Three: providence is often quiet. From Esther's unnamed God to Ezra's "good hand of his God upon him," this era teaches us to recognize the Lord's hand in circumstances that look, from the inside, like mere history.

[thoughtfully] Ten sections ago, we began with an empire. We end with a pattern.

Because here is the thing to hold onto. The Old Testament does not end in the ashes of exile. It ends with a decree of return. A rebuilt temple. And a people gathered home.

[warmly] And that — exactly that — is the shape of the hope the Restoration proclaims. Babylon carried Judah away. Persia carried her home. And the God who governed them both has set His hand "again the second time" — and He is gathering still.
