# SCRIPT

[warmly] Here's a strange thought to start with. Nearly everything we can read from ancient Babylon today — the chronicles, the ration tablets, the flood epic — is legible to us because one Persian king bragged about himself on a cliff. In triplicate.

Stay with me. Because this is the story of how the Persian empire wrote itself down — and how, two and a half millennia later, its writing habit handed the modern world the key to the entire ancient Near East.

The Persians wrote in several scripts and languages at once. And that habit turns out to matter enormously — for understanding the empire, and, unexpectedly, for our ability to read everything around it. The books of Ezra and Esther are steeped in this documentary world — decrees, archives, multilingual correspondence — and their accuracy about it is one more sign of how close their authors stood to the events. So let's follow the empire's writing, from the king's monumental stone down to the humble paperwork of everyday life.

[brightly] Start at the top: the king's script. For their grandest royal inscriptions, the Achaemenid kings used Old Persian cuneiform — and it is a genuine curiosity, worth pausing on. The old Mesopotamian cuneiform was a sprawling, centuries-old system of *hundreds* of signs. Old Persian cuneiform? Compact. Mostly *alphabetic*. Only about forty signs. And here's the remarkable part — it appears to have been more or less *invented*, under Darius the Great, for display. A deliberate prestige script, created for carving the king's words into stone.

It was rarely used for daily business. For that, the empire relied on Aramaic — hold that thought — and on the older scripts of its subject peoples.

And the great royal inscriptions almost always appear in *three* languages at once: Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian — that is, Akkadian. The empire announcing itself, trilingually, to its most important literate audiences.

[curious] Now — that trilingual habit was about to become one of the most consequential facts in the history of scholarship.

High on a limestone cliff, on the old road between Babylon and Ecbatana, Darius the Great carved the most important inscription of the ancient Near East: the Behistun Inscription, around 520 BC. In a giant relief, Darius stands triumphant over the rebels he defeated to seize the throne, with the winged symbol of Ahura Mazda above him. And around and below the figures, a long text tells the story of his victory — in Old Persian, in Elamite, and in Babylonian.

The same account. Three times over.

Nobody standing on that road could have guessed what the redundancy would one day be worth.

[brightly] Do you see what that is? One text, three scripts — Behistun became the *Rosetta Stone of cuneiform.* In the 1830s and 40s, a man named Henry Rawlinson famously scaled that cliff to copy the inscription. And because scholars could begin to work out the alphabetic Old Persian, the trilingual key let them decipher not only Old Persian itself — but, through it, the long-dead Babylonian and Assyrian writing of all Mesopotamia.

The chronicles. The ration tablets. The flood epic. Nearly all of it readable today… because Darius bragged, in triplicate, on a cliff.

[thoughtfully] And there is a striking providence of history in that. The empire which sent Judah *home* also, two and a half millennia later, handed the modern world the key to reading the empire that had carried Judah *away.*

Behistun gives us something else, too — the fullest first-person statement of Achaemenid ideology we have. Darius, again and again, credits Ahura Mazda for his kingship and his victories. And he rails against "the Lie" — *drauga* — embodied in the rebels he crushed. Kingship, in Darius's telling, is a grant from heaven; rebellion is not merely treason, it is falsehood itself. Listen to that voice and you hear a king who genuinely believed a high creator-god had appointed him. Which is exactly the mindset that made a ruler able to fund the temple of a foreign "God of heaven" — without discomfort.

[curious] But grand inscriptions only tell us what kings wanted remembered. Administrative records tell us how the empire actually *ran.* And for that, we go to Persepolis — where excavations recovered tens of thousands of clay tablets. The Persepolis Fortification Archive, from about 509 to 493 BC, and the smaller Treasury Archive — mostly written in Elamite, with some in Aramaic.

They are not glamorous. They record rations — grain, wine, livestock — issued to workers, officials, priests, and travelers moving on the king's business across the heartland. But that is *precisely* their value. Because they show a vast, literate, meticulous bureaucracy in motion. Women in the workforce, receiving rations. Religious offerings for many different gods, funded side by side. And authorized travelers drawing provisions along the road with *sealed documents* — which is exactly the kind of royal travel-authorization Nehemiah requests from Artaxerxes in Nehemiah chapter two.

[thoughtfully] Think about that pairing. The world of Persian administration that Ezra and Nehemiah describe from the *receiving* end — these tablets document from the *issuing* end. Two views of the same machine. And they match.

[warmly] Now for the connective tissue. Beneath the monumental Old Persian, beneath the regional Elamite, ran the language that actually carried the empire: Imperial Aramaic — the chancery language in which decrees and letters really traveled. We have it on the Elephantine papyri in Egypt. On leather documents from Bactria, at the empire's far eastern edge. On countless ostraca in between. The same standardized, official Aramaic — from Egypt to Afghanistan.

And *this* is why the Bible itself goes bilingual — in exactly the Persian-document sections. The book of Ezra reproduces official correspondence in Aramaic: the accusation letter to Artaxerxes and his reply, the exchange with Darius, Artaxerxes's commission to Ezra — that's Ezra chapters four through six, and again in chapter seven. The book preserves the documents in the very language they were issued in — and then switches back to Hebrew for the narrative. Portions of Daniel are likewise in Aramaic.

[curious] Some readers have treated that mixed-language texture as a puzzle, or a problem. It's the opposite. Far from a defect, the bilingual texture is a mark of *authenticity*: the compilers had real Persian-era documents in hand, and they quoted them as they stood.

And the language stayed. The Jews carried this Aramaic home with them, and it remained the daily speech of Judea for centuries — so that the language of the Persian bureaucracy became, in time, the mother tongue of Jesus of Nazareth. [warmly] Let that settle for a moment. The paperwork language of an empire… became the language of the Savior.

One more thread, and it ties the whole section together. Persia was a civilization of *files.* When the Jews' right to rebuild the temple is challenged — how is the case settled? Not by argument. By a search of the royal archives, where Cyrus's decree is found, on a scroll, at Ecbatana. When Mordecai's life hangs in the balance in Esther — what saves him? A court chronicle. A written record, read back to a king in the night. Decrees are drafted, sealed, copied, and dispatched — in writing, always in writing.

[thoughtfully] The post-exilic books *breathe* this documentary air. And it is the same air that produced Ezra the — notice the title — *scribe.* And a community re-founded, more than ever before, on a written and studied text.

[warmly] So here's where this one lands. An invented alphabet on a cliffside. Tens of thousands of ration receipts. A chancery language stretching from Egypt to Afghanistan. An empire that wrote *everything* down. And moving through all that paperwork — a small covenant people, learning from it, quoting it, and building their restored life around a book.

The empire's files eventually crumbled with the empire. But the people who learned, in that world, to live by what was written — they're still reading.
