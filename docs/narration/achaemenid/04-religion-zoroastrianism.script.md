# SCRIPT

[warmly] Here's something that would have stopped a returning exile in their tracks. After generations in Babylon — a world of idol processions, of gods who had to be fed and dressed and carried — the Jews who came home found themselves ruled by kings who had… no idols. No statues. No carved images at all. Kings who honored a single great god of light and truth, with sacred fires burning on open-air altars.

For the first time, God's people lived under rulers who were neither idol-worshippers of the Babylonian kind, nor followers of the God of Israel — but a *third thing* entirely. An Iranian religion we call *Zoroastrianism*. It's the faith of Cyrus's heirs. And understanding it clears up a great deal about the Persian world of Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel.

[thoughtfully] It also raises one of the most delicate questions in all of Bible study: did Persian religion *influence* Judaism? We'll walk right up to that question — carefully, and honestly. But first, the faith itself.

At the center of the Persian kings' inscriptions stands one god: *Ahura Mazda* — "the Wise Lord." Darius the Great opens his great Behistun inscription by crediting Ahura Mazda for everything: "By the favor of Ahura Mazda I am king; Ahura Mazda bestowed the kingdom upon me." And notice how this god is portrayed. Not as a statue. As the *maker* — of heaven and earth, of humankind, and of *happiness* for humankind. A high creator-god of truth and light. And opposed to him stands *drauga* — "the Lie."

[curious] Behind this faith stands a prophet: Zarathustra — the Greeks called him Zoroaster. An Iranian religious teacher whose dates are genuinely up for grabs; estimates range from around twelve hundred BC to around six hundred BC. His hymns, the *Gathas*, preserved in the sacred language Avestan, call people to worship Ahura Mazda and to choose truth — *asha* — over the Lie, in a cosmos understood as a moral struggle. And classic Zoroastrianism came to frame that struggle as a great cosmic contest: Ahura Mazda against a hostile spirit of darkness, Angra Mainyu — Ahriman. A *dualism* of good and evil, moving toward a final resolution in which good triumphs.

[thoughtfully] Now — before we go one step further, I have to hand you the single most important tool for this whole subject. Almost every question about early Zoroastrianism runs into the same wall: *the sources are late.* The Gathas may be very old — but they were passed down orally for centuries and written down far later. And the fuller Zoroastrian scriptures, the systematic theology? Those survive mainly in texts from the Sasanian era and after — the third to ninth centuries *AD*. That's a thousand years past the Achaemenids. So we often simply cannot be sure what a Persian in Ezra's day actually believed, as opposed to what later Zoroastrian tradition taught. Hold onto that. The honest scholar keeps coming back to it.

[brightly] So what did Persian worship actually *look* like? To a Judean — in some ways familiar, and in some ways strange.

First: no cult statues. Like Israel — and pointedly unlike Babylon — the Persians did not worship carved images of their gods in temples. The Greek historian Herodotus noticed this with real interest. The Persians, he wrote, "have no images of the gods, no temples nor altars, and consider the use of them a sign of folly." A returning exile who had grown up amid Babylon's idol-processions would have noticed the difference *immediately*.

Second: fire. Worship centered on tending a sacred fire — and let's be precise here: the fire was a *symbol* of Ahura Mazda's purity and truth, not a god itself — burning on raised altars, often on hilltops. The priests who tended those fires were called *magi*. [curious] Yes — that word. It's the word behind "magic." And it's the word behind the "wise men" of Matthew chapter two — Persian-tradition priest-astronomers.

Third: purity and ethics. Zoroastrian practice put great weight on ritual purity, and on a moral triad you can still quote today: good thoughts, good words, good deeds.

The Bible never describes Persian worship directly. But it does show the priesthood at work. Daniel is set over "the wise men of Babylon" — including "the magicians" and "the Chaldeans," in Daniel 2:48 — the learned priestly class the magi belonged to. And notice: Daniel presides over them without ever adopting their arts.

[thoughtfully] Alright. Now the delicate part.

Some scholars have proposed that during and after the exile, Jewish thought absorbed ideas from Persian religion. And the usual candidates get named: a sharpened sense of angels and demons. A cosmic opposition between good and evil. A developed afterlife, resurrection, and final judgment. An end-times hope of a world set right. These themes are more prominent in post-exilic and later Jewish writings than in the earliest ones — so, the argument goes, maybe Persia shaped them.

This deserves a careful, layered answer — not a slogan. Because honestly, this topic gets handled badly in *both* directions.

Start with the difficulties in the "Persia taught Israel" claim. First — the dating problem, again. Much of what gets confidently called "Zoroastrian" — the systematic dualism, the detailed resurrection doctrine — is firmly attested only in texts *later* than the Jewish writings it supposedly influenced. [curious] And when the borrower's document is older than the lender's… the direction of borrowing is not obvious, is it? Some scholars argue the influence, if there was any, ran the other way — or came from a shared older stock.

Second — internal roots. Many of these themes have clear seeds *within* Israel's own earlier scriptures. The divine council and its members — First Kings 22:19; Job 1:6. The hope of resurrection — Isaiah 26:19; Daniel 12:2. A coming day of the Lord, right there in the pre-exilic prophets. A theme becoming *more explicit* over time is not the same thing as a theme being *imported*.

And third — the decisive difference. Israel's faith was never dualist in the Zoroastrian sense. There are not two co-eternal powers. There is one God — sovereign even over the adversary; that's Job 1:12 — who declares, in words spoken through Isaiah *to Cyrus himself*: "I form the light, and create darkness… I the LORD do all these things." Isaiah 45:7. Many read that very verse as a deliberate rejection of a two-power cosmos — a monotheistic answer aimed straight at the Persian worldview, in the same breath that names the Persian king.

[warmly] Now — what *can* be said fairly? Jews and Persians lived side by side for two centuries. In Babylon. In Susa. In Yehud. And cultures in contact do share vocabulary and imagery. It would be strange if two peoples so long entangled had learned *nothing* from one another's ways of speaking about God, the heavens, and the end of days. So careful scholars speak of a *shared environment* — possible mutual influence at the level of imagery and emphasis — while resisting the flat claim that Judaism's core doctrines were "borrowed from Zoroastrianism." The evidence simply does not support so strong a statement.

[thoughtfully] And Latter-day Saints have a distinctive way of holding all this. We understand truth to have been revealed from the *beginning* — that Adam, Enoch, Noah, and Abraham knew of the plan of salvation, the adversary, the resurrection, and the final judgment long before any of these peoples. Moses chapter six lays that out. On that view, genuine echoes of these truths *anywhere* in the ancient world — including Persia — aren't a threat to be explained away. They're scattered light. Fragments and memories of an original knowledge, dimmed and reshaped in transmission. Where Zoroastrian teaching resembles biblical truth, we can see a distant echo of what was once revealed plainly. Where it diverges — into dualism, into the veneration of the elements — we can see how far a true seed can drift. That lets us take the parallels seriously, and *calmly* — without forcing a borrowing, and without fearing one.

[warmly] So here's the bottom line for your Bible reading. The Persian kings sincerely believed that a high creator-god had given them their empire. Which is exactly why they could hear a request to honor "the God of heaven" — Ezra and Nehemiah's favorite title for the LORD — as something *familiar*, and fund a temple to Him without feeling they had betrayed their own faith.

The influence debate? Hold it the way the best scholarship holds it: as a real and interesting *question* — not a settled fact — with the sober reminder that the sources are late, and the direction of any borrowing is genuinely uncertain.

But on the deepest point, there is no ambiguity at all. Israel's witness is to *one* sovereign God — maker of both the light and the dark — standing in clear, deliberate contrast to Persian dualism. [thoughtfully] And it says so… in the very chapter that blesses the Persian king.
