# SCRIPT

Here's a question that decided the fate of a kingdom. When two great empires go to war, and you are the little country caught in the middle — whose side do you take? For the last forty years of its life, the kingdom of Judah had to answer that question, over and over. And nearly every time, it answered wrong.

Picture the map. To the south, Egypt — the old, familiar power. To the east, Babylon — new, and terrible. Judah sat right on the road between them, too small to stand alone, too well-placed to be left alone. And its kings kept making the same bet: Egypt. Here's the twist that makes this more than politics. Judah's prophets were not neutral. One of them, the loudest, kept insisting the safe bet was Babylon — and they called him a traitor for it. Let me show you how it happened.

It starts before Babylon ever threatens Jerusalem. The year is 609 BC, and Pharaoh Necho marches his army north. Now, here's the surprise: he is not marching to attack Assyria. He is marching to save it. Assyria, the old superpower, is collapsing — and Egypt would rather prop up a dying neighbor than face a strong new one. Your Bible almost seems to say the opposite — that Necho went up "against" the king of Assyria. But that little word "against," in Hebrew, is a preposition that just as easily means "to," or "alongside." And the Babylonian records settle it: that year, Egyptian and Assyrian soldiers were fighting side by side. Necho was riding to the rescue. And the rising power he was riding against? Babylon. Though — careful here — not Nebuchadnezzar yet. In 609, Babylon's king was his father, Nabopolassar. Nebuchadnezzar was still just the crown prince. His crown was four years away.

And into that march steps Josiah, the last good king of Judah. He intercepts Necho at Megiddo. And he dies there. Why? Honestly — we don't fully know, and here I'm reading between the lines, so take this as a possibility, not a fact. Scripture never tells us what was in his head. But some historians notice something: Josiah's reforms had reached north, into the old lost kingdom of Israel — and they wonder if he was dreaming of gathering the scattered northern tribes back home, under a son of David. If that was the dream, then Egypt, propping up the empire that had swallowed those tribes, was the enemy of it. Maybe. What we know for certain is the result: by blocking Egypt, Josiah's last ride helped Babylon.

Josiah's death handed Judah straight back to Egypt. Necho pulls down Josiah's chosen heir, installs a new king named Jehoiakim, and lays on a heavy tax. And from that day, Judah's court belongs to Egypt — a pro-Egypt party running everything, always betting the pharaoh will come save them. But standing against them are the prophets. And loudest of all is Jeremiah. His message never changes, and to the royal court it sounds like treason: submit to Babylon. Serve the king of Babylon, and live. Not because Jeremiah loved Babylon — but because he was convinced Babylon was God's own instrument of judgment. He even calls Nebuchadnezzar, in the voice of the Lord, "my servant." Meanwhile, the comfortable prophets promise the opposite — Babylon will fall any day now. One of them, Hananiah, walks up and snaps the wooden yoke right off Jeremiah's neck in the temple, to drive the point home. They were sincere. They were also completely wrong.

And here is the tragedy. Judah makes the same mistake three times. Jehoiakim rebels — betting on Egypt. Babylon comes, and takes Jerusalem in 597, and carries the king and the nobles off into exile. Nebuchadnezzar props up a puppet — Josiah's son Mattaniah — and renames him Zedekiah, just so everyone remembers who is in charge. And Zedekiah? He does it again. Secret messengers to Egypt, begging for horses and soldiers. And Egypt does come — for a moment. When Pharaoh's army appears on the horizon, the Babylonians actually pull back from the walls. And then Egypt thinks better of the whole thing, turns around, and goes home. So the Babylonians come back, and this time they finish it. 586 BC — the walls breached, the temple burned. Every single time Judah leaned on Egypt, Egypt turned out to be exactly what an old proverb called it: a bruised reed. You lean on it, it snaps, and the splinter cuts your hand.

And Judah did not gamble alone. When Zedekiah planned his revolt, he had partners — envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, and the Phoenician cities, Tyre and Sidon, all gathered in Jerusalem to coordinate the rising. Tyre and Sidon were on Judah's side. Which is exactly what makes what happened next so bitter. When Jerusalem finally fell, Tyre did not mourn a fallen ally. Tyre celebrated. Listen to how Ezekiel captures it: "Aha — she is broken, the gateway of the peoples — and now it all turns to me. I shall be filled; she lies in ruins." Jerusalem had been a rival on the trade routes, and her death meant Tyre's profit. The prophet Amos had already named that sin — Tyre "remembered not the brotherly covenant." It was not a military betrayal. It was something quieter, and colder: a partner cashing in at your funeral.

And yet — as circumstances would have it — when Judah finally came home, a whole lifetime later, to rebuild the temple, guess who they bought their cedar from? Tyre and Sidon. Exactly as Solomon had, for the first temple. The neighbors who cheered the fall helped raise the house that rose from the ashes. What that homecoming was actually like — who was welcomed back, and who was turned away at the door — well, that is a story for another day, and another empire. Persia.

So the next time you read those last, grim chapters of Kings — the rebellions, the sieges, the fire — remember there was a fork in the road the entire time. Egypt, or Babylon. And a lonely prophet, shouting the unpopular answer, who turned out to be right.
