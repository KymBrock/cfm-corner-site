# SCRIPT

[curious] Let me start with a fact that should stop you in your tracks. The empire that burned Jerusalem to the ground… that carried Judah into captivity… that became the Bible's forever-symbol of worldly power — that empire lasted less than *seventy years.*

Seventy years. [thoughtfully] Which happens to be exactly the number the prophet Jeremiah gave it, while it was still on the *rise.*

But here's the twist. Behind that brief, blazing empire stood almost *two thousand years* of accumulated fame. When Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt Babylon, he wasn't building something new. He was restoring a city that was already ancient — older to him than the apostles are to us. So to understand Babylon, we have to walk a very long timeline. Don't worry — I'll be your guide. Let's go.

[brightly] It opens with one remarkably patient man. Around 1792 BC, a king named Hammurabi came to the throne of a modest little city-state called Babylon. And for thirty years, he waited — a careful junior partner among stronger kings. Then, in a single decade, he outmaneuvered every one of them, and for the first time, southern Mesopotamia answered to a single ruler — sitting at Babylon.

You've probably heard of his law code — nearly three hundred judgments carved on a great black stone pillar, beneath an image of the king receiving justice from the sun god. Its style — "if a man does this, then the penalty is that" — is the very same style you'll find in the law of Moses in Exodus. Scholars see both drawing on a shared, older legal tradition — the common law of the ancient Near East — rather than one copying the other. [thoughtfully] The resemblances are real. And so are the differences: Israel's law roots justice in *covenant with God*, not in a king's boast.

Now, Hammurabi's empire barely outlived him. But something more durable had happened. His dynasty made Babylon the home of the god Marduk — and Marduk's city became the religious and intellectual capital of Mesopotamia. A position it would hold, through conquest and catastrophe, for the next *seventeen hundred years.*

[curious] Which brings us to the long middle — and here's the part that matters most for reading your Bible. For most of Israel's early story, Babylon was… nobody. A people called the Kassites ruled it, quietly, for four hundred years. Through the Exodus. Through the judges. Through David and Solomon. Babylon was no threat to *anyone.* The superpowers of that age were Egypt and the Hittites — and then, for a while, nobody at all.

[thoughtfully] So when Babylon finally steps onto the Bible's stage, it doesn't arrive as an empire. It arrives as a *victim.*

Because from the ninth century on, the rising power was Assyria — Babylon's northern sibling, and its tormentor. And Assyria had a Babylon problem it could never solve. Babylon was too *holy* to simply crush — the Assyrians shared its gods, craved coronation there. But Babylon kept rebelling. So you get a century of fire: revolt, punishment, guilty rebuilding, over and over.

[brightly] And right in the middle of that century sits a name your Bible knows: Merodach-baladan. A chieftain of the southern marshes who kept seizing Babylon's throne out from under Assyria. At one point, he sent envoys all the way to Jerusalem — to King Hezekiah. Officially, a get-well visit. Really? A rebel shopping for allies. And Hezekiah, flattered, showed them everything — all his treasure, all his armory.

[thoughtfully] And the prophet Isaiah looked at those Babylonian envoys and said something that must have sounded absurd. He said: everything they've seen will one day be carried off — to Babylon. And your own sons will serve in the palace of Babylon. Understand — at that moment, Babylon was the *hunted* one. Assyria was the hunter. A century later, it happened exactly as Isaiah said.

[curious] So how did the victim become the empire? Astonishingly fast. In 626 BC, a Chaldean general named Nabopolassar seized Babylon's throne. By 612, allied with the Medes, he had *burned Nineveh itself* — the fall the prophet Nahum sings about. By 609, Assyria simply… didn't exist anymore.

Then came his son. In 605 BC, at a place called Carchemish, the crown prince Nebuchadnezzar shattered the army of Egypt. Weeks later his father died, and he raced home across the desert to seize the crown. [thoughtfully] And little Judah, caught between the two giants, now had a new master. Judah's last four kings would live and die inside that grip — through the first deportation in 597, and the destruction of Jerusalem in 586.

And then — almost as fast as it rose — it was over. Nebuchadnezzar's heirs lasted six years between them. The throne passed to an elderly outsider named Nabonidus, who wandered off to a desert oasis for a decade and left his son Belshazzar minding the city. And when Cyrus of Persia marched in 539 BC, the greatest walled city on earth opened its gates almost without a fight.

[warmly] Seventy years. From Babylon's rise to Babylon's fall — one human lifetime. Count it from the year Babylon took the west, in 605, to Cyrus's decree that sent Judah home, in 538 — seventy years. Or count from the temple's destruction to its rededication — again, seventy years. Jeremiah had said the nations would serve Babylon "seventy years." [thoughtfully] And a prophet said it out loud, in advance, while the empire still looked invincible from inside Jerusalem's walls.

That's the shape of the whole story. Babylon: ancient before it was mighty… and mighty for barely a lifetime.
