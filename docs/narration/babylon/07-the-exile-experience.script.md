# SCRIPT

Let me start with a receipt.

In a storeroom of Nebuchadnezzar's palace, a scribe pressed a wedge into wet clay and wrote out a monthly allowance of oil. Palaces did this every day. But the name on the list is *Ya'u-kīnu, king of the land of Yahudu.* Jehoiachin. King of Judah. The captive king of Second Kings — alive, on the empire's books, drawing rations.

That tablet was published in 1939. For most of history we could see this catastrophe only from the inside — Psalm one thirty-seven's weeping, Ezekiel's visions, Jeremiah's letters. Now we have the paperwork.

Let's walk it. Gently — this one carries some weight.

Babylon didn't empty Jerusalem all at once. It came in waves. In 605 BC, hostages from the royal house — Daniel among them — plus the temple vessels. In 597, the great deportation: King Jehoiachin, the queen mother, the officials, the fighting men, and all the craftsmen and smiths. Kings puts it bleakly. "None remained, save the poorest sort of the people of the land." Ezekiel walked out in that wave. Then 586 — the temple burns.

How many? The Bible is honestly untidy here. A round total — ten thousand captives — and an oddly exact accounting in Jeremiah fifty-two: four thousand six hundred persons.

But notice what Babylon *took.* The king. The leadership. The soldiers. And the craftsmen. Selective, not total — an empire-builder's shopping list, leaving a peasant remnant to keep the land producing tribute. Judah splits into three streams: the exiles in Babylonia, the remnant in the land, and a group that fled to Egypt.

And here's the part I find almost unbearable and almost beautiful at once. It was the *Babylonian* stream — the one that looked most finished — that the prophets called the "good figs." The carriers of the future.

So where did they land? Ezekiel tells us: Tel-abib, by the river of Chebar. Both names turn out to be real Babylonian. The "river" is the Kabaru canal, in the Nippur countryside. And Tel-abib is Akkadian — *til abūbi,* "flood mound" — the scribes' term for an abandoned settlement mound. Deportees planted on crown land beside the canals. Farm colonists of the king.

And then, in the 1990s, that picture acquired paperwork.

About two hundred cuneiform tablets — the Āl-Yāhūdu archive, published by Laurie Pearce and Cornelia Wunsch in 2014 — document a village near Nippur whose Babylonian name means, simply, *Judah-town.* They begin in 572 BC. Fourteen years after the temple burned. One honest caveat: these surfaced on the antiquities market, not a controlled dig — scholars are careful to flag that. Their contents are considered authentic.

And what do these people do? Utterly ordinary things. People with unmistakably Judean names — names built on the divine name, like *Yahû-azāri,* "YHWH is my help" — lease fields. Pay taxes. Take loans. Marry. Inherit. Go to court. Along canals — exactly where Ezekiel said they were.

Now put that next to a letter. To the exiles of 597, Jeremiah wrote the most counterintuitive pastoral letter in scripture. "Build ye houses, and dwell in them; and plant gardens… and seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it." Settle in. The stay has a fixed term, and a promised end.

And the Āl-Yāhūdu tablets read like the *receipts* of a community that did exactly that. Houses built. Fields leased. Families raised. Taxes paid. And identity kept. The practical shape of Judah's survival matches, document by document, the counsel of the prophet who told them how to survive it.

But — and this matters — none of that normalcy should soften the wound.

These people watched their city starve and burn. The poetry they left is scripture's rawest. "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." Sit with that geography a moment — the canals of their new farmland, doubling as the waters of mourning. And their captors, calling for a show: "Sing us one of the songs of Zion."

Beneath the grief lay an emergency. In the ancient Near East's standard logic, a destroyed temple meant a *defeated god.* So — was the LORD weaker than Marduk?

Ezekiel answered by geography. His inaugural vision, the divine throne-chariot, appears *in Babylonia.* "Among the captives by the river of Chebar." Wheels within wheels. A throne that *moves.* God had not been left behind in the rubble. He was never a territorial god at all — He could be with His people in the land of their captors.

And out of that came a quiet revolution: a nation learning to be a covenant people *without* land, temple, or king.

With sacrifice impossible, the portable signs moved to the center. Sabbath — the sign "between me and you throughout your generations." Circumcision. Food laws. Prayer toward Jerusalem. And notice — that's exactly where the exile's famous tests fall. The king's food. The king's image. The king's prayer ban.

Ezekiel keeps showing us the elders of Judah sitting in his house, seeking a word from the LORD. Worship as word and prayer and study, rather than altar. Many scholars trace the synagogue's roots to those gatherings — the institution is only fully visible later, so hold that loosely. But *something* like it had to be born here.

And the scribes became the lifeline. Cut off from the temple, the treasure became the *texts.* Judah went into Babylon a temple-people, and came out — permanently — a scripture-people. The figure at the exile's far end is Ezra: "a ready scribe in the law of Moses."

Isaiah gave the era its own metaphor. "I have chosen thee in the furnace of affliction."

What went in was a kingdom like its neighbors — throne, borders, temple, and a chronic weakness for its neighbors' gods. What came out was something history hadn't seen: a people defined by covenant, scripture, and Sabbath, capable of keeping faith *anywhere on earth.* Which is to say — finally shaped for the promise made to Abraham at the beginning. That in his seed "shall all families of the earth be blessed."

By the rivers of Babylon, there they sat down. And then — remarkably — they stood back up.
