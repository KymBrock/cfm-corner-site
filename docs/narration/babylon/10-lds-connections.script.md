# SCRIPT

Let me start with a claim that might reframe your whole year of study. The Book of Mormon does not begin *near* this story. It begins *inside* it.

Not in a vague way. Not thematically. The book dates itself, in its very first sentence of history, to the exact decade we have spent nine sections walking through: "in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah, king of Judah… there came many prophets, prophesying unto the people that they must repent, or the great city Jerusalem must be destroyed."

Zedekiah's first year. That is a date we can fix — not from scripture, but from a clay tablet. The Babylonian Chronicle puts it at five ninety-seven BC, in the aftershock of the first deportation, when Nebuchadnezzar had just carried off Jehoiachin and, in the Chronicle's own dry phrasing, "appointed a king of his own choice." That king of his own choice *is* Zedekiah. The Book of Mormon's opening verse is standing in the rubble of that decision.

Now look at those "many prophets." That is not a literary flourish — the Bible names them. Jeremiah, preaching his temple sermons and walking around wearing a wooden yoke. Habakkuk, wrestling with the Chaldean rise. Zephaniah. Ezekiel, already among the first exiles. And a man most of us have never heard of: Urijah, son of Shemaiah — who preached what Lehi preached, fled to Egypt for his life, was *extradited*, and was executed.

Sit with that one. Because it means the death threats against Lehi were not storybook peril. They were standard operating procedure.

Read this way, First Nephi's opening chapters fit their decade like a hand in a glove. A city drunk on pro-Egypt optimism. Prophets denounced as traitors for saying Babylon would win. Records kept on brass plates, in an age when Judah's scribal culture was at its absolute peak. And a wealthy family with a land of inheritance, liquidating its gold and silver to get out of a doomed city.

And here is the structural thing I cannot stop thinking about. What *is* the Book of Mormon, formally? It is exile literature. Like Ezekiel's community out on the Chebar canal, Lehi's family is a covenant remnant carried out of Jerusalem in the Babylonian crisis — sustained by records, by prophets, by promises of gathering. Its founding anxieties are the exile's anxieties: keep the record, keep the covenant, remember Jerusalem.

The only difference… is the direction of travel. Judah's exiles were carried east, into the empire. Lehi's family was led west, out of it — to run the same experiment, covenant life without the old temple mount, on a new continent. Complete with a temple built, in Nephi's words, "after the manner of… Solomon."

Then there is the second escape. The stranger one.

The people of Zarahemla, when Mosiah's Nephites stumble onto them, say they "came out from Jerusalem at the time that Zedekiah, king of Judah, was carried away captive into Babylon" — led by Mulek, a son of Zedekiah. Which is a *bold* claim, because Second Kings records Nebuchadnezzar slaughtering the sons of Zedekiah at Riblah, before the blinded king's eyes.

And the Book of Mormon knows it. It makes the exception explicit, in a question: "Will ye say that the sons of Zedekiah were not slain, all except it were Mulek?" An infant, unlisted among the executed adult heirs, smuggled out through the very gap in the walls that Second Kings itself describes… is precisely the kind of detail that official history would never record.

How does a Judahite refugee party cross an ocean? Let me be honest about where scholarship stands. Latter-day Saint researchers have long pointed to the Phoenicians — the one Mediterranean people with the ships and the range. John Sorenson developed the case; Hugh Nibley noted, decades earlier, that Egyptian-financed ships of that era were "manned… by Syrian and Phoenician crews." And Tyre's navy *was* the ally of Zedekiah's anti-Babylon coalition. It is coherent. But it remains a reconstruction, offered as one — the Book of Mormon itself says nothing about the route. And one caution for teachers: the popular etymology that Zarahemla means "seed of the king" is not the preferred scholarly reading. The Onomasticon derives it from *zera-hemlah* — "seed of compassion." A lovely name for a refugee people preserved by mercy.

So Lehi shows the Lord leading a remnant *out* of Babylon's reach. Daniel shows the other calling — faithfulness *inside* the empire. Which is, after all, where most of God's people have to live.

And Daniel's episodes read like a curriculum. Dietary resolve settled before the test ever arrived — he "purposed in his heart." Excellence in Babylon's own learning, without adopting Babylon's gods. Three friends at the furnace, offering faith with no guaranteed outcome — "but if not…" Prayer kept, windows open, at known cost.

And Daniel's grandest image — the stone "cut out of the mountain without hands" that shatters the statue of empires and fills the whole earth — the Restoration gave that stone an address. But notice where Babylon sits in that vision. It is only the golden head. The first and most splendid of a procession of kingdoms whose common fate is to become "like the chaff of the summer threshingfloors."

Which is why the Doctrine and Covenants opens with Babylon and nearly closes with it. In the Lord's preface, the modern world is diagnosed with the old city's name: every man walking after the image of his own god, "whose substance is that of an idol, which waxeth old and shall perish in Babylon, even Babylon the great, which shall fall." Idolatry, redefined for our age as self-made gods.

And in the great gathering revelation, the exile's summons is reissued to the whole church: "Go ye out from Babylon. Be ye clean that bear the vessels of the Lord." The temple vessels of five eighty-six BC — transfigured into the covenants we carry. The pioneers sang it across the plains: "O Babylon, O Babylon, we bid thee farewell." And the symbol has since turned inward. Leaving Babylon today is not a wagon journey. It is a daily orientation — in the world, not of it. Which is exactly Jeremiah's balance for the exiles: build, plant, bless the city you live in… and never mistake it for Zion.

And there's one connection deeper than any name or escape — it reaches back before the world itself. Genesis has no war before creation. But the Restoration does. In the premortal council, John's "war in heaven" broke out — Michael against the dragon — and it was fought over agency itself. Two stepped forward. The first, "like unto the Son of Man," said only, "Here am I, send me." The second wanted the Father's own honor, and promised to redeem everyone by force — he "sought to destroy the agency of man" so that not one soul would be lost. The Father chose the first. The second "was angry, and kept not his first estate." Now set that against Babylon. Babylon, too, had a war before creation — but there, the war *was* the creation: the world hacked out of a dead goddess, humanity kneaded from a murdered god's blood. The Restoration keeps the war and refuses the slaughter. There was a conflict before the world — but the world is not its trophy. Creation still comes the way Genesis says it does — by a word, freely spoken, to make room for children who could choose. Babylon's cosmos is what's left when gods fight for power. Ours is what a Father builds in order to give it away.

So here is the arc, end to end. Babylon scattered Judah in three directions — into Babylonia, into Egypt, and, by the Book of Mormon's witness, across the sea. And every stream was remembered. They were scattered, and scourged by other nations — yet, as Nephi promised, "then will he remember the covenants which he made to their fathers."

Ten sections ago we started with mud and rivers, and a city already older to Nebuchadnezzar than the apostles are to us. We end here. Babylon's whole genius was to make itself look permanent — and it is the golden head that becomes chaff. The exiles it carried away, in every direction, are still being gathered home.
