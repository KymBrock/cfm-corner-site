# SCRIPT

For seventeen centuries, the greatest city in the world was a rumor.

Not a ruin you could tour. Not a pin on a map. A rumor — a name in your Bible, a name in Herodotus, attached to a low brown mound in Iraq that nobody could read. Literally could not read. The wedge-script Babylon invented had gone dark; to every living human being on earth, it was scratches in dried mud.

Then, in roughly a hundred years, all of it came back. The script was cracked. The mounds were opened. And the libraries of the Bible's great adversary began — tablet by tablet — to *corroborate* the Bible's account of it.

Let me take you to the mound.

Babylon sits on the Euphrates, about eighty-five kilometers south of Baghdad, near the town of Hillah. The early explorers didn't know what to do with it. Claudius Rich surveyed it in eighteen eleven and again in eighteen seventeen. Austen Henry Layard put in a few soundings and walked away. Here's the problem: Babylon was built of *mudbrick.* No marble. No statuary waiting three feet down. Just brown dirt on brown dirt — so that generation of diggers, chasing museum pieces, went north to the Assyrian palaces instead, where the reliefs practically fell into your hands.

Babylon needed someone patient. In eighteen ninety-nine, it got him.

Robert Koldewey and his German expedition dug this site for *eighteen continuous years* — from eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen seventeen. Straight through Ottoman rule. Straight through a world war. And what he did was quietly revolutionary: he learned to *read* mudbrick. To trace a wall the same color as the ground around it. Earlier diggers had shoveled straight through those walls without ever seeing them.

And out of the dirt came Nebuchadnezzar's city — in plan. The double walls. The Processional Way. The Southern Palace — and in its storerooms, the ration tablets recording oil issued to Jehoiachin, king of Judah, a captive on the payroll of the empire that took him. The foundations of Etemenanki. And the glazed brick of the Ishtar Gate, blue as deep water, shipped to Berlin and reassembled there by nineteen thirty.

Koldewey's methods helped found modern Near Eastern archaeology. Which is a polite way of saying: he taught the field to be *patient.* Hold onto that word.

Because the site's modern story is a hard one. In the nineteen eighties, Saddam Hussein rebuilt walls and palace rooms directly on top of the ancient foundations — stamping his bricks, in conscious imitation of Nebuchadnezzar, with his own name as the city's restorer. Then, in two thousand three and four, coalition forces made the ruins a military base. Camp Alpha. It did documented damage to the Processional Way and the Ishtar Gate area, and drew international protest.

But conservation came back. And in twenty nineteen, Babylon was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Today you walk paving at the level of Nebuchadnezzar's own street, past a replica of the gate — and meet the Lion of Babylon, carved in basalt, still trampling its enemy after two thousand six hundred years.

Now, Babylon never stood alone — and neither do her discoveries.

South of her lies Ur: the moon god's city, the traditional Ur of the Chaldees, the world Abraham left — though many scholars now place his Ur in the north, near Haran. Leonard Woolley dug it from nineteen twenty-two to nineteen thirty-four and found the Royal Tombs, and the best-preserved ziggurat in Iraq. And here's a detail I love. That ziggurat had been restored by Nabonidus, Babylon's last king — and it was *at Ur* that they found his cylinder, a prayer naming "Belshazzar, the eldest son, my offspring." The Belshazzar of Daniel — in his father's own words.

Then Uruk — biblical Erech, named in Nimrod's kingdom in Genesis ten. The world's first true city, and the birthplace of *writing itself,* around thirty-four hundred BC. Nippur, holy city of Enlil — never a political capital, always a religious one — and the hub of the countryside where Judah's exiles were settled: the Chebar canal, the district called Āl-Yāhūdu, "Judah-town," the Murašû archive. Borsippa, whose vitrified ziggurat towers so dramatically that travelers for centuries mistook *it* for the Tower of Babel. Sippar, whose temple gave up tens of thousands of tablets — and a library found in place, still filed in its jars on its shelves. And Nineveh, for one reason: Ashurbanipal's library, where Babylon's classics — the flood tablet, the Enūma Eliš — were found.

So picture a shelf. The flood tablet George Smith deciphered in eighteen seventy-two, electrifying Victorian London with a Babylonian deluge. Hammurabi's stele — hauled off to Susa by Elamite raiders around eleven fifty-five BC, and dug up there by French archaeologists in nineteen oh-one. The Babylonian Chronicles — including the one dating Jerusalem's capture to the sixteenth of March, five ninety-seven BC. A date, in their records, for a siege in yours. The Cyrus Cylinder, pulled from Babylon's foundations by Hormuzd Rassam in eighteen seventy-nine. And the Babylonian Map of the World, with Babylon at the center of everything, because of course it is.

And now a caution — because the same century that gave us all this taught two hard lessons.

First: *provenance matters.* The Āl-Yāhūdu tablets, published in twenty fourteen, surfaced on the antiquities market rather than in a documented dig. That raises real questions about looting, and occasionally forgery. Scholars flag them. So do we.

Second: *sensations need patience.* From the Victorian "Babel-und-Bibel" shouting matches to this morning's headlines, first claims about Bible-related finds get revised. Routinely. The finds that lasted — the chronicles, the ration tablets — lasted because careful people checked.

Which is the whole model for faith and scholarship together. Neither credulity nor cynicism. Verification. And time.

One last thing. You do not need a visa for Iraq to stand in front of Babylon. The gate is in Berlin. The law code is in Paris. The flood tablet and the chronicles and the cylinders are in London. The lion panels are in New York.

Babylon spent seventeen centuries as a rumor. Now she's scattered across the world in glass cases — still, somehow, the city everyone came to see.
