# SCRIPT

Here is a sentence that sounds like exaggeration, and isn't. When a Judean exile was marched into Babylon, he was marched into the largest city the world had ever built.

Roughly two thousand acres. A population in the hundreds of thousands. Jerusalem — the city he had just lost — covered maybe a *twentieth* of that.

Nothing had prepared him. Nothing *could* have.

And it wasn't only Judeans. A century later the Greeks came — and the Greeks, not easily impressed, gave up on measured prose. Herodotus said Babylon "surpasses in splendour any city in the known world."

So let's walk it. Don't worry — I'll be your guide.

Start with the walls, because the walls *were* the argument. The inner city straddled the Euphrates inside a double ring, and the ancient names survive on the king's own bricks: Imgur-Enlil, "Enlil approved," and behind it Nimitti-Enlil. Then Nebuchadnezzar added an outer wall east of the water — the whole circuit ran to something like eighteen kilometers.

Mudbrick at the core. Fired brick on the face. Bitumen for mortar. Thick enough, ancient writers insisted, that a chariot could *turn around* on the rampart walk.

Now, Herodotus says those walls stood ninety meters high, and that is cheerful nonsense. But the truth needed no help. Something like twenty-five meters of wall, rising out of a water-filled moat, kilometer after kilometer. No army of that age could storm it — which is why every conqueror of Babylon, from Cyrus onward, came in through the *gates*, never over the walls.

Hold that thought. Because Jeremiah looked at those walls and said Babylon's "broad walls" would be "utterly broken." To anyone standing beneath them, that was the least likely sentence in prophecy.

Eight great gates pierced the inner wall, each named for a god. The most magnificent was the Ishtar Gate, in the north — the gate the New Year procession came through. Picture a double gatehouse over fourteen meters high, faced in glazed brick of deep lapis blue. And marching across that blue, in molded relief, hundreds of animals: white-and-gold aurochs bulls for the storm god Adad, and the mushkhushshu — Marduk's dragon, scaled body and horned head.

Through the gate ran the Processional Way — a paved avenue up to twenty meters wide, its walls glazed with striding lions, the beast of Ishtar.

Nebuchadnezzar boasted he'd made the gate "a wonder for all peoples to behold." And he was right, in a way he could never have imagined. Robert Koldewey's German expedition dug it out brick by glazed brick, and today the Ishtar Gate fills a museum hall in Berlin. The blue that awed the exiles *still saturates the room.*

And Ezekiel — a deportee of five ninety-seven — describes images "pourtrayed upon the wall" in vermilion, "after the manner of the Babylonians of Chaldea"… a man who had walked past exactly that.

South of the gate stood the thing that owned the skyline. Etemenanki. "House of the Foundation-Platform of Heaven and Earth" — the ziggurat of Marduk. The excavated foundations give a square about ninety-one meters on a side, roughly as tall. A stepped mountain of mudbrick, crowned with a temple of blue-glazed brick — so the summit dissolved into the sky. And the king recorded that he made its head "rival the heavens."

That phrase should sound familiar.

Genesis eleven describes a tower on the plain of Shinar "whose top may reach unto heaven," built of burned brick with bitumen for mortar. Exactly the materials. Exactly the plain. Exactly the boast. Scholars — Andrew George foremost among them — broadly agree that the Tower of Babel narrative has Etemenanki in view. Readers differ on the *direction* — whether Genesis preserves an ancient memory, or was shaped as polemic against the tower the exiles were staring at. But the target isn't in doubt. And the irony is devastating: a man-made mountain with its head in heaven, built to make a name — and the LORD has to *come down* just to see it.

At its foot sprawled Esagila — Marduk's temple, the Vatican of Mesopotamia. And that is where the vessels of the LORD's house were stored — in the house of another god, until the night a regent poured wine in them, and Cyrus sent them home.

Nearby stood the Southern Palace, its façade glazed blue. There, the young Judean nobles of Daniel one were schooled. In its storerooms, Koldewey's team found ration tablets naming Jehoiachin, king of Judah. And in its throne room, presumably, a court watched a hand write on the wall.

And the famous gardens? Here's where I have to be honest with you.

The Hanging Gardens made the Seven Wonders — terraces of trees on vaulted galleries, watered by hidden machinery, built for a queen homesick for mountains. Lovely story. The problem is that Nebuchadnezzar, a king who itemized *everything*, never mentions them. Herodotus never mentions them. And the spade has produced no agreed trace. The Assyriologist Stephanie Dalley has argued that the wonder belongs to a different king and city altogether — Sennacherib's garden at Nineveh. Others hold the traditional attribution. It is an open question. And it's a useful reminder that "everyone knows" is not evidence.

So stand your exile on the Processional Way in five seventy BC. Blue lions to the horizon. The ziggurat rivaling heaven. The walls that could not fall. Every visible fact argued that Marduk had won, and the LORD of a burned hilltop temple had lost.

The prophets' astonishing wager was to read that skyline *backwards*. To say that *this* — all of it — is the thing that passes. Isaiah: "And Babylon, the glory of kingdoms, the beauty of the Chaldees' excellency, shall be as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah."

Within a lifetime, the gates stood open to a Persian. Within centuries, the city was mounds.

The exiles who believed the prophets over their own eyes… are the reason there was a people left to come home.
