# SCRIPT

Let me start with an accident of history that gave us almost everything we know about Babylon. Papyrus rots. Parchment burns. But clay? A clay tablet sitting in a burning archive doesn't die — it gets *fired.* The disaster bakes it hard. So the libraries of Mesopotamia survive precisely because they were destroyed, and hundreds of thousands of tablets are still with us. More preserved writing than any other ancient civilization left behind.

Which means when Nebuchadnezzar ordered that select young exiles be taught "the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans," we don't have to guess what that meant. We can read the textbooks. Let's go find out what Daniel actually studied.

It starts with wedges. Cuneiform — the word just means "wedge-shaped," from the marks a cut reed presses into damp clay — it began in the city of Uruk, around thirty-four hundred to thirty-three hundred BC. The world's first writing. And here's the deflating part: it wasn't invented for poetry, or prayer, or epic. It was invented for *accounting.* Temple bureaucrats keeping track of sheep, and grain, and beer rations. Literature came later. The receipts came first.

Over centuries the little pictures flattened into abstract clusters of wedges, and the system stretched to write not just Sumerian but Akkadian — the Semitic language whose two great dialects, Babylonian and Assyrian, ran the Near East for two thousand years.

It was *hard.* Several hundred signs, and each one could carry multiple sounds and multiple meanings depending on where it sat. Mastering it took years. Which is exactly why scribes became a professional class. It's why kings bragged when they could read — Ashurbanipal boasted that he could decipher tablets from *before the flood.* And it's why Babylonian learning lasted so long. The difficulty of the script made the school… and the school carried the culture.

Now here's something quieter, and I think more remarkable. By Daniel's day, everyday life was increasingly happening in *Aramaic* — a cousin language written with a mere twenty-two alphabetic letters, in ink, on things that don't survive. Reliefs show the two systems side by side: one scribe pressing wedges into clay, the man beside him writing Aramaic with a pen. Aramaic became the empire's connective tissue. Which is why Judah's officials could already speak it back in Hezekiah's day. Why the exiles came home speaking it. Why chapters of Daniel and Ezra are written in it. And why Jews to this day write Hebrew in what is technically the Aramaic square script — a quiet, permanent souvenir of Babylon.

Even the writing on Belshazzar's wall is Aramaic. Mene, mene, tekel, upharsin — weights and measures. A mina, a shekel, halves. The court's cuneiform experts could see the words. They just couldn't read them as a *sentence* until Daniel supplied the meaning.

So — the school. The Babylonians called it the *edubba.* The tablet house. And we can watch the classroom happen, because the exercise tablets survive: teacher's elegant model line on one side, the student's wobbly copy on the other, sometimes with the corrections still sitting there in the clay. Students started with single wedges and sign lists. Then long bilingual dictionaries — Sumerian to Akkadian — the world's first reference works. Then proverbs, contracts, letters. And finally the classics: Gilgamesh, the hymns, the omen series.

And note *this*: Sumerian was dead as a spoken language since roughly the age of the patriarchs. They kept copying it anyway — for another fifteen hundred years. Exactly the way Latin lived on in medieval Europe.

The schoolboy texts are almost painfully familiar. Up at dawn with his lunch from mother. Caned for being late. Caned for sloppy signs. Caned for talking. One famous composition ends with the boy's father inviting the teacher to dinner and loading him up with gifts — after which the boy's prospects improve *miraculously.*

Daniel's three-year royal course fits this world precisely. Language and script. The literary classics. Then the professional corpus of the wise men — omens, dream manuals, astronomy, mathematics. And the book of Daniel is careful about what that meant spiritually. He mastered the curriculum. He declined the court's food. He never practiced its divination. When the king demanded the future, Daniel *prayed.* Educated *in* Babylon — never *by* it. That's the whole exile in one biography.

Mesopotamia invented the library along with the book. The greatest was Ashurbanipal's at Nineveh — he sent agents through Babylonia with shopping lists, demanding tablets by *title.* And the irony is perfect: when Nineveh burned in six-twelve BC, the fire baked the library into permanence. So when Layard and Rassam dug it out in the eighteen-forties and fifties, Babylon's classics — the Enuma Elish, the flood tablet of Gilgamesh — came back to the world out of the ruins of Babylon's *destroyer.*

And on those shelves sits a body of wisdom literature wrestling with the very questions Job and Ecclesiastes wrestle with. Ludlul bel nemeqi — the "Babylonian Job" — a righteous man who loses health and honor despite perfect piety. The Babylonian Theodicy, a debate between a sufferer and his orthodox friend. Israel's sages knew this international conversation, and they *joined* it. With one decisive difference: their answer began with the fear of the Lord.

Then the numbers. Babylonian mathematics ran on base sixty — the first place-value system in history, two thousand years before decimals reached the West. Sixty divides cleanly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve. Fractions behave. And that choice still governs your wristwatch and every circle you'll ever measure. Their problem tablets solve what we'd call quadratic equations, compute square roots to six decimal places, and the tablet called Plimpton three twenty-two is a systematic table of Pythagorean triples — a *millennium* before Pythagoras.

And the sky. Babylon's greatest science grew straight out of its religion: because the heavens carried divine messages, the heavens got *watched* — systematically, nightly, for centuries. The Astronomical Diaries record planetary positions, eclipses, weather, river levels, and market prices, night after night, for some seven hundred years. The longest continuous scientific observation program ever conducted. And patience became prediction: they found the eclipse cycle, they built mathematical models of the moon and planets, and those numbers pass straight into Greek astronomy. Hipparchus and Ptolemy cite Babylonian records and use Babylonian parameters.

So when Isaiah taunts Babylon — "let now the astrologers, the stargazers, the monthly prognosticators, stand up, and save thee" — understand that the barb lands *because* these were the finest astronomers alive. The critique isn't that they were frauds at watching heaven. It's that watching heaven cannot save you. The stars are creatures. Not counselors.

They drew a map of it, too. The Babylonian Map of the World: the earth as a disk, ringed by a bitter river of ocean, with Babylon — naturally — at the center. It's a real map *and* a theological statement. And it is exactly the mental map the exiles had to live inside. A world whose official center was Babylon… while their prophets kept insisting the true center was a ruined city on a ridge in Judah. To which they would return.
