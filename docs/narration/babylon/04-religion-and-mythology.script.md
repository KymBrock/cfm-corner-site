# SCRIPT

Let me start somewhere strange. In Babylon, the gods *ate.* Twice a day. Real meals — bread, meat, beer — set before a statue behind a curtain. The gods were washed. Dressed. Perfumed. And when they traveled, somebody had to *carry* them.

Hold onto that. It's going to matter enormously.

Because the exiles of Judah walked into the most religiously saturated landscape on earth — a civilization that had been feeding and consulting its gods for over *two thousand years.* Nearly everything the prophets say about idols was forged here.

Mesopotamians imagined the cosmos as a *state* — a divine bureaucracy. Each god held an office; each city was his estate, the people his staff. And Sin, the moon, was god of Ur *and* Harran — traditionally believed to be Abraham's two hometowns, though many scholars now place his Ur in the north, near Haran itself.

And then there's Marduk. Marduk started small — the patron of a modest town. But as Babylon rose, *he* rose, absorbing the offices of older gods until Babylonian theology crowned him king of the whole pantheon. Your Bible calls him Bel. His son Nabu, god of writing and destiny, your Bible calls Nebo.

And here's the key: a Babylonian name wasn't a label — it was a tiny sentence, built around a god and a request. Which is why the royal names were miniature prayers. Nebuchadnezzar means "O Nabu, protect my heir." So when Babylon renamed four Hebrew captives — Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, Abed-nego — it pressed those gods onto young men who pointedly declined to invoke them.

Now, the epic — the Enūma Eliš, "When on high." People call it the Babylonian creation epic. That's slightly wrong. It's an *enthronement* text: creation is the evidence, Marduk's kingship the verdict.

Seven tablets. The primeval waters — fresh-water Apsu, salt-water Tiamat — beget the gods. The young gods make noise; war breaks out. And when Tiamat, the ocean-mother turned monster, marshals an army of dragons, only young Marduk will face her — and he names his price first: supreme kingship. He kills her, splits her carcass in two, and *builds the world out of her body.* Then, from the blood of a rebel god, he has humanity made — as a labor force. So the gods can rest.

Now hear Genesis one against that. The echoes are real. Both begin in watery formlessness. The Hebrew word for the deep — *tehom* — is the linguistic cousin of *Tiamat.* A firmament divides waters above from waters below. A seven-part structure ends in rest.

And here I have to be honest with you about the scholarship. When the epic was published in eighteen seventy-six, the first scholars to read it concluded Genesis borrowed from Babylon. The leading modern authority, W. G. Lambert, pushed back hard — showing the Enūma Eliš is itself late and idiosyncratic, not *the* standard creation story of the Near East, and that direct borrowing is unlikely. Most scholars today say something more careful: Genesis is speaking a shared ancient vocabulary — and using it *polemically.*

Because the differences are the sermon. When read against Babylon, Genesis one is quietly devastating. Creation is no war-prize — no cosmos wrenched from a slain god, no rival God must conquer to build it. God speaks — and it is so. The sea isn't a slain goddess; it's a creature, bounded on command. The sun and moon — *mighty gods* in Babylon — aren't even named. They're "the greater light" and "the lesser light." Lamps, hung by their Maker. And humanity isn't an afterthought brewed from rebel blood. Humanity is the summit. Made in God's image. Blessed.

Same with the flood. Babylon told that story long before any of the Bible was written down — in the Atrahasis epic, and in Tablet Eleven of Gilgamesh. The boat. The animals. The birds sent out — a dove, a swallow, a raven. Unmistakable, and honestly, unsurprising: two traditions remembering the same story in the same flood plain. What differs is the theology. Babylon's gods send the flood because we're *noisy.* They panic when it comes. They swarm the survivor's sacrifice "like flies" — because they're hungry. Genesis grounds it in moral judgment, and ends not with divine snacking but with covenant. A bow set in the cloud.

A real event leaves many memories. The differences show which tradition remembered its *meaning.*

Now — back to those meals. Once a year, at the spring equinox, came the Akitu, the New Year festival. Twelve days, the Enūma Eliš recited before Marduk's statue. And then the most extraordinary rite of all: the *king himself,* stripped of his regalia, slapped across the face by the high priest, made to declare on his knees that he had not sinned against the city — then re-crowned, and slapped once more. Hard enough, the texts specify, to bring tears. Tears were a good omen.

And Isaiah watched all of this. Isaiah forty-four and Jeremiah ten read like eyewitness reports from the idol workshop — the goldsmith's plating, the nails so the god won't topple over. Then Isaiah forty-six turns that procession upside down: "Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth… they are a burden to the weary beast." And against them, God says: *I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you.*

Babylon's gods must be carried. Israel's God carries.

One more layer. No civilization ever invested more in knowing the future — sheep livers, oil on water, dreams, above all the night sky, read by trained experts. Daniel's magicians and astrologers and Chaldeans are that establishment's guilds. And the joke at their expense is precise: the world's best future-readers cannot tell the king his own dream. "There is a God in heaven that revealeth secrets." And He is not on the payroll.

So what did seventy years inside the idol factory accomplish?

Judah went into Babylon a people repeatedly seduced by exactly this world. High places. Rooftop worship of the host of heaven. Cakes for the queen of heaven. It came home *changed.* Seeing those gods up close — fed by cooks, carried by porters — turned the prophets' satire into national conviction. The old wholesale idol-worship — the bowing to carved images — lost its grip.

But *cured* would be too strong a word. The exile curbed Israel's idolatry; it did not scrub Babylon off entirely. They carried the empire home in their very calendar — the month-names they still keep — and one of them was Tammuz. The dying god Ezekiel had caught the women of Jerusalem weeping for. A people finished with feeding statues still brought home, in subtle and small ways, a memory of Babylon they never quite shook. And before we judge them too harshly — look at our own week. Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday are four Norse gods in a row: Tiw, Woden, Thor, and Frigg. Saturday is Saturn's. We wear a whole pantheon in our calendar and never notice — far more of it than the exiles ever brought home.

Still, the conviction held. Jeremiah put it like this: "The gods that have not made the heavens and the earth, even they shall perish from the earth."

And he wrote that one verse in Aramaic. The language of the empire.

As if to make sure the neighbors could read it.
