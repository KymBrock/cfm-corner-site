# SCRIPT

[thoughtfully] Assyrian kings did not write about their failures.

Their annals are victory monuments in prose — cities burned, kings flayed, the enemy's land "turned into ruin-heaps and mounds." So when the great clay prism of Sennacherib reaches 701 BC, and the little kingdom of Judah, something remarkable happens. The record boasts, at length, about everything Assyria did to Hezekiah — and then, at the one moment the reader waits for… it goes quiet.

It never says he took Jerusalem.

[warmly] And on this one event, Assyria's own scribes and the books of Kings and Isaiah agree — on the humiliation, and on the strangest part: the city did not fall.

Hezekiah of Judah was a reformer — and, eventually, a rebel. After the north fell in 722, Judah survived as a vassal, paying tribute. Hezekiah changed that. Trusting Egypt and a wave of anti-Assyrian revolt, he "rebelled against the king of Assyria, and served him not" — and prepared his capital for the reckoning he knew would come.

The Chronicler describes it in engineering detail. Hezekiah took counsel "to stop the waters of the fountains which were without the city" — denying the besiegers water — "built up all the wall that was broken… and another wall without… and made darts and shields in abundance," and "stopped the upper watercourse of Gihon, and brought it straight down to the west side of the city of David."

[brightly] And these are among the few Bible verses you can still *walk through.* Hezekiah's Siloam Tunnel — a 533-meter channel carved through solid rock beneath the City of David — survives. You can wade its length today. Near its outlet, an inscription records the moment the two digging teams, tunneling from opposite ends, broke through — "pickaxe against pickaxe."

[thoughtfully] Then Sennacherib came.

"Now in the fourteenth year of king Hezekiah did Sennacherib king of Assyria come up against all the fenced cities of Judah, and took them." Sennacherib's own prism supplies the number the Bible leaves at "all": *forty-six* walled cities — with two hundred thousand, one hundred fifty people, he claims, driven out as spoil. Judah was dismantled, fortress by fortress, until only Jerusalem was left.

The centerpiece was the siege of Lachish, Judah's second city — a conquest Sennacherib thought worth an entire *room.* At Nineveh, in his "Palace Without Rival," Austen Henry Layard found a suite carved floor to ceiling with a single panorama — the storming of Lachish — the king enthroned over a caption reading: "Sennacherib, king of the world… sat upon a throne and the booty of Lachish passed before him." And at Lachish itself, the siege-ramp still lies heaped against the wall — arrowheads, sling-stones, scorch — the only Assyrian siege-ramp ever recovered.

Facing annihilation, Hezekiah sued for terms. "I have offended," he sent word to Lachish; "return from me: that which thou puttest on me will I bear." Sennacherib fixed the price at "three hundred talents of silver and thirty talents of gold," and Hezekiah stripped the temple to pay it — even the gold he had himself overlaid on the temple doors.

[curious] Here the two records nearly touch. The prism lists the tribute as thirty talents of gold — the very figure — and *eight hundred* talents of silver. The gold agrees to the talent. The silver differs — and honest readers note it rather than smoothing it over.

Now comes the line every student of the Bible learns by heart: "As for Hezekiah the Judahite, who did not submit to my yoke: forty-six of his strong, walled cities… I besieged and took… He himself, like a caged bird I shut up in Jerusalem, his royal city. I threw up earthworks against him — the one coming out of the city-gate, I turned back to his misery."

[thoughtfully] For any other rebel city, the sentence continues: *I besieged it, I conquered it, I burned it, I carried off its people.* That's the formula, repeated hundreds of times. Here, the formula breaks. He penned Hezekiah in like a bird in a cage… and then the account simply moves on. He never claims to have taken Jerusalem. For a monarch whose entire genre exists to trumpet conquests, that omission is not modesty. It is the loudest thing in the text.

Between the tribute and the deliverance comes a war of words at the wall. Sennacherib sends his chief herald — the Rabshakeh — "from Lachish to king Hezekiah with a great host against Jerusalem."

The Rabshakeh's speech is a masterclass in psychological warfare. Hezekiah's officials beg him to switch languages — "talk not with us in the Jews' language in the ears of the people that are on the wall" — which is exactly what he wants. He refuses, raises his voice "in the Jews' language," and promises the defenders vines and fig trees if they'll abandon their king.

Then he makes his fatal theological move. He recites the empire's scoreboard: "Hath any of the gods of the nations delivered at all his land out of the hand of the king of Assyria?… that the LORD should deliver Jerusalem out of mine hand?" Every other god had failed — and he files the LORD on the same list as the beaten gods of Hamath and Sepharvaim. The people "held their peace, and answered him not a word." And the whole crisis now stands reframed: a contest between the God of Israel and the gods of the nations.

Hezekiah takes the letter into the temple, spreads it before the LORD, and prays — not for rescue as such, but for vindication: "that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that thou art the LORD God, even thou only." Isaiah answers with a contemptuous oracle: the king "shall not come into this city… by the way that he came, by the same shall he return" — for the LORD would defend the city "for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake."

And then, in a single verse, the siege ends.

"And it came to pass that night, that the angel of the LORD went out, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians an hundred fourscore and five thousand: and when they arose early in the morning, behold, they were all dead corpses."

[thoughtfully] "So Sennacherib king of Assyria departed, and went and returned, and dwelt at Nineveh."

The account is honest about what it doesn't report. It never says the Assyrians assaulted the walls and were repulsed. It says the army was struck in the night, and the king went home. Herodotus later preserved a garbled Egyptian version blaming field-mice that gnawed the Assyrians' bowstrings — a folk-memory some read as pointing to disease. The Bible names the cause an angel; a naturalistic reading names a plague. Both agree on the outcome. And neither record — Assyrian nor biblical — has Jerusalem falling.

What is not in dispute is the shape of the ending. Assyria took forty-six cities. Penned the king in his capital. Extracted his gold. And left without the prize.

[warmly] Here is why this matters. The deliverance of 701 wasn't merely a battle Judah survived. It was an *idea* Judah carried for the next century. Out of that night grew a doctrine: *Zion is inviolable. The LORD dwells here; he will never let his city and his temple fall.* A true memory of a real deliverance — that curdled into a false security.

The prophets named it. Micah answered the presumption with a sentence nearly unthinkable from a Judean prophet: "Therefore shall Zion for your sake be plowed as a field, and Jerusalem shall become heaps." A century later, in the temple gate, Jeremiah confronted it head-on: "Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these." The very deliverance that had proved God *would* defend his city was now being used to prove God *must* defend it — no matter what its people did.

The paradox, in one line: the night that saved Jerusalem in 701 planted the confidence that helped doom it in 586. The city Assyria could not take… Babylon eventually burned.

[thoughtfully] And it is precisely into this hardened confidence that the Book of Mormon opens — "in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah, king of Judah… there came many prophets, prophesying unto the people that they must repent, or the great city Jerusalem must be destroyed." Lehi is one of them — carrying the same unwelcome message as his contemporary Jeremiah: this city, temple and all, *will* fall. And "the Jews did mock him… and they also sought his life." To a people still leaning on 701, a prophet who said otherwise sounded like a traitor, or a fool.

He was neither.

[warmly] The God who would not let Assyria take Jerusalem was never a God who could be presumed upon. And the same voice that broke the Rabshakeh at the wall spoke, a century later, through the prophets a comfortable city would not hear.
