# SCRIPT

[warmly] For Latter-day Saints, Assyria is where the great arc of the last days begins its first swing.

The doctrine that fills the Restoration — the *gathering* of Israel — presupposes a scattering. And the scattering has a starting gun: the Assyrian deportations of the eighth century BC, when the ten northern tribes were marched off the map. But Assyria bequeathed Jerusalem something subtler and more dangerous, too — the conviction, born at the wall in seven-oh-one BC, that Zion simply could not fall. A century later, that conviction had curdled into the false security the Book of Mormon opens by confronting.

Two threads, from Nineveh to the Restoration.

[thoughtfully] Start with the scattering — not an abstraction, but history. It starts here. When the Assyrian king took Samaria, Second Kings records the fate with a geographer's precision: he "carried Israel away into Assyria, and placed them in Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes"… "until the LORD removed Israel out of his sight… So was Israel carried away out of their own land to Assyria unto this day."

Ten tribes went north. And did not come back.

*That* is the wound the whole doctrine of the gathering exists to answer.

The Book of Mormon — written by heirs of that scattered house — treats the loss as living memory. Nephi tells his people that "the more part of all the tribes have been led away; and they are scattered to and fro upon the isles of the sea; and whither they are none of us knoweth, save that we know that they have been led away." Centuries later, the risen Christ speaks of the same tribes as a flock still out of reach: "I have other sheep, which are not of this land…"

[thoughtfully] One honest note here, and it matters: the Book of Mormon does not name Assyria in these verses, and it would overreach to claim it does. But the "led away" it mourns is the deportation this guide has traced — the first great dispersion, of which every later scattering, Babylon included, is the sequel.

Then the Restoration gives that dispersion a homeward direction. In eighteen thirty-one, the Lord's great gathering revelation looks north — to the same countries Assyria emptied: "And they who are in the north countries shall come in remembrance before the Lord… and there shall they fall down and be crowned with glory, even in Zion." Five years later, in the Kirtland Temple, Moses appears and "committed unto us the keys of the gathering of Israel from the four parts of the earth, and the leading of the ten tribes from the land of the north."

[curious] The tribes Assyria drove into "the land of the north" are, in Latter-day Saint doctrine, precisely the tribes the last dispensation holds the keys to bring home.

And the famous phrase — "the lost ten tribes"? A later coinage, not a biblical title — the reality behind it is Second Kings seventeen. Assyria's deportation policy was engineered to dissolve a conquered people's identity by resettlement, and it largely succeeded: the northern tribes vanish from the record as distinct communities. [warmly] The Latter-day Saint claim is not that they ceased to exist. It's that the Lord kept track of what the empire tried to erase.

[thoughtfully] Now the second thread. The stranger one.

You'll remember seven-oh-one BC — the night the Assyrian army broke before Jerusalem's wall and the city was spared. That deliverance was real, and it was the Lord's. But over the following century it hardened into a theology of guaranteed immunity: because Zion had been saved *once*, Zion could never fall.

The prophets watched that presumption metastasize. Micah — preaching in Hezekiah's own day — mocked the leaders who "lean upon the LORD, and say, Is not the LORD among us? none evil can come upon us." And he answered them flat: "Therefore shall Zion for your sake be plowed as a field, and Jerusalem shall become heaps." A century later, Jeremiah found the same false confidence dressed in temple robes: "Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these."

And *this* is the exact air Lehi breathes on the first page of the Book of Mormon.

The book dates itself: "in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah, king of Judah… there came many prophets, prophesying unto the people that they must repent, or the great city Jerusalem must be destroyed." Lehi joins them: "Wo, wo, unto Jerusalem, for I have seen thine abominations!… that it should be destroyed… and many should be carried away captive into Babylon." And the city's response is the reflex of a people certain their Zion is untouchable: "the Jews did mock him… And when the Jews heard these things they were angry with him… and they also sought his life, that they might take it away."

[thoughtfully] That is the through-line. The false security Assyria's failed siege bred in seven-oh-one is the same false security that gets Lehi driven out of Jerusalem in about six hundred. The Book of Mormon opens on the wrong side of the presumption that Zion cannot fall — with the one family that believed the prophets, and left. Within a decade, the city was gone. The sober counterweight to the miracle: deliverance is a gift, never a guarantee — and a people can turn last century's mercy into this century's complacency.

[brightly] There's also a more *literal* way Assyria enters the Book of Mormon: Nephi copies Isaiah into it — and not just the comforting chapters. The Assyrian ones. The long block of Isaiah two through fourteen is reproduced almost in full as Second Nephi twelve through twenty-four — the heart of Isaiah's response to the Assyrian emergency, woven into Nephite scripture and read across the ocean.

The pivot of the whole block is Assyria as a tool in the Lord's hand — wielded as an instrument of judgment, then held to account for its own arrogance. Nephi preserves the oracle intact: "O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger, and the staff in their hand is their indignation. I will send him against a hypocritical nation… to take the spoil, and to take the prey." And the same chapter promises that the survivors will learn the lesson Hezekiah modeled: "the remnant of Israel… shall no more again stay upon him that smote them, but shall stay upon the Lord, the Holy One of Israel, in truth."

[warmly] That is the doctrinal payoff, in the Book of Mormon's own words: empires rise and fall as the Lord's instruments — and the enduring safety was never the wall. It was reliance on *him.*

One note for careful teachers: the Book of Mormon text of these Isaiah chapters shows small wording differences from the King James — here, "their indignation," where Isaiah ten five reads "mine indignation." Quote each from its own page, rather than blending them.

[thoughtfully] Which brings us to Hezekiah — and let me frame this the way it deserves: what follows is offered as a devotional reading, a pattern drawn from the narrative rather than a doctrine the text states outright.

Hezekiah stands in the Books of Kings as their model of trust: "He trusted in the LORD God of Israel; so that after him was none like him among all the kings of Judah… For he clave to the LORD, and departed not from following him." What makes the portrait usable is *when* he trusted. Not in calm — but with the most efficient army on earth encamped at his gate, and the Rabshakeh shouting that no god had ever saved a city from Assyria. The odds were, humanly, hopeless. The reliance was total. And the deliverance came from a quarter no strategist could have planned.

Read devotionally, Hezekiah is the answer to the false security this story warns against — the difference between *presuming* on the Lord and actually *leaning* on him. His confidence was not that Jerusalem was magic; it was that God was faithful. [warmly] The promise is not that the army goes away on our timetable — it's that reliance on the Holy One of Israel is never misplaced.

[thoughtfully] The arc closes where the doctrine of the gathering makes the whole Assyrian story bearable.

In the very Isaiah block Nephi copied, the prophet looks past the scattering to its reversal — and names Assyria *first* among the places the exiles will be recovered from: "the Lord shall set his hand again the second time to recover the remnant of his people… from Assyria, and from Egypt… and from the islands of the sea. And he shall set up an ensign for the nations, and shall assemble the outcasts of Israel, and gather together the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth."

The empire that began the scattering is, by name, the first place the gathering undoes it.

And the Book of Mormon carries that promise forward as its own hope — the resurrected Christ swearing that "as surely as the Lord liveth, will he gather in from the four quarters of the earth all the remnant of the seed of Jacob, who are scattered abroad."

[warmly] The empire out of the north opened the age of scattering when it marched the ten tribes into Halah and Habor. And the Restoration — holding the keys Moses restored for "the leading of the ten tribes from the land of the north" — is the age that gathers them home.

Eleven sections ago, we met an empire that seemed unstoppable. We watched it swallow the northern kingdom, break against Jerusalem's wall, and vanish so completely that its capitals became grassy hills. Assyria did everything an empire can do to make its work permanent. And everything it scattered, the Lord has promised to gather.

From the first deportation to the final ensign, it is one story. [thoughtfully] And the Lord kept both ends of it.
