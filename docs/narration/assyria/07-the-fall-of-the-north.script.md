# SCRIPT

[thoughtfully] Of the twelve tribes of Israel, ten — the whole northern kingdom — disappear from the historical record in the space of a single generation.

They didn't die out in a plague, or dissolve in a slow decline. They were *removed* — deliberately, administratively — by the Assyrian empire, and scattered so thoroughly across its provinces that no one has ever been able to point to their descendants with certainty since.

This is the heaviest chapter in the guide — so let's take it slowly.

[warmly] The north didn't fall all at once. It fell in *pieces* — a decade-long amputation, limb by limb. It began under Tiglath-pileser the Third, who rebuilt the Assyrian war machine. Around 733 to 732 BC, he sheared off Israel's entire northern and eastern territory — and Kings is unusually specific about what went first: "In the days of Pekah king of Israel came Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria, and took Ijon, and Abel-beth-maachah, and Janoah, and Kedesh, and Hazor, and Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali, and carried them captive to Assyria."

In one campaign, the north lost Galilee, the Jezreel corridor, and the whole of Transjordan — Gilead. Tiglath-pileser's own annals confirm it — the plunder, and the deportation. Assyria had begun eating Israel from the edges inward.

What remained was a rump state around the capital, Samaria — ruled by Hoshea, the last king, who paid tribute until he gambled on rebellion, withholding it and sending to Egypt for help.

[thoughtfully] And Assyria did not forgive a broken oath.

The empire came for the capital itself. The city held out with grim endurance. "Then the king of Assyria came up throughout all the land, and went up to Samaria, and besieged it three years."

Three years.

When the wall finally gave, the scribe of Kings records the destination with the precision of a shipping manifest: "In the ninth year of Hoshea the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria, and placed them in Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes."

Those are real places — scattered *on purpose.* Halah and the Habor lay in the far north of Mesopotamia; the cities of the Medes, hundreds of miles farther east, on the Iranian plateau. The northern tribes were not resettled together in one exile colony where they might keep their cohesion, as Judah later would be in Babylonia. They were dispersed — fragments planted among unrelated peoples at opposite ends of the empire.

[curious] So which king actually took Samaria? The Bible leaves the conqueror unnamed at the capture — the Assyrian sources show why. Shalmaneser the Fifth laid and pressed the siege, but died just as the city fell, in 722 BC. His successor, Sargon the Second, seized the throne and, in his own annals, claimed the victory for himself, boasting that he "besieged and conquered Samaria." His inscriptions even put a number on it: twenty-seven thousand, two hundred ninety inhabitants.

[thoughtfully] That figure is a claim to be weighed, not a census to be trusted. But it gives the scale — and it names the human beings behind the Bible's bare phrase, "carried Israel away."

Now the book of Kings does something no Assyrian inscription ever does. It stops, and explains *why.* For this historian, the armies were only the instrument; the cause was covenant failure. "For so it was, that the children of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, which had brought them up out of the land of Egypt… and had feared other gods."

The charge sheet is specific: idolatry, the high places, the golden calves — above all "the sins of Jeroboam," the counter-worship set up when the north broke from the house of David. And underneath runs a second charge: they would not listen. "Yet the LORD testified against Israel, and against Judah, by all the prophets, and by all the seers… Notwithstanding they would not hear, but hardened their necks."

The summary is unsoftened: "Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel, and removed them out of his sight: there was none left but the tribe of Judah only."

[thoughtfully] Hear that verdict for what it is — scripture written from Judah's standpoint, grieving and accusing at once. Theology, not neutral chronicle.

The land did not stay empty. As the Israelites went out, other conquered peoples were marched in: "And the king of Assyria brought men from Babylon, and from Cuthah, and from Ava, and from Hamath, and from Sepharvaim, and placed them in the cities of Samaria instead of the children of Israel."

What follows is one of Kings' strangest episodes. The new settlers "feared not the LORD" — and, in the account's telling, "the LORD sent lions among them, which slew some of them." The colonists concluded they had offended "the God of the land," and Assyria sent back a deported Israelite priest to teach them. The result was a hybrid: "They feared the LORD, and served their own gods" — a syncretism the chapter says persisted "unto this day."

[thoughtfully] For later readers, that passage became the charter for viewing the *Samaritans* as a foreign, half-pagan people — and right here, honesty requires care. This is the Judahite account — one side of a centuries-long dispute over who was true Israel. The Samaritans have never accepted it. They identify as descendants of Ephraim and Manasseh — the sons of Joseph, genuine Israelite tribes — who remained in the land and kept the ancient worship on Mount Gerizim. Modern scholarship complicates both certainties: deportation was selective, never total; a substantial Israelite population certainly remained; and the community that emerged was very likely a *mixture* of surviving Israelites and resettled newcomers. Studies of the small community that survives today, still worshipping on Gerizim near Nablus, point to deep and real Israelite roots.

So what became of the ten tribes? Two honest answers sit side by side.

What the historian can see is assimilation. Cut off from their land, their sanctuary, and one another, the deportees within a generation or two would have been absorbed into the surrounding populations — exactly as Assyria intended. Meanwhile, many northerners never left at all, or fled south — Jerusalem expanded sharply, apparently absorbing refugees. Hezekiah's Passover appeal, sent north after the fall, addresses precisely these survivors: "turn again unto the LORD… and he will return to the remnant of you, that are escaped out of the hand of the kings of Assyria." The ten tribes were not spirited away to a hidden country; for the most part they were absorbed — some into the nations of the empire, many into Judah, which carried their blood and their memory forward.

[warmly] And yet the tribes were never simply written off. The prophets who announced the exile also promised a gathering — and Judaism and, later, the Restoration have held that promise open for twenty-seven centuries. Amos, even as he foretold the captivity, insisted the scattering was a sifting, not an annihilation: "I will sift the house of Israel among all nations, like as corn is sifted in a sieve, yet shall not the least grain fall upon the earth." Dispersed — but not one grain lost. Later prophets built on that seed — the two sticks of Ephraim and Judah reunited, the outcasts of Israel gathered "from the four corners of the earth." And Latter-day Saints inherited and sharpened that hope: the gathering of scattered Israel, including the lost tribes, is a central article of faith and a defining work of the last days.

The two answers aren't really rivals. History describes the *means* — assimilation, absorption, quiet survival inside other peoples. Faith describes the *meaning* — that a God who counts grains does not lose them; that a people mixed into all nations is a people positioned, not erased.

[thoughtfully] One last thing. The charge that Israel "would not hear" the prophets isn't a rhetorical flourish. We still have the sermons they refused. Amos, a Judean shepherd sent north to a complacent court, announced captivity in one unadorned sentence: "Therefore will I cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus, saith the LORD, whose name is The God of hosts."

Hosea, a northerner himself, diagnosed the fatal reflex — a nation lurching between foreign patrons instead of returning to God: "Ephraim also is like a silly dove without heart: they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria." He foretold the scattering — "and they shall be wanderers among the nations" — yet his most piercing line holds the whole tragedy and the whole hope in one breath: "O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help."

[warmly] That's the fall of the north in a sentence. The ruin was self-inflicted, and long foretold. The help was never withdrawn. Ten tribes marched out of history down the Assyrian road — and the same prophets who watched them go left, in the covenant, the promise that the road runs both ways.
