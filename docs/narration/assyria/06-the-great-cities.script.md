# SCRIPT

[warmly] Here's something odd about Assyria. It didn't have a capital so much as a *succession* of them — and every move was a sermon in stone.

Where Babylon poured its imagination into one city on the Euphrates, Assyria's kings built and abandoned capitals up and down the Tigris — each a fresh king's statement that the empire had outgrown the last. Ashur. Kalhu. Dur-Sharrukin. Nineveh. Four cities, four reigns, one relentless upward reach. So let's walk them in order — watching a kingdom talk to itself about power, all the way to the city the Bible knew best.

Start with the oldest. The empire took its name from a city, and the city from a god — all three, in Assyrian, are the single word *Aššur.* On a rocky spur above the Tigris, a settlement already ancient when the other capitals were open fields — and never chiefly a fortress. Ashur was a *holy city*, the earthly home of the national god: his temple and ziggurats anchored the skyline, near the twin-towered temple of Anu and Adad and the sanctuary of Ishtar, its earliest levels reaching back into the third millennium BC.

[thoughtfully] Above all — Ashur was where the kings came home to be buried. Beneath the Old Palace, German excavators under Walter Andrae found vaulted royal tombs: monarchs who might reign from a glittering new capital were laid, at last, in the city of the god who had made them kings. Ashur remained the coronation and burial heart of the realm — where a king's legitimacy came from. The Bible remembers the name in "Asshur" — ancestor, land, and god-city at once.

[brightly] City two. In the ninth century BC, Ashurnasirpal the Second refused his predecessors' city and built his own — as Assyrian kings would again and again. He chose Kalhu — the *Calah* of Genesis, modern Nimrud — and turned a provincial town into a world capital. Scripture names it in the Table of Nations: "and Calah, and Resen between Nineveh and Calah: the same is a great city."

The centerpiece was the Northwest Palace — why the modern world knows Assyria at all. Its state apartments were lined, room after room, with carved alabaster reliefs — the king at war, at the hunt, before the gods — still crisp, many originally painted. When Austen Henry Layard dug them out in the eighteen-forties and shipped them to London, they reshaped Europe's picture of the biblical world *overnight.*

At the gateways stood the guardians that became the face of Assyria: the lamassu.

[curious] Let me describe one — a whole theology in a single block of stone. The body of a bull or a lion: strength. The wings of an eagle: swiftness, and the divine. The bearded head of a man in the royal horned crown: wisdom and sovereignty. Carved from single blocks, many tons each. My favorite detail: *five legs.* From the front: four legs, standing. From the side: four legs, striding. From every angle, complete. They stood at thresholds to bless those who entered rightly, and terrify those who did not. Stone doorkeepers, for an empire that wanted every visitor to feel small.

Kalhu gave up another treasure — the Nimrud ivories: thousands of delicately carved panels in Phoenician and Syrian styles, tribute and booty from across the Levant, some tumbled into a well where the damp preserved them. Among the finest small-scale art of the ancient Near East.

[thoughtfully] One hard thing must be said. In twenty-fifteen, the site of Nimrud was deliberately destroyed — reliefs and lamassu that had stood for nearly three thousand years, demolished. Much survives in the world's museums. Worth naming plainly, without spectacle: some of what I'm describing can now be seen only in London, Baghdad, and the photographic record.

City three barely lived. Sargon the Second — the king who deported the northern kingdom of Israel — declined to inherit a city at all. On empty ground northeast of Nineveh, he raised an entire capital from *nothing* and named it for himself: Dur-Sharrukin, "Fortress of Sargon" — modern Khorsabad. A perfect walled square, nearly two kilometers on a side, went up in a decade — palace on a raised terrace, temples, ziggurat, lamassu-guarded gates — paid for by an empire's plunder at its height.

Sargon moved in around seven-oh-six BC. The next year he was killed on campaign — and, catastrophically in Assyrian eyes, his body was never recovered. An omen so grave his son read it as a verdict on the whole project. The court abandoned Dur-Sharrukin and shifted to Nineveh; the gleaming city, scarcely broken in, was left to the wind. [thoughtfully] One of history's cleaner parables of imperial vanity: a monarch built a metropolis to outlast the ages — and did not survive to keep a single New Year in it.

[warmly] And so to the last and greatest — the city the Bible names more than any other Assyrian place. Turning from his father's abandoned dream, Sennacherib made an old Tigris city the capital of the world. Nineveh swelled to roughly seven square kilometers behind a great wall pierced by fifteen named gates — perhaps the largest population the world had yet seen. Its two mounds — Kuyunjik, and Nebi Yunus, "the Prophet Jonah" — still rise above modern Mosul.

At its heart, Sennacherib built what he called — without modesty — the "Palace Without Rival." Its reliefs raised Assyrian carving to its peak — siege engines, marching armies, captive peoples, in documentary precision. The most famous shows the fall of a Judahite city — the Lachish reliefs — a scene we can read against Kings and Isaiah. And in this same complex, and in the palace of his grandson Ashurbanipal, sat the great royal library — tens of thousands of tablets.

[curious] A city that size needed water — for a huge population, and for the parks a great king was expected to plant. Sennacherib built canals from the northern hills, and to carry one across a valley he raised the Jerwan aqueduct — a stone water bridge hundreds of meters long, roughly *two million* dressed blocks, among the earliest monumental aqueducts known anywhere. The king who could carry a river over a valley did not stop at defenses. He watered a paradise.

And then — the gardens. Sennacherib's inscriptions describe a great royal garden beside his palace — a "wonder for all peoples" — planted with trees from across the empire on raised terraces, watered by a mechanism the king says he cast in bronze, lifting water continually to the height of the plantings.

Sound familiar? It should — for the wrong reason. The classical world remembered the Hanging Gardens as one of the Seven Wonders… and attached them to *Babylon.* Yet Babylon's abundant records never mention such gardens, and no site there has been agreed on. The Assyriologist Stephanie Dalley has argued the wonder was real but *misfiled* — that the terraced, machine-watered garden of Greek tradition is actually Sennacherib's garden at Nineveh, its fame drifting onto Babylon's more familiar name. [thoughtfully] A fascinating thesis — and genuinely contested; other specialists are unpersuaded, and the debate is unresolved. What's *not* in doubt: Sennacherib built *a* spectacular terraced garden at Nineveh. Whether it's *the* Hanging Gardens is exactly the kind of question the tablet and the spade keep the right to reopen.

No Assyrian city looms larger in the Bible. Nineveh's founding stands at the head of the Table of Nations: "Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh." Centuries later, the book of Jonah gives it a phrase no reader forgets: "an exceeding great city of three days' journey." Jonah is sent because "their wickedness is come up before me" — and the book ends with God's mercy on "more than sixscore thousand persons."

But the mercy Jonah resented did not last forever. Nahum devotes his whole book to Nineveh's ruin — "Woe to the bloody city!" — and Zephaniah joins him: God would "make Nineveh a desolation, and dry like a wilderness." In six-twelve BC, the great city fell to the Medes and Babylonians — so completely that within a few centuries, travelers could stand on its buried mounds without knowing a city was under their feet.

[warmly] Now stand back and see all four skylines at once. Each king reaching higher. Building larger. Insisting more loudly that *his* city would never end. Sargon's pristine capital outlived him by a year. Sennacherib's "Palace Without Rival" became a mound that hid a library no one knew was there. And the "exceeding great city" that Jonah walked vanished so thoroughly that its very existence passed, for a time, out of memory.

The prophets read these skylines the way they read Babylon's — as monuments to a glory that was always, from the first brick, on its way to becoming dust.

[thoughtfully] How the mounds were lost — and how spades and a few obstinate men brought the winged bulls back into the light — is a story for another day.
