# SCRIPT

[thoughtfully] Most empires try to hide their dark side. Assyria *advertised* it. It carved its conquests into stone and hung them on the palace walls.

So we don't have to guess at its cruelty — the Assyrians documented it themselves. We'll read that self-portrait honestly, neither flinching from it nor sensationalizing it — and then hear how the prophets read it: as a rod in the hand of God. Wielded… and then broken.

[warmly] Assyria was not the first kingdom to fight wars, or the first to build an empire. What it did — over roughly three centuries in the early first millennium BC — was turn conquest itself into a *permanent institution of the state.* The ancient world's first standing professional army. The engineering to crack walled cities. And terror and mass deportation as routine tools of policy. The result: the first true military superpower, and the empire that swallowed the northern kingdom of Israel whole.

[curious] Start with the army. Earlier Near Eastern armies were *seasonal* — farmers called up after the harvest, disbanded before planting. Assyria's genius was to build a core that never went home: a standing force built around the *kisir sharri*, the "royal cohort," topped up each season by provincial levies. Paid, provisioned from imperial granaries. A professional army, in a world of part-timers.

Three advantages compounded that. *Iron* — from the ninth century BC, harder than bronze, and abundant enough to arm *tens of thousands.* The *cavalry* — mounted archers and lancers who could range ahead of the column and run down a broken enemy. And the most decisive — *siege warfare.*

[thoughtfully] Why did the siege matter so much? In the ancient Near East, the walled city was the unit of power. Break the wall, and you took the state. Fail, and your campaign dissolved into a season of raiding. Most powers couldn't reliably take a fortified city *at all* — they blockaded, and waited. Assyria could *storm* walls. That turned every fortress from a refuge into a trap — and it best explains why this empire kept growing.

[curious] And Assyrian siegecraft was a system, not a stunt. The palace reliefs — especially the great Lachish series, carved for Sennacherib — show the whole apparatus at work, like a technical diagram in stone. At the center, the *siege ram*: a wheeled, armored engine housing a metal-tipped beam, its crew shielded by wickerwork and hide. To reach the wall, engineers built vast graded ramps of earth, rubble, and timber — and at Lachish, in Judah, archaeologists found one: some fifteen to nineteen *thousand tons* of stone and soil, the oldest siege ramp yet excavated anywhere. Sappers undermined the wall's base; archers covered them from behind tall wicker screens; scaling ladders went up wherever the wall could be reached. Combined-arms engineering the world had not seen — the ancestor of every siege train for the next two thousand years.

And the Bible *fills this picture in*: the Rabshakeh who taunts Hezekiah at Jerusalem's wall is the herald of exactly this machine — which had already reduced Judah's fortified towns one after another.

[thoughtfully] Now the hard subject. The Assyrian kings did not merely commit atrocities in the heat of war; they *boasted* of them afterward, in carefully composed inscriptions. Ashurnasirpal the Second's annals recite the brutal public execution of rebel leaders in the king's own voice — not as regrettable excess, but as the just and glorious reward of rebellion. The empire's own words about itself.

And the key is this: the cruelty was a *message*, not a mood. The horrors fell on cities that had *revolted* after submitting — on oath-breakers — and were publicized so the next city would calculate the cost… and open its gates instead. The reliefs served the same function — not private art, they lined the throne rooms where the king received foreign envoys. A delegation arriving to negotiate walked a corridor of carved conquest before ever reaching the king. The message was intentional: *this is what awaits those who break faith with Assyria. Choose.*

[curious] Some of the boasting is formulaic — a genre with conventions. But these were not idle threats: the destruction layers of real cities confirm Assyria did what it said. When Nahum called Nineveh "the bloody city," he was quoting the empire's own reputation back at it.

[thoughtfully] Terror broke a city's *will*. Assyria's most consequential invention broke a nation's *identity* — and it wasn't a weapon at all: systematic, two-way mass deportation.

The logic was ruthless. A people rooted in its own land — its sanctuaries, its elites, its memory — could always rebel again. So Assyria *uprooted* them, marching whole communities hundreds of miles and resettling them among strangers. Then it imported *other* deported peoples into the emptied land. Cut off from home, surrounded by strangers with other gods, the transplanted lost the cohesion to revolt. Within a generation or two, distinct nations dissolved into the imperial mass. Deportation was the empire's way of *digesting* what its army had swallowed.

The scale is staggering. In the standard study of the practice, Bustenay Oded counted the kings' own recorded figures: well over *four million people* moved across three centuries — and many campaigns give no numbers, so the true total was higher. Not an occasional punishment: a running administrative program. And the reliefs show the marches with a grim, bureaucratic calm: families with ox-carts and bundles, scribes tallying the count.

[thoughtfully] And this — *this exact machinery* — is what fell on the northern kingdom of Israel. When Samaria finally fell, after a three-year siege, the record is precise: "the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria, and placed them in Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes." That's the outbound half — and it's why we speak of the "lost" ten tribes. Not slaughtered. *Scattered*, and absorbed, by design.

The inbound half followed at once: "the king of Assyria brought men from Babylon, and from Cuthah, and from Ava, and from Hamath, and from Sepharvaim, and placed them in the cities of Samaria instead of the children of Israel." Five foreign populations, themselves deported from elsewhere. The mixed people that resulted is the deep origin of the Samaritans.

[warmly] How did the prophets make sense of this? With a startling conclusion: the terror-state was, in the hand of God, an *instrument*. Isaiah says it straight out: "O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger, and the staff in their hand is mine indignation."

A rod is a *tool* — it has no purpose of its own. But Assyria imagined itself the author, and Isaiah lets it speak its arrogance aloud: "By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom… I have removed the bounds of the people, and have robbed their treasures." [curious] That's almost a paraphrase of the royal annals. Isaiah has *heard* how Assyria talks about itself — and turns its own voice into its indictment.

The fatal error was mistaking instrument for author. "Howbeit he meaneth not so," the prophet says — Assyria intends only "to destroy and cut off nations not a few." So the same God who wielded the rod would judge it for its pride: "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith? or shall the saw magnify itself against him that shaketh it?" [thoughtfully] The axe does not congratulate itself for the woodsman's work. Used for a season, broken for its arrogance — that is the whole of Isaiah's doctrine of Assyria.

And if Isaiah shows the rod raised, Nahum shows it broken — his whole oracle is "the burden of Nineveh." The state everyone feared becomes the one everyone is glad to see fall: "all that hear the bruit of thee shall clap the hands over thee: for upon whom hath not thy wickedness passed continually?" The measure Assyria used… was measured back to it.

[warmly] Set the pieces together. The standing army and its iron made Assyria strong. The siege engineers made that strength decisive. Advertised terror made resistance look suicidal. And two-way deportation dissolved the conquered before they could rise again. A machine engineered, end to end, to conquer and to hold — and for three centuries, it worked.

And yet. [thoughtfully] The same book of Jonah that sends a reluctant prophet to "Nineveh, that great city" insists that even the terror-capital was not beyond the reach of mercy. God's closing question: "should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand?"

To the prophets, this empire was two things at once: a rod God was willing to wield against a faithless people — and a city God was willing to spare, when it turned. When at last it would neither relent nor stop boasting, the rod was broken. [warmly] And the reliefs that had terrified a hundred envoys were buried under the ash of Nineveh — waiting twenty-five centuries for archaeologists to read them again.
