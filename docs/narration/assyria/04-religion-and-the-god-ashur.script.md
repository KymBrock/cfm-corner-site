# SCRIPT

[warmly] I want to teach you one word. And by the end, it will explain why Assyria did what it did — the wars, the boasting, the sermon shouted at Jerusalem's wall.

The word is *Ashur.*

Here's the strange part. In Assyrian, that one word names *three things at once.* A god. A city on the west bank of the Tigris. And the land and people that grew out from that city. God, city, and nation — a single word. When an Assyrian said "Ashur," only context told you which one he meant. The three were inseparable.

[thoughtfully] And that's not a scholarly footnote. That is the *engine* of Assyrian history.

Most Mesopotamian gods governed a domain — the sky, the storm, the sun, the sweet water under the earth. Ashur governed… nothing so specific. No myth of his own. No fixed sphere. In the oldest layers, not even a consort. What he had was a *name* — and the name was the state.

Follow the logic. If the god *is* the state, then worshipping him and enlarging the state are the *same act.* The god's glory grows as his borders grow. Warfare becomes liturgy — holy war, in the most literal sense, waged to bring more of the earth under the peace and order of Ashur.

And the king? The king was Ashur's chief priest and earthly viceroy. He ruled "by the command of Ashur," and reported his conquests back like a servant rendering accounts — the royal annals often take the form of a *letter to the god*, describing the year's campaign as tribute laid at his feet. So rebellion against Assyria was never just politics. It was *sacrilege* — and the king punished it the way a priest purges a defilement.

[curious] As the empire's ambitions grew, so did its god. Assyrian theologians recast Ashur as the true king of the gods — and a version of the Babylonian creation epic was rewritten with Ashur's name swapped in for Marduk's. The whole cosmos, re-founded on parchment, under a northern king. And that winged disk hovering over the king in the reliefs, an archer inside its ring? Most often understood as Ashur — or the sun-god Shamash — riding to war above his viceroy.

Ashur didn't rule alone. Around him stood the gods Assyria had inherited, almost wholesale, from the older civilization of the south — one Mesopotamian tradition, the same deities and offices. What Assyria added was a martial coloring. The same gods… *armed.*

No deity shows this more clearly than Ishtar. Across Mesopotamia, the goddess of love and war and the planet Venus — in Assyria, above all the ferocious lady of battle, worshipped with two distinct personalities: the Ishtar of Nineveh, and the Ishtar of Arbela. In the surviving oracles, it's Ishtar of Arbela who reassures kings like Esarhaddon on the eve of war. "Fear not," the goddess says through her prophets. "I am with you."

Beside her, the familiar company: Nabu, god of writing and wisdom. Shamash, the sun, guarantor of justice, who answered the oracle-questions. Sin, the moon. Adad, the storm. Ninurta and Nergal, the war-gods whose violence suited the empire's temper. And beside the temples rose the stepped ziggurats — at Ashur, at Nimrud, at Nineveh — artificial mountains where heaven and earth were imagined to touch.

[warmly] Let me show you the empire's most haunting religious image. If you've ever stood before the Assyrian reliefs in the British Museum, you've met it. A stylized sacred tree — interlaced branches and palmettes — flanked by towering winged figures, some eagle-headed, some human-faced with elaborate beards, a cone in one hand, a small bucket in the other.

Scholars call them *apkallu* — the primeval sages of Mesopotamian tradition, the bringers of civilization from before the flood, recast here as protective genies. The cone-and-bucket gesture seems to be purification, or blessing — sprinkling the tree, and the king who stands within the scene. What the tree meant is still debated — cosmic order, fertility, the life of the nation. But notice *where* it appears: at the throne-room and the doorways — the points that most needed guarding — with the king at the center. It's Ashur's theology preached in stone: the monarch at the axis of the sacred order — the human hinge between the gods and the world they had entrusted to Assyria.

[thoughtfully] Now — if the gods governed the world, knowing their intentions was the highest practical art, and no state pursued it more systematically. Divination here was not folk superstition. It was a *branch of government*, staffed by learned professionals who reported directly to the king — their letters survive in the thousands.

The queen of the sciences was extispicy — reading the liver of a sacrificed sheep. A trained diviner — the *bārû* — put a yes-or-no question to Shamash and Adad, then inspected the organ against a vast catalogue of ominous marks, each feature a letter in a divine script. Kings put *nothing* important to chance — whether to campaign, whom to appoint, whether a prince would recover — all went to the liver, checked and rechecked. Alongside it ran the watching of the sky against the great omen series — an eclipse a coded warning a wise king could act on. And when the omens threatened the king *personally*, Assyria could even enthrone a substitute king — a stand-in to absorb the predicted doom, while the true monarch waited in reserve.

[curious] Now — did Assyria force its god on the peoples it conquered? Popular retellings assume yes. The reality, as Mordechai Cogan showed, was more measured. Fully *annexed* provinces did take on cultic obligations to the imperial gods. And the foreigners deported into the emptied land of Samaria brought and kept their own gods — a patchwork the biblical historian describes with something close to disgust. But *vassal* kingdoms that kept their thrones — Judah among them — were generally not compelled to install Ashur in their temples. What bound a vassal was the loyalty oath — the *adê* — sworn before the gods of both parties. To break faith with Assyria was to break faith with heaven itself.

And yet the theology never needed a statute to make itself felt. It announced itself, unforgettably, at the wall of Jerusalem in seven-oh-one BC — when Sennacherib's field commander, the Rabshakeh, stood within earshot and preached the gospel of Assyrian power. "Hath any of the gods of the nations delivered at all his land out of the hand of the king of Assyria?… Have they delivered Samaria out of mine hand?… that the LORD should deliver Jerusalem out of mine hand?"

[thoughtfully] Notice the shape of that reasoning. He doesn't deny that Judah has a god. He simply *files* the LORD in a long list of national gods — every one of which has already failed. The unspoken premise: the god of the strongest empire is the strongest god, and the record of conquest is the scoreboard of heaven. Ashur has beaten every god he has faced. The LORD will be no exception.

And here, two theologies stand face to face — and the difference is the whole point.

A god who *is* the nation can never rebuke the nation. His will and the state's ambitions are the same thing. He rises with its armies, and when the armies fall, so does he. Which is exactly what the prophet Nahum promised over doomed Nineveh: "Out of the house of thy gods will I cut off the graven image… I will make thy grave; for thou art vile."

Israel's God is of another order entirely. And Isaiah says so by turning Assyria's own logic *inside out.* Yes, Assyria conquers — but not because Ashur outranks the LORD. Assyria conquers because the LORD is *using* it. "O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger." The empire that thought itself the arm of its god is, unknowingly, a tool in the hand of Another. And a tool that boasts against the hand that wields it? "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?" The same God who raises the empire to discipline his people will judge the empire for the cruelty it enjoyed along the way. Assyria's theology had no category for that: a god who commands the nations — and then holds even his own instrument to account.

[warmly] Scripture savors a smaller irony. The king who mocked the gods of the nations died at prayer to his own — "as he was worshipping in the house of Nisroch his god… his sons smote him with the sword."

And one more thing Assyria's theology could never have imagined. God sent Jonah to that same great city. The empire built on holy war was not beyond the reach of mercy — a God who spared the city its own prophet wanted destroyed.

Assyria built an empire in which the god *was* the state. Israel bore witness to a God the empire, in the end, could only serve.
