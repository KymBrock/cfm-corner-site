# SCRIPT

[warmly] For nearly two and a half thousand years, Assyria was a name without a face.

The prophets had condemned it; the Greeks had half-remembered it; travelers rode over the grassed mounds of its capitals without suspecting a city lay beneath their hooves. The winged bulls were down there in the dark — the reliefs, the tens of thousands of tablets — but the script was unreadable, the sites unmarked. For all practical purposes, the empire that had erased the northern kingdom of Israel… had itself been erased.

Then, in a single astonishing generation in the mid-nineteenth century, it all came back. The mounds were opened. The wedge-script was cracked. And Assyria walked out of the ground, and onto the walls of London and Paris.

[brightly] This is that story — who dug, what they found, and where you can stand in front of it today.

[thoughtfully] Around eighteen twenty, Claudius James Rich — the British East India Company's resident in Baghdad — mapped the great mounds across the Tigris from Mosul that local tradition *already* called Nineveh, and wrote them up so persuasively that others came to dig. He reconnoitered; the spade work began with a Frenchman. Paul-Émile Botta, the French consul at Mosul, probed Kuyunjik — the true Nineveh — in eighteen forty-two, and found little. In eighteen forty-three, on a tip from local villagers, he shifted a few miles north to Khorsabad — and almost at once struck carved stone walls: room after room, lined with reliefs, guarded by colossal human-headed bulls.

It was the first great Assyrian excavation in history — the sensation of its age. What Botta had found was Sargon the Second's palace at Dur-Sharrukin — but with no way yet to read the inscriptions, he announced he had found *Nineveh.* [curious] He had not — he had found the one capital built and abandoned in a single reign. A fitting emblem of the era: finds came out of the ground *years* before anyone could read their labels. Botta's reliefs went to Paris, opening as the Louvre's Assyrian galleries in eighteen forty-seven — the first Assyrian museum on earth.

[brightly] But the figure who fixed Assyria in the popular imagination was a restless young Englishman: Austen Henry Layard. In November eighteen forty-five he began to dig at Nimrud — the biblical Calah — and inside *days* was walking the alabaster-lined halls of the Northwest Palace. Relief cycle after relief cycle came up — and at the palace gates, the guardians that became the face of the empire: the great lamassu, five-legged human-headed winged bulls cut from single blocks weighing many tons.

Getting them home was an epic — rollers overland to the Tigris, rafts buoyed by inflated goatskins down to Basra, then ships for London. Thirty-ton stone bulls, over desert and river and ocean: a wonder of Victorian obstinacy. [warmly] In the British Museum they caused a genuine popular sensation, and Layard's book, *Nineveh and Its Remains*, became a runaway bestseller. For Bible readers who had known Assyria only as a name of dread — here, suddenly, were its kings, its armies, its very doorkeepers, hauled into the daylight.

Among Layard's Nimrud finds was one object of singular biblical importance: the Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser the Third, with its kneeling figure captioned "Jehu, son of Omri" — the earliest known depiction of an Israelite king, bowing in tribute to an Assyrian one. Layard later broke into Sennacherib's "Palace Without Rival" at the true Nineveh — home of the siege-of-Lachish panels we can read straight against the book of Kings.

[thoughtfully] When Layard left archaeology for politics, the work passed to a name worth saying plainly: Hormuzd Rassam — a Mosul-born Assyrian Christian who became one of the great excavators of the century. In eighteen fifty-three, at Kuyunjik, he uncovered the North Palace of Ashurbanipal, and with it two supreme treasures. The lion-hunt reliefs — the wounded lioness dragging her paralyzed hindquarters, carved with an observation of animal agony that has no equal in ancient art. And another cache of the royal library tablets — the collection whose most famous fragment, the flood tablet, a self-taught engraver named George Smith would read out to a stunned London two decades later. The older tellings often folded Rassam's work into Layard's — so say it clearly: a great deal of what fills the Assyrian galleries was found by an Iraqi.

[curious] Now — all of this came out of the ground while the inscriptions on it were still *mute.* The key was found not in a museum, but on a cliff face in western Persia. At Behistun, Darius the First had carved the same royal boast in three scripts — Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian cuneiform — a hundred meters up sheer rock. A British army officer, Henry Creswicke Rawlinson, repeatedly *scaled* it to copy the signs. Old Persian, far simpler, was worked out first — building on guesses by Grotefend — and became a bilingual key, a stone Rosetta, to the far harder Babylonian beside it, and thence to the whole Akkadian corpus pouring in from the digs. The breakthrough wasn't Rawlinson's alone — the Irish clergyman Edward Hincks made some of the sharpest insights.

But could anyone *prove* the decipherers were really reading, not inventing? With hundreds of signs, each carrying several possible values, skeptics reasonably wondered. So in eighteen fifty-seven, the Royal Asiatic Society sent an unpublished inscription of Tiglath-Pileser the First to four scholars — Rawlinson, Hincks, Fox Talbot, and Jules Oppert — who worked *independently* and mailed back sealed translations. Opened together… the four versions substantially agreed. [warmly] Cuneiform could be read. A dead script, and a dead empire, had genuinely been recovered.

So where is it all now? The British Museum holds the richest collection anywhere: the Lachish reliefs, the Black Obelisk, the lion hunt, the tens of thousands of tablets of Ashurbanipal's library — flood tablet included — and colossal lamassu flanking the gallery doorways. The Louvre holds Botta's Khorsabad finds, the first Assyrian sculptures ever put on public display. Chicago's Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures has a Khorsabad lamassu and a Sennacherib prism recording the campaign that shut Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage." And the Iraq Museum in Baghdad holds the homeland collection. Between London and Paris alone, you can meet nearly every object this guide has described.

[thoughtfully] And among the plainer-looking tablets in the British Museum sits the one that carries more evidential weight than any winged bull: the "Fall of Nineveh Chronicle" — a Babylonian tablet recording, in dry administrative prose, the campaigns that brought the empire down, its central entry describing the allied Medes and Babylonians taking the great city in six twelve BC. The fall itself, dated from the enemy's own records.

And a Judahite prophet had devoted an entire book to announcing exactly this. Nahum opens, "The burden of Nineveh." He calls it "the bloody city." He foretells that "all they that look upon thee shall flee from thee, and say, Nineveh is laid waste." The chronicle does not quote Nahum, of course. The honest point is narrower, and *better*, than any forced proof: an event the prophets said was coming is independently dated in the archives of the people who made it happen. The city Jonah was sent to warn and Nahum was given to condemn fell when and as the Bible said — then vanished so utterly that its site had to be rediscovered before the chronicle recording its end could be dug back up… and read.

[thoughtfully] The story's most recent chapter is a hard one to tell. In twenty fifteen, militants of the so-called Islamic State, then holding Mosul, turned deliberately on the Assyrian heritage — filming themselves smashing sculptures in the Mosul Museum, defacing the lamassu at Nineveh's Nergal Gate, and, the following month, bulldozing and detonating the palace mounds of Nimrud — the very site of Layard's winged bulls and the Black Obelisk. Reliefs and colossi that had stood for nearly three thousand years — that had survived the burning of their own empire — were destroyed in an afternoon. A real and irreversible wound was done to the ground itself.

But the same modern world that recorded the loss has gone to work against it. Because Assyria's objects were so thoroughly drawn, catalogued, and photographed, much can be reconstructed even where the original is gone: crowd-sourced projects rebuilding smashed statues in three dimensions from tourists' old snapshots; full-scale replicas of destroyed lamassu standing in public squares from London to New York — acts of deliberate defiance. None of it brings back the stone. But the labor of keeping — the patient copying, cataloguing, and guarding of what the past left behind — is itself a kind of resistance to oblivion.

[warmly] There's a strange symmetry to Assyria's afterlife. The prophets said the empire would fall and be forgotten — and it was, so completely that Nineveh became a grassy hill men rode over without knowing. Then it came back — not on its own terms, but on history's: its boasts in stone dug out of the ruin the prophets foretold, its reliefs of conquered peoples studied by the descendants of those peoples, its winged guardians standing watch over museum doorways instead of palace gates. Whether in triumph or in judgment — Assyria kept, in the end, exactly the memorial the Bible assigned it.
