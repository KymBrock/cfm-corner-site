# SCRIPT

[warmly] Assyria is remembered for cruelty. The flayings boasted of in its annals. The impaled captives on its reliefs. The deportations that emptied nations. Now hold this beside it. The same empire assembled, in a palace at Nineveh, the *first systematically collected library in human history* — and buried it so well, when the city burned, that a Victorian engraver could dig its tablets back out and read to a stunned London a flood story older than Genesis.

It's Assyria's strangest legacy. Let's find out how.

Start with the writing — which Assyria didn't invent, but *inherited.* Cuneiform — "wedge-shaped," from the marks a cut reed presses into damp clay — arose in the Sumerian south around thirty-three hundred BC, and was adapted to write Akkadian, whose two dialects — Babylonian and Assyrian — ruled Mesopotamian record-keeping for two thousand years.

It was *hard.* Several hundred signs, each with multiple sounds and meanings. Years to master — why scribes were a guarded professional class. The difficulty of the script made the school. And the school carried the tradition.

[curious] By the height of the empire, a second script was rising beside the wedges. As Assyria swallowed the Aramaean states of Syria and deported their populations, *Aramaic* — a related tongue of just twenty-two letters, written in ink — became the common language of an empire too big for one people's speech. The reliefs show it: two scribes over the spoils of a captured city — one pressing wedges into clay, one writing Aramaic with a pen. A two-script chancery — the sacred wedges for scholarship and kings, the quick alphabet for governing the world. The alphabet would win in the end. But for a few centuries, the empire held both.

So — the library. Assembled by the last great Assyrian king, Ashurbanipal, at Nineveh. Archives had existed for centuries — working files piling up where business happened. Ashurbanipal did something *new.* He set out deliberately to *gather* — sending agents south into Babylonia with what amount to acquisition orders, demanding tablets *by title*, requisitioning private scholarly libraries, and setting his own scribes to copy whatever couldn't be seized. Catalogued. Shelved by genre. So far as we know, the first library in history systematically *collected* rather than merely accumulated.

[brightly] Stranger still: the king could *read.* Assyrian monarchs were normally warriors who left literacy to their scribes. But Ashurbanipal boasts, in his own inscriptions, of a scribal education — the intricate Sumerian, the obscure Akkadian, even inscriptions "from before the flood." The library bears out the interest behind the boast.

What was on the shelves? Some thirty thousand tablets and fragments survive, now in the British Museum — perhaps a thousand or more distinct works. The omen series. The sign-lists and dictionaries of scribal training. Medicine and magic. Rituals, incantations, the royal correspondence. And the classics — hymns, laments, wisdom debates, and the epics. Gilgamesh, chief among them.

[thoughtfully] Then came the fire. In six-twelve BC, Nineveh fell to the Medes and Babylonians, and its palaces burned. And the fire that destroyed the city *fired* the library — baking the tablets into near-permanence, sealed under the collapsing walls. There they lay until the eighteen-forties and fifties, when Austen Henry Layard and his assistant Hormuzd Rassam dug into the mounds across the river from Mosul and shipped tens of thousands of broken tablets to the British Museum — where for two decades they sat largely unread. A mountain of fragments, waiting for someone patient enough to join the pieces.

[warmly] That someone was George Smith. A young man of working-class London. Trained as a bank-note engraver. He taught himself cuneiform — *in his lunch hours* — from museum publications, and was hired to sort and mend the tablets.

In eighteen seventy-two, working through a broken tablet from the library, Smith realized what he was reading. An account of a great flood. A man instructed to build a vessel. The loading of living things. A deluge. The ship grounding on a mountain. And birds — a dove, then others — sent out to test whether the waters had fallen.

It was, unmistakably, a version of the story of Noah — written centuries before the Hebrew text, and buried in the rubble of an Assyrian palace. By a widely repeated — and no doubt polished — account, the ordinarily reserved Smith leapt up and rushed about the room.

[brightly] On the third of December, eighteen seventy-two, he read a paper on the tablet to the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London — with the Prime Minister, Gladstone, reportedly in the audience. A sensation. *The Daily Telegraph* newspaper funded Smith to return to Nineveh and search for the missing lines. And against very long odds… he found a fragment that filled part of the gap.

The tablet was the eleventh of twelve. The epic it belonged to was *Gilgamesh* — a king who, grieving his friend's death and terrified of his own, journeys to the ends of the earth to find the one man ever granted immortal life — the flood-survivor, Utnapishtim — only to learn he cannot keep even a plant of youth, and comes home to accept that the wall of his city is the immortality a man actually gets. The oldest great poem in any human language — still being reassembled today.

[thoughtfully] Now, Smith's tablet reopened a question that has never quite closed: what *is* the relationship between Mesopotamia's flood and Genesis?

The oldest telling on these shelves — the Atrahasis epic — frames the deluge as the gods' solution to human *noise*: mankind has grown loud, the gods cannot sleep, and when lesser plagues fail, they resolve on a flood — but the wise god Ea warns one man to build a boat. Tablet Eleven of Gilgamesh retells it through the survivor's own mouth: the boat-building, the animals aboard, a storm so violent the gods themselves cower and weep, the grounding on Mount Nimush, and the birds — a dove and a swallow that return, a raven that does not.

The parallels with Genesis are real — and unsurprising. The vessel of refuge. The birds sent to scout the waters. The landfall on the "mountains of Ararat" — the highland of Urartu, on Assyria's own northern frontier. The raven and the dove. The survivor's sacrifice. Two traditions, rooted in the same flood-prone plain, remember a flood in the same shapes. That much the honest reader should simply grant.

But the *differences* are the sermon. In the Mesopotamian versions, the flood is not justice — it's *annoyance.* The gods are tired of the noise. They quarrel and deceive one another. They panic when the waters rage beyond control. And when the survivor sacrifices, they gather "like flies" over the offering — because in a week without human worshippers, no one had fed them. They're *hungry.*

Genesis inverts every beat. The flood comes as *moral* judgment, on a world whose thoughts were "only evil continually." One sovereign God both judges *and remembers.* And when the LORD "smelled a sweet savour," the story turns not to divine appetite but to a promise — never to do this again. A covenant. Sealed with a bow set in the cloud.

[warmly] This is not the Bible copying Babylon. It is a shared ancient memory, told by peoples who understood heaven very differently — and the retelling is itself the argument. Latter-day Saints, who accept revealed knowledge of these primeval events reaching back through the patriarchs, need not be unsettled that Mesopotamia remembered a flood too. A real event leaves many memories; the surprise would be if the flood plain had *forgotten.* What the comparison shows is which tradition kept the *meaning.* The version buried at Nineveh keeps the raven and the dove and the ark — and loses the righteousness of God. The version carried by the covenant people kept the righteousness above all.

And this was a *working* library — the reference desk for the scholars who advised the crown. The sky, believed to carry the gods' intentions, was watched systematically for centuries, and out of that long watch grew the real foundations of astronomy, passed generations later to the Greeks. Observe exhaustively. Record precisely. Preserve everything. That habit is why so much of the ancient world survives at all.

[thoughtfully] Sit with the irony. Assyria was the empire the prophets called "the rod of mine anger" — the power that erased the northern kingdom of Israel. The city Jonah was sent to warn, and Nahum was given to condemn. And when Nineveh fell exactly as the prophets said it would, the fire that *judged* the city is the very fire that *preserved* its library.

It gathered the books. Then it burned around them. And the clay held.

[warmly] Out of the ruins of the empire that always came "out of the north" came the flood — not as judgment this time, but as a story. Read again to the world by a self-taught engraver, bent over a broken tablet, in a London museum.
