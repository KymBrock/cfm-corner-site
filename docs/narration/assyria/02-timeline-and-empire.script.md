# SCRIPT

[thoughtfully] Two hundred years after the fall, a Greek soldier named Xenophon marched his mercenaries past a set of enormous ruins on the Tigris. He asked what city had once stood there. And no one could tell him. *No one knew.*

The city was Nineveh — capital of the first empire in history to rule from the Persian mountains to the Nile. Within a lifetime of its fall, travelers could walk over its ruins and not know a city had ever stood there.

[warmly] That's today's story: two thousand years in the making… and gone in a single generation. Four movements — merchant city, first surge, world empire, sudden fall — with Assyrian kings stepping, by name, into the pages of your Bible.

[brightly] Movement one — and here's the surprise. Assyria did not begin as a military state. It began as a *trading city.*

Ashur, on its rocky bluff over the Tigris, was already a going concern when Abraham's world was young. Around 2000 BC its merchants ran one of the earliest long-distance trade networks we can document, from colonies hundreds of miles away in central Anatolia — the largest at a place called Kanesh. Out went tin and fine woolen textiles by donkey caravan; back came silver and gold. Tens of thousands of their letters and contracts survive — and they read like the paperwork of any modern firm: partnerships, interest rates, insurance against bandits, exasperated notes from wives to husbands too long on the road.

[curious] Old Ashur was a compact city-state answering, in theory, to the god Ashur himself — a deity, uniquely, *identical* with the city. No empire. No standing army. No ambition beyond the next caravan. This is the Assyria of the patriarchs' era — a world away from the war machine to come.

[brightly] Movement two — the first surge. In the Late Bronze Age, the merchant city became a territorial power. Under Ashur-uballit the First, Assyria stepped onto the international stage — trading letters with the pharaohs of Egypt as one great king to another. A century later, Tukulti-Ninurta the First became the first Assyrian ruler to conquer *Babylon*, hauling off its sacred statue of Marduk. A king so formidable, some scholars suspect his memory lies behind the biblical *Nimrod* — out of whose kingdom, Genesis says, came Nineveh and Calah.

[thoughtfully] And then… a long eclipse. The collapse of the Bronze Age world — drought, famine, and the great Aramean migrations into Mesopotamia — pressed Assyria back almost to its heartland for nearly two centuries.

Mark this. That eclipse of the northern powers is *precisely* the window in which the judges led Israel — and in which David and Solomon built a united kingdom that would have been unthinkable a few generations earlier… or later. For Israel, the silence of the north was a providential silence. [thoughtfully] And when Assyria at last recovered its strength — that window slammed shut.

[curious] Movement three: the world empire. The Neo-Assyrian Empire — 911 to 609 BC — is the Assyria the prophets knew: Isaiah, Hosea, Amos, Jonah, Nahum. A line of aggressive kings rebuilt Assyrian power and pushed it beyond anything before — with a new, deliberate brutality they boasted of in their own inscriptions. Ashurnasirpal the Second moved the capital to a rebuilt Kalhu — the Calah of Genesis, modern Nimrud — and lined its palace walls with reliefs that showed the world Assyria's calculated use of terror as policy.

His son, Shalmaneser the Third, turned that army west — into Israel's neighborhood. And here, for the first time, Assyrian stone and biblical history *touch.*

In 853 BC, at the Battle of Qarqar, Shalmaneser fought a coalition of Syrian and Palestinian kings. His victory stele — the Kurkh Monolith — lists among his opponents "Ahab the Israelite," fielding two thousand chariots. [curious] The Bible never mentions this battle at all. Yet there is Ahab, king of Israel, named in an enemy's war record — the earliest reference to an Israelite monarch anywhere outside scripture.

A dozen years later came something more striking still. His Black Obelisk carries the earliest surviving *picture* of a named Israelite anywhere on earth: a figure bowing to the ground before the Assyrian king, captioned "Jehu, son of Omri" — the very Jehu of Second Kings, bringing tribute around 841 BC. [thoughtfully] A humbling image: the reforming, blood-soaked usurper of the northern throne… face to the dust, before the empire that would one day carry his people away.

After a lull, the empire found its greatest organizer: Tiglath-Pileser the Third, 745 to 727 BC. He rebuilt the army, professionalized the provinces, and made *mass deportation* a systematic tool of statecraft. He's also the first Assyrian king the Bible names in the thick of Israel's affairs — under *two* names. Scripture calls him "Pul" when Menahem of Israel buys him off: "And Pul the king of Assyria came against the land: and Menahem gave Pul a thousand talents of silver." Chronicles lists both names in a single breath — one man — and the Babylonian king-lists agree: his throne-name was Pulu. [thoughtfully] Later, invited into the region by King Ahaz of *Judah*, the same king tore the northern kingdom apart — took Ijon, Gilead, Galilee, "all the land of Naphtali, and carried them captive to Assyria." The deportations that would empty Israel had begun.

They finished under his successors. Shalmaneser the Fifth laid the final siege to Samaria — and either he or his successor, Sargon the Second, took it; the Bible credits an unnamed "king of Assyria," and Sargon's own inscriptions claim the capture. Either way, the result was final: "the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria." The northern kingdom — ten tribes strong — ceased to exist. The year was 722 BC.

[curious] And Sargon deserves a pause. He's named in scripture exactly *once* — "when Sargon the king of Assyria sent" his commander against Ashdod, in Isaiah. For most of the nineteenth century, this mighty Sargon appeared in no other ancient record at all — and skeptics treated him as a biblical error. Then, in 1843, excavations at Khorsabad uncovered Sargon's own vast palace, its walls covered with his annals. [warmly] A king known from a single Bible verse turned out to have built one of the largest palaces in the ancient world.

[thoughtfully] Movement four: the summit, and the sudden fall. Under Sargon's son Sennacherib, Assyria stood at Jerusalem's wall. His campaign of 701 BC devastated Judah — "Sennacherib king of Assyria come up against all the fenced cities of Judah, and took them" — and his own prism boasts of shutting up Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage." But Jerusalem did not fall. The siege broke — and Sennacherib "departed, and went and returned, and dwelt at Nineveh." That single deliverance planted a conviction in Judah that Zion could never be taken.

And still the empire grew. Sennacherib's son Esarhaddon did what no Mesopotamian king had ever managed — he conquered *Egypt*, taking Memphis in 671 BC. The empire reached its greatest extent, from the Nile to the Zagros. His son Ashurbanipal pushed on to sack Egyptian Thebes, then turned to something unexpected: the great *library of Nineveh* — scribes sent across the empire to copy every text worth keeping, the first systematic collection of knowledge on earth. [brightly] When the mounds of Nineveh were opened in the 1850s, that library gave the modern world the Epic of Gilgamesh — and its startlingly familiar flood story.

[thoughtfully] And then it ended, with a speed that still astonishes historians. Ashurbanipal died around 631 BC — and within a generation the empire simply came apart: civil war among his heirs, revolt in Babylonia under Nabopolassar, the rise of the Medes on the eastern frontier. The two powers made common cause. In 614, the Medes stormed Ashur, the old holy city. In 612, the allied armies took Nineveh itself — the "bloody city" of Nahum's vision — and burned it. The fall the prophet had sung of *in advance*: "Nineveh is laid waste: who will bemoan her?" A last remnant held out at Harran until 609 BC. And then the name that had terrified the ancient world for three centuries… was simply gone.

[warmly] The prophets never saw Assyria as merely a military problem. Isaiah calls the empire "the rod of mine anger" — a tool in God's hand to discipline his people — and warns, in the same breath, that the rod would itself be broken for its arrogance, as if an axe should boast against the one who swings it. A nation used, and then a nation judged — on a timescale a prophet could announce before it happened.

The empire that seemed eternal from beneath its walls lasted, as a world power, barely three hundred years. As the master of Israel's fate — barely one. [thoughtfully] Which is why, two centuries later, a Greek soldier could stand on the greatest ruins on the Tigris… and no one could even tell him the city's name.
