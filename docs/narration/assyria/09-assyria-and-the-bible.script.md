# SCRIPT

[warmly] Before Babylon… there was Assyria.

For most of two centuries, the prophets of Israel and Judah wrote in its shadow — and scripture never quite made up its mind about it. Assyria is the empire God calls a tool in His own hand, then puts on trial for arrogance. The city He sends a prophet to destroy — then, to that prophet's fury, spares. The "bloody city" whose fall fills an entire book of the Bible… and the "great city" God says He cannot help but pity.

No other foreign power draws so contradictory a response from the Hebrew Scriptures — and none is more thoroughly corroborated by its own monuments.

[thoughtfully] Start with the strangest thing scripture ever says about Assyria: that God loved it.

Jonah opens with a commission that would have struck any Israelite as absurd. "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it." *Nineveh.* The capital of the empire that had erased the ten northern tribes from the map. To send a Hebrew prophet *there*, with a word that might lead to its rescue, was to name the unthinkable.

Jonah runs — the storm, the sailors, the great fish. Only when the word comes a second time does he go. And the message lands in a single, unadorned Hebrew sentence: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown."

Then comes the reversal at the heart of the book. Assyria *repents.* "So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them." And God relents: "God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not."

[curious] And the prophet? Not relieved. *Livid.* Jonah had fled, he now admits, precisely because he feared this outcome: "I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil." [thoughtfully] Jonah never doubted God's mercy. He objected to its *reach* — that it should stretch even to Assyria.

The book's last verse is God's answer — a question. "And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?" Sixscore thousand is a hundred and twenty thousand — and those who cannot tell right hand from left most naturally means the children. God's final word about the capital of the cruelest empire of its age is not a threat. It's a plea for pity. And the book ends there, on a question God never answers for us.

[thoughtfully] Now turn the coin over. If Jonah is grace to Nineveh, Nahum is its judgment — and the two books were placed in the same scroll of the Twelve. Jonah is all narrative and no oracle; Nahum is all oracle and no narrative — three chapters that are nothing but the fall of the Assyrian capital. "The burden of Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite."

Nahum writes with the repentance of Jonah's generation long spent and forgotten. His Assyria is unrepentant, gorged on plunder, and doomed. "Woe to the bloody city! it is all full of lies and robbery; the prey departeth not." And then the sentence itself: "Nineveh is laid waste: who will bemoan her? whence shall I seek comforters for thee?" No one will mourn her, Nahum says. The very pity Jonah's God had extended… is now withdrawn.

History proved the oracle right. In six twelve BC, a coalition of Babylonians and Medes stormed and burned Nineveh — and within a few years the Assyrian empire was gone, never to rise again.

[warmly] Grace and judgment, bound into the same scroll — and scripture does not resolve them for us.

Isaiah gives Assyria its deepest theological treatment — and a title no reader forgets: "O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger, and the staff in their hand is mine indignation. I will send him against an hypocritical nation… to take the spoil, and to take the prey."

A *rod.* An instrument in God's hand, sent to discipline a faithless people. But a rod that begins to imagine itself the arm that swings it has forgotten what it is. Assyria thinks it conquers by its own strength and wisdom — and for that arrogance, it will itself be judged. Then the image every reader remembers — the tool that mistakes itself for the craftsman: "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith? or shall the saw magnify itself against him that shaketh it?"

[thoughtfully] That is the theological heart of scripture's whole quarrel with Assyria. God can use an unjust empire to accomplish a just purpose — *without* excusing the empire's injustice. The axe is still answerable for its pride. The scholar Peter Machinist showed that Isaiah's boasting Assyrian tracks the actual rhetoric of the royal inscriptions — the kings' own self-glorification becomes the evidence against them.

Isaiah also grounds this in datable fact: "In the year that Tartan came unto Ashdod, (when Sargon the king of Assyria sent him,) and fought against Ashdod, and took it." That Sargon is Sargon the Second — the only mention of him by name in the entire Bible. A king so obscure that some once doubted he existed… until his vast palace at Khorsabad was unearthed in the nineteenth century.

[curious] And Isaiah wasn't alone. Assyria is the unnamed weather behind the whole eighth century. Amos, a Judean shepherd sent north, warns: "Therefore will I cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus, saith the LORD" — and beyond Damascus lies the Assyrian heartland. Hosea names the destination outright: "the Assyrian shall be his king, because they refused to return." Micah pronounces Samaria's doom: "Therefore I will make Samaria as an heap of the field." And Zephaniah foretells the very desolation Nahum sang. One continuous argument: Assyria is real. Assyria is God's instrument. And Assyria will answer for itself.

[brightly] Now the second thread. The books of Kings read like a roll-call of Assyrian monarchs — and the empire's own monuments name the same men. Sometimes the same events — from the *other side.*

The most striking convergence is a black limestone monument of Shalmaneser the Third, found at Nimrud in eighteen forty-six: the Black Obelisk. One register shows a figure bowing to the ground before the Assyrian king — and the caption reads, in the standard scholarly translation, the tribute of "Jehu, son of Omri." Jehu — king of Israel. Dated to eight forty-one BC, it is the earliest surviving image believed to depict a named Israelite king — or at the least, his official envoy. One caveat: Assyria used Omri's name as its standing label for the whole kingdom — Jehu wasn't actually of Omri's line. What the stone establishes is not a Bible verse. It's a *person*, kneeling in Assyrian stone within a few years of his reign.

An earlier inscription of the same king — the Kurkh Monolith — lists the coalition Shalmaneser fought at the Battle of Qarqar, in eight fifty-three BC. Among the allied kings: "Ahab the Israelite." The Bible never mentions Qarqar; the Assyrian record never mentions the theology of Ahab's reign. Two independent sources, each preserving what the other omits — same king, same throne, same decade.

And down the list it goes. Tiglath-pileser the Third, whose annals name "Menahem of Samaria" among his tributaries. Sargon the Second, whose annals claim Samaria's capture. Sennacherib, whose prism records shutting Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage." Esarhaddon, successor to his murdered father — exactly as Second Kings reports. The Bible even quietly notes that "Pul" and "Tiglath-pileser" are one man — and the records confirm it: Pul was his Babylonian throne-name. A detail *that* fine tells you how close to the ground the biblical writers stood.

[thoughtfully] And let me say this carefully. These artifacts do not "prove the Bible" in any wholesale sense. They carry no covenant, and no theology. What they establish is sturdier: the historians of Israel and Judah knew exactly who was on the Assyrian throne — and when.

Assyria, then, is the empire scripture wrestles with most before Babylon. It is the *rod*, raised in God's hand against a faithless people — and the *defendant*, tried and condemned for imagining the hand was its own. The target of Nahum's unflinching woe, and the object of Jonah's reluctant, world-enlarging grace.

When Babylon rose in its place, scripture would inherit these same categories. But it was Assyria that taught the prophets the pattern: the God of Israel is sovereign even over the empires that terrorize His people. He will judge their pride without pretending they were not His instrument. [warmly] And His mercy may reach farther than His own prophets wish it would — all the way to a great and cruel city that could not tell its right hand from its left.
